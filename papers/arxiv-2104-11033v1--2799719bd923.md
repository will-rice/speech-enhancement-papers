# Nonlinear Spatial Filtering in Multichannel Speech Enhancement

Kristina Tesch    Timo Gerkmann ^(†)^(†)thanks: The authors are with the
Signal Processing Group, Department of Informatics, Universität Hamburg,
22527 Hamburg, Germany (e-mail: kristina.tesch@uni-hamburg.de;
timo.gerkmann@uni-hamburg.de).

###### Abstract

The majority of multichannel speech enhancement algorithms are two-step
procedures that first apply a linear spatial filter, a so-called
beamformer, and combine it with a single-channel approach for
postprocessing. However, the serial concatenation of a linear spatial
filter and a postfilter is not generally optimal in the [MMSE](#id4)
([MMSE](#id4)) sense for noise distributions other than a Gaussian
distribution. Rather, the [MMSE](#id4) optimal filter is a _joint
spatial and spectral nonlinear_ function. While estimating the
parameters of such a filter with traditional methods is challenging,
modern neural networks may provide an efficient way to learn the
nonlinear function directly from data. To see if further research in
this direction is worthwhile, in this work we examine the potential
performance benefit of replacing the common two-step procedure with a
joint spatial and spectral nonlinear filter.

We analyze three different forms of non-Gaussianity: First, we evaluate
on super-Gaussian noise with a high kurtosis. Second, we evaluate on
inhomogeneous noise fields created by five interfering sources using two
microphones, and third, we evaluate on real-world recordings from the
CHiME3 database. In all scenarios, considerable improvements may be
obtained. Most prominently, our analyses show that a nonlinear spatial
filter uses the available spatial information more effectively than a
linear spatial filter as it is capable of suppressing more than $`D-1`$
directional interfering sources with a $`D`$-dimensional microphone
array without spatial adaptation.

Multichannel, speech enhancement, nonlinear spatial filtering, neural

## I Introduction

In our everyday life, we are surrounded by background noise for example
traffic noise or competing speakers. Hence, speech signals that are
recorded in real environments are often corrupted by noise. Speech
enhancement algorithms are employed to recover the target signal from a
noisy recording. This is done by suppressing the background noise or
reducing other unwanted effects such as reverberation. This way, speech
enhancement algorithms aim to improve speech quality and
intelligibility. Their fields of application are manifold and range from
assisted listening devices to telecommunication all the way to
[ASR](#id8) ([ASR](#id8)) front-ends \[[1](#bib.bib1), [2](#bib.bib2)\].

If the noisy speech signal is captured by a microphone array instead of
just a single microphone, then not only tempo-spectral properties can be
used to extract the target signal but also spatial information. Spatial
filtering aims at suppressing signal components from other than the
target direction. The filter-and-sum beamforming approach
\[[3](#bib.bib3), Sec. 12.4.2\] achieves this by filtering the
individual microphone signals and adding them. In the frequency domain,
this means to compute the scalar product between a complex weight vector
and the vector of spectral representations of the multichannel noisy
signal. Hence, the beamforming operation is linear with respect to the
noisy input.

The beamforming weights are chosen to optimize some performance measure.
For example, minimizing the noise variance subject to a distortionless
constraint leads to the well-known [MVDR](#id2) ([MVDR](#id2))
beamformer \[[4](#bib.bib4), Sec. 3.6\]. The noise suppression
capability of such a spatial filter alone is often not sufficient and a
single-channel filter is applied to the output of the spatial filter to
improve the speech enhancement performance. The second processing stage
in this two-step processing scheme is often referred to as the
postfiltering step.

Single-channel speech enhancement has a long research history that has
led to a variety of solutions like the classic single-channel Wiener
filter \[[3](#bib.bib3), Sec 11.4\] or other estimators derived in a
statistical framework \[[5](#bib.bib5), [6](#bib.bib6),
[7](#bib.bib7)\]. Many recent advances in single-channel speech
enhancement are driven by the modeling capabilities of [DNN](#id18)
\[[8](#bib.bib8), [9](#bib.bib9), [10](#bib.bib10), [11](#bib.bib11)\].

It seems convenient to independently develop a spatial filter and a
postfilter and combine them into a two-step procedure afterward as shown
in Figure
[1](#S1.F1 "Fig. 1 ‣ I Introduction ‣ Nonlinear Spatial Filtering in Multichannel Speech Enhancement").
If the noise follows a Gaussian distribution, this approach can even be
regarded as optimal in the [MMSE](#id4) sense as Balan and Rosca
\[[12](#bib.bib12)\] have shown that the [MMSE](#id4) solution can
always be separated into the linear [MVDR](#id2) beamformer and a
postfilter. However, this separability into a linear spatial filter and
a postfilter only holds under the restrictive assumption that the noise
is Gaussian distributed. The work of Hendriks et al.
\[[13](#bib.bib13)\] points out that the [MMSE](#id4) optimal solution
for non-Gaussian noise joins the spatial and spectral processing into a
single nonlinear operation. Throughout this work, we call such an
approach a _nonlinear spatial filter_ for brevity even though spectral
processing steps are also included. An illustration is given in Figure
[1](#S1.F1 "Fig. 1 ‣ I Introduction ‣ Nonlinear Spatial Filtering in Multichannel Speech Enhancement").

The result of Hendriks et al. reveals that the common two-step
multichannel processing scheme cannot be considered optimal for more
general noise distributions than a Gaussian distribution. This leads to
the question if we should invest in the development of nonlinear spatial
filters for example using [DNN](#id18). Today, single-channel approaches
often use the possibilities of [DNN](#id18) to learn complex nonlinear
estimators directly from data. In contrast, the field of multichannel
speech enhancement is dominated by approaches that use [DNN](#id18) only
for parameter estimation of a beamformer \[[14](#bib.bib14),
[15](#bib.bib15)\] or restrict the network architecture in a way that a
linear spatial processing model is preserved \[[16](#bib.bib16)\]. Only
a few approaches with and without [DNN](#id18) \[[17](#bib.bib17),
[18](#bib.bib18), [19](#bib.bib19)\] have been proposed that extend the
spatial processing model to be nonlinear. Still, the questions of how
much we can possibly gain by doing this, in which situations, and also
where the benefit of using a nonlinear spatial filter comes from have
not been addressed adequately. These are the questions that we aim to
investigate in this paper.

This work is based on a previous conference publication
\[[20](#bib.bib20)\]. In \[[21](#bib.bib21)\] we have studied related
aspects of these questions. Here, we extend our previous work by more
detailed derivations and new analyses that provide some insight into the
functioning of the nonlinear spatial filter. In Section
[III](#S3 "III Linearity of the optimal spatial filter ‣ Nonlinear Spatial Filtering in Multichannel Speech Enhancement"),
we provide a detailed overview of the theoretical results from a
statistical perspective. We include the previously outlined results and
also provide a new simplified proof for the finding of Balan and Rosca
in \[[12](#bib.bib12)\]. We then evaluate the performance benefit of a
nonlinear spatial filter for heavy-tailed noise in Section
[IV-A](#S4.SS1 "IV-A Heavy-tailed noise distribution ‣ IV Evaluation of the benefit of a nonlinear spatial filter in non-Gaussian noise ‣ Nonlinear Spatial Filtering in Multichannel Speech Enhancement"),
for an inhomogeneous noise field created by five interfering human
speakers in Section
[IV-B](#S4.SS2 "IV-B Inhomogeneous noise field (interfering speech) ‣ IV Evaluation of the benefit of a nonlinear spatial filter in non-Gaussian noise ‣ Nonlinear Spatial Filtering in Multichannel Speech Enhancement"),
and real-world noise recordings in Section
[IV-C](#S4.SS3 "IV-C Real-world CHiME3 noise ‣ IV Evaluation of the benefit of a nonlinear spatial filter in non-Gaussian noise ‣ Nonlinear Spatial Filtering in Multichannel Speech Enhancement").
In Section
[V](#S5 "V Interpretation: a nonlinear spatial filter enables superior spatial selectivity ‣ Nonlinear Spatial Filtering in Multichannel Speech Enhancement"),
we investigate the improved exploitation of spatial information by the
nonlinear spatial filter and discuss practical issues of the used
analytic nonlinear spatial filter. Even though nonlinear spatial filters
would most likely be implemented using [DNN](#id18) in the future, in
our analyses we rely on statistical [MMSE](#id4) estimators to provide
more general insights than by using [DNN](#id18)-based nonlinear spatial
filters which would be highly dependent on the network architecture and
training data.

## II Assumptions and notation

We assume that the signals recorded by a $`D`$-dimensional microphone
array decompose into a target speech and a noise component. For each
microphone-channel $`\ell\in\{1,...,D\}`$, we segment the time-domain
signal into overlapping windows and transform the signal to the
frequency domain using the [DFT](#id1) ([DFT](#id1)) to obtain the
[DFT](#id1) coefficients $`{Y}_{\ell}(k,i)`$ with frequency-bin index
$`k`$ and time-frame index $`i`$. Throughout this work, we use segments
of length 32 ms with 16 ms shift and apply the square-root Hann function
for spectral analysis and synthesis. By the additive signal model, the
noisy [DFT](#id1) coefficient can be written as the sum of the clean
speech and the noise [DFT](#id1) coefficients
$`{S}_{\ell}(k,i)\in\mathbb{C}`$ and $`N_{\ell}(k,i)\in\mathbb{C}`$,
i.e.,

|     |                                  |                    |                                                 |     |     |
| --- | -------------------------------- | ------------------ | ----------------------------------------------- | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle{Y}_{\ell}(k,i)`$ | $`\displaystyle=`$ | $`\displaystyle{S}_{\ell}(k,i)+N_{\ell}(k,i).`$ |     | (1) |

As we model the [DFT](#id1) coefficients to be random variables and
assume independence with respect to the frequency-bin and time-frame
index, we drop the indices $`(k,i)`$ to simplify the notation. We
indicate random variables with uppercase letters and use lowercase
letters for their respective realization. Furthermore, we assume all
[DFT](#id1) coefficients to be zero-mean and speech and noise to be
uncorrelated.

We stack the noisy and noise [DFT](#id1) coefficients into vectors
$`\bm{\mathbf{Y}}=[{Y}_{1},{Y}_{2},...,{Y}_{\ell}]^{T}\in\mathbb{C}^{D}`$
and
$`\bm{\mathbf{N}}=[N_{1},N_{2},...,N_{\ell}]^{T}\in\mathbb{C}^{D}`$and
obtain the vector of speech [DFT](#id1) coefficients
$`\bm{\mathbf{S}}\in\mathbb{C}^{D}`$ by multiplying the clean speech
signal coefficient $`{S}\in\mathbb{C}`$ with the so-called steering
vector $`\bm{\mathbf{d}}\in\mathbb{C}^{D}`$, which accounts for the
propagation path between the target speaker and the microphones. We can
then rewrite the signal model as

|     |                                  |                    |                                                      |     |     |
| --- | -------------------------------- | ------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------- | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{Y}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=`$ | $`\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{d}}{S}+\bm{\mathbf{N}}.`$ |     | (2) |

The noise correlation matrix is denoted by
$`\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{n}=\mathbb{E}[\bm{\mathbf{N}}\bm{\mathbf{N}}^{H}]\in\mathbb{C}^{D\times D}`$
with the statistical expectation operator $`\mathbb{E}`$ and
$`(\cdot)^{H}`$ denoting the Hermitian transpose. The spectral power of
the target speech signal is given by
$`\sigma_{s}^{2}=\mathbb{E}[|{S}|^{2}]\in\mathbb{R}^{+}`$. When
appropriate, we use the polar representation for complex-valued
quantities, e.g., $`{s}=|{s}|e^{j\varphi_{s}}\in\mathbb{C}`$, and then
let $`\varphi`$ denote the phase of the complex number.

## III Linearity of the optimal spatial filter

In this section, we aim to provide a more complete picture of the nature
of the optimal spatial filter by aggregating existing results and
presenting more straightforward derivations for some of these. We
identify the noise distribution as the key to linearity versus
non-linearity of the spatial filter and also to the separability of
spatial and spectral processing. Accordingly, in our considerations we
distinguish the two cases of Gaussian distributed noise and non-Gaussian
distributed noise or, more precisely, noise that follows a Gaussian
mixture distribution.

### III-A Gaussian Noise

We start with revisiting the results from Balan and Rosca
\[[12](#bib.bib12)\] and then provide a simplified proof that may be
easier to follow. We assume that the vector of noise [DFT](#id1)
coefficients $`\bm{\mathbf{N}}`$ follows a multivariate complex Gaussian
distribution with zero mean and covariance matrix
$`\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{n}`$, i.e.,
$`\bm{\mathbf{N}}\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{n})`$. As we
employ an additive signal model, the conditional distribution of the
noisy [DFT](#id1) coefficient vector $`\bm{\mathbf{Y}}`$ given
information on the reference clean speech [DFT](#id1) is a multivariate
complex Gaussian distribution centered around the vector of clean speech
[DFT](#id1) coefficients $`\bm{\mathbf{d}}{s}`$ with the same covariance
matrix $`\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{n}`$. The corresponding conditional
[PDF](#id3) ([PDF](#id3)) is given by \[[22](#bib.bib22), Thm. 15.1\]

|     |                                                      |        |                    |                                  |                         |
| --- | ---------------------------------------------------- | ------ | ------------------ | -------------------------------- | ----------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle p\_{\bm{\mathbf{Y}}}(\bm{\mathbf{y}} | {s})`$ | $`\displaystyle=`$ | $`\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{\pi^{D} | \bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}\_{n} | }\exp\left\{-(\bm{\mathbf{y}}-\bm{\mathbf{d}}{s})^{H}\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}\_{n}^{-1}(\bm{\mathbf{y}}-\bm{\mathbf{d}}{s})\right\}.`$ |     | (3) |

Our goal is to show that the linear [MVDR](#id2) beamformer defined as

|     |                                                                                                                                                                                 |     |     |
| --- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle T\textsubscript{MVDR}(y)=\dfrac{\bm{\mathbf{d}}^{H}\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{n}^{-1}\bm{\mathbf{y}}}{\bm{\mathbf{d}}^{H}\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{n}^{-1}\bm{\mathbf{d}}}`$ |     | (4) |

is the optimal spatial filter with respect to the [MAP](#id7)
([MAP](#id7)), [MMSE](#id4) and [ML](#id5) ([ML](#id5)) optimization
criterion if the noise follows a Gaussian distribution.

Balan and Rosca \[[12](#bib.bib12)\] rely on the concept of sufficient
statistics to prove the property in question for the [MMSE](#id4)
optimization criterion. In our context, the MVDR beamformer
$`T\textsubscript{MVDR}`$ is a _sufficient statistic in the Bayesian
sense_ if

|     |                              |                    |                    |                              |                                           |
| --- | ---------------------------- | ------------------ | ------------------ | ---------------------------- | ----------------------------------------- | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle p\_{{S}}({s} | \bm{\mathbf{y}})`$ | $`\displaystyle=`$ | $`\displaystyle p\_{{S}}({s} | T\textsubscript{MVDR}(\bm{\mathbf{y}}))`$ |     | (5) |

holds for every observation $`\bm{\mathbf{y}}`$ and every prior
distribution of $`{S}`$ \[[23](#bib.bib23), Thm. 2.4\]. We infer from
([5](#S3.E5 "In III-A Gaussian Noise ‣ III Linearity of the optimal spatial filter ‣ Nonlinear Spatial Filtering in Multichannel Speech Enhancement"))
that all information about $`{S}`$ contained in the noisy observation is
retained in the output of the [MVDR](#id2) beamformer despite the fact
that the [MVDR](#id2) beamformer reduces the dimension of the
multidimensional input to one dimension. Note that the variable of
interest $`{S}`$ in the above definition is a random variable. In
contrast, $`T\textsubscript{MVDR}`$ is a _sufficient statistic in the
classical sense_ for the true clean speech [DFT](#id1) coefficient
$`{s}`$, which is not assumed to be a random variable, if the
conditional distribution of the noisy observation $`\bm{\mathbf{Y}}`$
given $`T\textsubscript{MVDR}(\bm{\mathbf{Y}})`$ does not depend on
$`{s}`$ \[[24](#bib.bib24), Def. IV.C.1\].

As a first step, Balan and Rosca deduce that the [MVDR](#id2) beamformer
is a sufficient statistic in the classical sense from the Fisher-Neyman
factorization theorem \[[24](#bib.bib24), Prop.
IV.C.1\]\[[25](#bib.bib25), Cor. 2.6.1\], which is applicable since the
conditional [PDF](#id3) of the observation $`\bm{\mathbf{Y}}`$ given
$`{S}`$ in
([3](#S3.E3 "In III-A Gaussian Noise ‣ III Linearity of the optimal spatial filter ‣ Nonlinear Spatial Filtering in Multichannel Speech Enhancement"))
can be rewritten as

|     |                                                      |                    |                                                                                                                                                                                      |                                             |                                                                         |
| --- | ---------------------------------------------------- | ------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle p\_{\bm{\mathbf{Y}}}(\bm{\mathbf{y}} | {s})`$             | $`\displaystyle=`$                                                                                                                                                                   | $`\displaystyle\underbrace{\frac{1}{\pi^{D} | \bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}\_{n}                                                 | }\exp\{-\bm{\mathbf{y}}^{H}\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{n}^{-1}\bm{\mathbf{y}}\}}_{h(\bm{\mathbf{y}})}`$ |     | (6) |
|     |                                                      |                    | $`\displaystyle\times\underbrace{\exp\left\{\bm{\mathbf{d}}^{H}\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}\_{n}^{-1}\bm{\mathbf{d}}\left(2\Real\left\{{s}^{\*}T\textsubscript{MVDR}(\bm{\mathbf{y}})\right\}- | {s}                                         | ^{2}\right)\right\}}\_{g({s},T\textsubscript{MVDR}(\bm{\mathbf{y}}))}`$ |                                                                                                 |     |
|     |                                                      | $`\displaystyle=`$ | $`\displaystyle h(\bm{\mathbf{y}})g({s},T\textsubscript{MVDR}(\bm{\mathbf{y}}))`$                                                                                                    |                                             |                                                                         |
|     |                                                      | $`\displaystyle=`$ | $`\displaystyle h(\bm{\mathbf{y}})g({s},{z}).`$                                                                                                                                      |                                             |                                                                         |

under the Gaussian noise assumption. In the last line of equation
([6](#S3.E6 "In III-A Gaussian Noise ‣ III Linearity of the optimal spatial filter ‣ Nonlinear Spatial Filtering in Multichannel Speech Enhancement")),
we replaced the random variable
$`T\textsubscript{MVDR}(\bm{\mathbf{Y}})`$ with $`{Z}`$, i.e.,

|     |                      |                    |                                                           |     |     |
| --- | -------------------- | ------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------- | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle{Z}`$ | $`\displaystyle=`$ | $`\displaystyle T\textsubscript{MVDR}(\bm{\mathbf{Y}}),`$ |     | (7) |

and will now continue to use this substitute when it improves the
readability. In a second step, Balan and Rosca conclude that the
[MVDR](#id2) beamformer is a sufficient statistic in the Bayesian sense
because any statistic that is sufficient in the classical sense is also
sufficient in the Bayesian sense \[[23](#bib.bib23), Thm. 2.14.2\].

We now provide a proof of the $`T\textsubscript{MVDR}`$ being a
sufficient statistic of $`{S}`$ in the Bayesian sense, which does not
require a reference to advanced stochastic theorems. For this, we
compute a factorization of the likelihood [PDF](#id3) of $`{Z}`$
$`p_{Z}({z}|{s})`$ with $`{Z}`$ defined in
([7](#S3.E7 "In III-A Gaussian Noise ‣ III Linearity of the optimal spatial filter ‣ Nonlinear Spatial Filtering in Multichannel Speech Enhancement"))
as the output of the [MVDR](#id2) beamformer for the noisy input
$`\bm{\mathbf{Y}}`$. From the properties of the multivariate complex
Gaussian distribution undergoing a linear transformation
\[[22](#bib.bib22), Appx. 15B\], we infer that $`{Z}`$ given $`{S}`$ is
distributed according to a one-dimensional complex Gaussian distribution
with mean $`{s}`$ and variance
$`(\bm{\mathbf{d}}^{H}\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{n}^{-1}\bm{\mathbf{d}})^{-1}`$,
i.e.,

|     |                            |        |                    |                                                                                                                     |     |
| --- | -------------------------- | ------ | ------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle p\_{Z}({z} | {s})`$ | $`\displaystyle=`$ | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{CN}\left({s},(\bm{\mathbf{d}}^{H}\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{n}^{-1}\bm{\mathbf{d}})^{-1}\right).`$ |     | (8) |

The corresponding [PDF](#id3) at the output of the beamformer can be
factorized as

|     |                                                                                                                       |                    |                                                 |                                                                                                                                                                               |           |
| --- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------ | ----------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------- | --------------------------- | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle p\_{Z}({z}                                                                                            | {s})`$             | $`\displaystyle=`$                              | $`\displaystyle\underbrace{\frac{\bm{\mathbf{d}}^{H}\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{n}^{-1}\bm{\mathbf{d}}}{\pi}\exp\{-\bm{\mathbf{y}}^{H}\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{n}^{-1}\bm{\mathbf{y}}\left | {z}\right | \}}\_{f(\bm{\mathbf{y}})}`$ |     |     |
|     | × ⏟ exp { $`\bm{\mathbf{d}}`$^H$`\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}`$\_n^-1$`\bm{\mathbf{d}}`$ ( 2 Re { s^\*z } - —s—^2 ) } \_g(s, z) |                    |                                                 |                                                                                                                                                                               | (9)       |
|     |                                                                                                                       | $`\displaystyle=`$ | $`\displaystyle f(\bm{\mathbf{y}})g({s},{z}).`$ |                                                                                                                                                                               |           |

Using
([6](#S3.E6 "In III-A Gaussian Noise ‣ III Linearity of the optimal spatial filter ‣ Nonlinear Spatial Filtering in Multichannel Speech Enhancement"))
we rewrite the posterior distribution as

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\begin{IEEEeqnarraybox}[]{[}][c]{rCl}p_{{S}}({s}|\bm{\mathbf{y}})&=&\dfrac{p(\bm{\mathbf{y}}|{s})p({s})}{\int_{\mathbb{C}}p(\bm{\mathbf{y}}|{s})p({s})ds}\\
&=&\dfrac{h(\bm{\mathbf{y}})g({s},{z})p({s})}{\int_{\mathbb{C}}h(\bm{\mathbf{y}})g({s},{z})p({s})ds}.\end{IEEEeqnarraybox}
``` |  | (10) |

Since the term $`h(\bm{\mathbf{y}})`$ in the denominator does not depend
on the integration variable $`{s}`$, this term cancels with the
corresponding term in the numerator. Next, we extend the fraction with
the term $`f(\bm{\mathbf{y}})`$ from
([9](#S3.Ex4 "In III-A Gaussian Noise ‣ III Linearity of the optimal spatial filter ‣ Nonlinear Spatial Filtering in Multichannel Speech Enhancement"))
to obtain

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\begin{IEEEeqnarraybox}[]{[}][c]{rCl}p_{s}({s}|\bm{\mathbf{y}})&=&\dfrac{f(\bm{\mathbf{y}})g({s},{z})p({s})}{\int_{\mathbb{C}}f(\bm{\mathbf{y}})g({s},{z})p({s})ds}\\
&=&p_{S}({s}|{z})\\
&=&p_{S}({s}|T\textsubscript{MVDR}(\bm{\mathbf{y}})),\end{IEEEeqnarraybox}
``` |  | (11) |

which is the identity we wanted to prove (cf.
([5](#S3.E5 "In III-A Gaussian Noise ‣ III Linearity of the optimal spatial filter ‣ Nonlinear Spatial Filtering in Multichannel Speech Enhancement"))).
Consequently, as the posterior given the noisy observation
$`\bm{\mathbf{y}}`$ equals the posterior given the output of the MVDR
beamformer $`T\textsubscript{MVDR}(\bm{\mathbf{y}})`$, we find that

|  |  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle T\textsubscript{MAP}(\bm{\mathbf{y}})`$ | $`\displaystyle=`$ | $`\displaystyle\argmax_{{s}\in\mathbb{C}}p_{S}({s}|T\textsubscript{MVDR}(\bm{\mathbf{y}}))`$ |  | (12) |

holds. The [MVDR](#id2) beamformer reduces its multidimensional input to
a single-channel output and, therefore, the right-hand side of
([12](#S3.E12 "In III-A Gaussian Noise ‣ III Linearity of the optimal spatial filter ‣ Nonlinear Spatial Filtering in Multichannel Speech Enhancement"))
can be seen as a single-channel postfilter working on the output of the
[MVDR](#id2) beamformer. Since the [MMSE](#id4) estimator complies with
the mean of the posterior, a similar decomposition in a linear spatial
filter and a spectral postfilter is given by

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\begin{IEEEeqnarraybox}[]{[}][c]{rCl}T\textsubscript{MMSE}(\bm{\mathbf{y}})&=&\mathbb{E}[{S}|\bm{\mathbf{y}}]\\
&=&\mathbb{E}[{S}|T\textsubscript{MVDR}(\bm{\mathbf{y}})].\end{IEEEeqnarraybox}
``` |  | (13) |

Because the relationship
([5](#S3.E5 "In III-A Gaussian Noise ‣ III Linearity of the optimal spatial filter ‣ Nonlinear Spatial Filtering in Multichannel Speech Enhancement"))
holds for all prior distributions of $`{S}`$, a decomposition of the
[MAP](#id7) and [MMSE](#id4) estimators into a linear spatial filter
followed by a postfilter exist independently from any further
assumptions regarding the prior distribution of the clean speech
[DFT](#id1) coefficient.

Finally, we consider the [ML](#id5) estimator. Starting from
([6](#S3.E6 "In III-A Gaussian Noise ‣ III Linearity of the optimal spatial filter ‣ Nonlinear Spatial Filtering in Multichannel Speech Enhancement"))
and exploiting the monotony of the logarithm and Euler’s formula, we
find the representation

|  |  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle T\textsubscript{ML}(\bm{\mathbf{y}})`$ | $`\displaystyle=`$ | $`\displaystyle\argmax_{{s}\in\mathbb{C}}p_{\bm{\mathbf{Y}}}(\bm{\mathbf{y}}|{s})`$ |  | (14) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=`$ | $`\displaystyle\argmax_{{s}\in\mathbb{C}}2\Real\{{s}^{*}\underbrace{T\textsubscript{MVDR}(\bm{\mathbf{y}})}_{=z}\}-|{s}|^{2}`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=`$ | $`\displaystyle\argmax_{{s}\in\mathbb{C}}2\cdot|{s}|\cdot|z|\cdot\cos(\varphi_{z}-\varphi_{s})-|{s}|^{2}.`$ |  |  |

Clearly, this function is maximized when the phase of $`{s}`$ matches
the phase of $`T\textsubscript{MVDR}(\bm{\mathbf{y}})`$, as then the
cosine function is maximized. Equating the derivative with respect to
$`|s|`$ to zero and solving for $`|s|`$ reveals that the magnitude of
the [MVDR](#id2) beamformer maximizes the likelihood. Thus,
$`T\textsubscript{ML}(\bm{\mathbf{y}})=T\textsubscript{MVDR}(\bm{\mathbf{y}})`$,
i.e. the MVDR beamformer is the maximum likelihood estimator of the
clean speech [DFT](#id1) coefficient as also stated in
\[[26](#bib.bib26), Sec. 6.2.1.2\].

### III-B Non-Gaussian Noise

As we have seen, if the noise [DFT](#id1) coefficients follow a Gaussian
distribution, then a linear spatial filter can be considered optimal.
However, Hendriks et al. \[[13](#bib.bib13)\] have shown that this does
not need to be the case for non-Gaussian distributed noise. In their
work, they model the noise distribution with a multivariate complex
Gaussian mixture distribution. The $`M`$ Gaussian mixture components
with respective covariance matrix $`\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{m}`$,
$`m\in\{1,...,M\}`$, are assumed to be zero-mean such that the
conditional PDF given the clean speech is given by

|  |  |  |  |  |  |

|  | $`\displaystyle p_{\bm{\mathbf{Y}}}(\bm{\mathbf{y}}|{s})`$ | $`\displaystyle=`$ | $`\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{M}c_{m}\mathcal{CN}(\bm{\mathbf{d}}{s},\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{m}).`$ |  | (15) |

that the amplitude $`A_{S}`$ and phase $`\varphi_{S}`$ of the clean
speech [DFT](#id1) coefficient are independent. They model the phase to
be uniformly distributed over the interval $`[0,2\pi)`$ and assume the
amplitude to be generalized-Gamma distributed (\[[7](#bib.bib7), Eq.
1\], with $`\gamma=2`$ and $`\beta=\nu/\sigma_{s}^{2}`$). The
corresponding [PDF](#id3)

|  |  |  |  |  |

## IV Evaluation of the benefit of a nonlinear spatial filter in non-Gaussian noise

Section
[III](#S3 "III Linearity of the optimal spatial filter ‣ Nonlinear Spatial Filtering in Multichannel Speech Enhancement")
points out that using a nonlinear spatial filter is [MMSE](#id4)-optimal
and, thus, may be beneficial if the noise does not follow a Gaussian
distribution. It is well known that the [DFT](#id1) coefficients of
speech are often better modeled by a more heavy-tailed distribution than
a Gaussian if originating from [STFT](#id13) ([STFT](#id13)) segments
with short duration \[[6](#bib.bib6), [29](#bib.bib29)\]. Consequently,
one may argue that this as well applies to noise [DFT](#id1)
coefficients if the background noise stems from human speakers. In any
case, Martin \[[29](#bib.bib29)\] observed that heavy-tailed
distributions also provide a good fit for [DFT](#id1) coefficients of
some types of noise in the one-dimensional case.

In this section, we investigate the potential of the optimal nonlinear
spatial filter versus the classical separated setup with a linear
spatial filter and a spectral postfilter for noise with a non-Gaussian
distribution. Section
[IV-A](#S4.SS1 "IV-A Heavy-tailed noise distribution ‣ IV Evaluation of the benefit of a nonlinear spatial filter in non-Gaussian noise ‣ Nonlinear Spatial Filtering in Multichannel Speech Enhancement")
presents our findings for noise that departs from Gaussianity by means
of heavier tails but with a rather simple spatial structure. We
published parts of this analysis and of the analysis in
Section [IV-C](#S4.SS3 "IV-C Real-world CHiME3 noise ‣ IV Evaluation of the benefit of a nonlinear spatial filter in non-Gaussian noise ‣ Nonlinear Spatial Filtering in Multichannel Speech Enhancement")
in \[[20](#bib.bib20)\]. However, here we also include the multichannel
Wiener filter for comparison, compute more detailed performance metrics,
and have made changes to the speech power parameter estimation scheme.
In Section
[IV-B](#S4.SS2 "IV-B Inhomogeneous noise field (interfering speech) ‣ IV Evaluation of the benefit of a nonlinear spatial filter in non-Gaussian noise ‣ Nonlinear Spatial Filtering in Multichannel Speech Enhancement")
we provide results for noise that is modeling a spatially more diverse
noise field created by five interfering human speakers and in Section
[IV-C](#S4.SS3 "IV-C Real-world CHiME3 noise ‣ IV Evaluation of the benefit of a nonlinear spatial filter in non-Gaussian noise ‣ Nonlinear Spatial Filtering in Multichannel Speech Enhancement")
we evaluate the nonlinear spatial filtering approach based on real-world
noise recordings from the CHiME3 database. Please find audio examples
for all experiments on our website¹¹ 1
[https://uhh.de/inf-sp-nonlinear-spatial-filter-tasl2021](https://uhh.de/inf-sp-nonlinear-spatial-filter-tasl2021).

### IV-A Heavy-tailed noise distribution

In our first experiment, we investigate the performance of the nonlinear
spatial filter $`T\textsubscript{MMSE}`$ by mixing the target speech
signal at the microphones with multichannel noise that is sampled from a
heavy-tailed Gaussian mixture distribution.

#### IV-A1 Noise distribution model

We construct a Gaussian mixture distribution with an adjustable
heavy-tailedness by combining Gaussian components with scaled versions
of the same covariance matrix. Therefore, we set the $`m`$th mixture
component’s covariance matrix $`\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{m}`$ to be

|  |  |  |  |  |  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{m}`$ | $`\displaystyle=`$ | $`\displaystyle\dfrac{b^{m-1}}{r}\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{n}`$ |  with | r | = | ∑\_m=1^M c_m b^m-1 |  | (20) |

and scaling factor $`b\in\mathbb{R}^{+}`$. The constant $`r`$ ensures
correct normalization such that the overall covariance matrix of our
scaled Gaussian mixture distribution remains $`\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{n}`$.

We rely on the kurtosis to quantify the heavy-tailedness of the scaled
Gaussian mixture distributions. It is a statistical measure that
accounts for the likelihood of the occurrence of outliers
\[[30](#bib.bib30)\] and it has been extended for real-valued
multivariate distributions by Mardia \[[31](#bib.bib31)\]. We extend it
to complex-valued random vectors $`\bm{\mathbf{X}}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}`$
with mean $`\bm{\mathbf{\mu}}`$ and covariance $`\bm{\mathbf{C}}_{x}`$
by defining its kurtosis to be

|  |  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\kappa_{\mathbb{C}}(\bm{\mathbf{X}})`$ | $`\displaystyle=`$ | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[(2(\bm{\mathbf{X}}-\bm{\mathbf{\mu}})^{H}\bm{\mathbf{C}}_{x}^{-1}(\bm{\mathbf{X}}-\bm{\mathbf{\mu}}))^{2}\right].`$ |  | (21) |

A complex-valued $`n`$-dimensional Gaussian distribution can
equivalently be formulated as a real-valued $`2n`$-dimensional Gaussian
distribution \[[22](#bib.bib22), Thm. 15.1\]. The additional factor of
two in
([21](#S4.E21 "In IV-A1 Noise distribution model ‣ IV-A Heavy-tailed noise distribution ‣ IV Evaluation of the benefit of a nonlinear spatial filter in non-Gaussian noise ‣ Nonlinear Spatial Filtering in Multichannel Speech Enhancement"))
ensures that the same kurtosis value results for both formulations of
the same distribution. Using \[[32](#bib.bib32), Sec. 8.2.4\], we
compute the kurtosis of a vector $`\bm{\mathbf{N}}`$ distributed
according to a scaled Gaussian mixture distribution to obtain

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\kappa_{\mathbb{C}}(\bm{\mathbf{N}})=2D(2+2D)\underbrace{\sum_{m=1}^{M}c_{m}\dfrac{b^{2(m-1)}}{r^{2}}}_{q}`$ |  | (22) |

and observe that $`\kappa_{\mathbb{C}}(\bm{\mathbf{N}})`$ is given by
the kurtosis of a $`D`$-dimensional complex Gaussian distribution
multiplied by a factor $`q`$ that depends on the scaling factor $`b`$
and the number of mixture components $`M`$.

#### IV-A2 Experimental setup

In our test scenario, we use five microphones arranged in a linear array
with 5 cm spacing and broadside orientation towards the target signal
source and model the propagation path between the target speaker and the
microphones based on time delays only. We perform the evaluation using
48 clean speech signals taken from the WSJ0 dataset \[[33](#bib.bib33)\]
that are balanced between female and male speakers. The noise DFT
coefficients are samples from a scaled Gaussian mixture distribution
with scale factor $`b=2`$ and a variable number of mixture components
with equal weight $`c_{m}=\frac{1}{M}`$, $`m\in\{1,...,M\}`$. The noise
covariance matrix $`\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{n}`$ models a diffuse noise
field with a small portion (factor of 0.05) of additional spatially and
spectrally white noise as in \[[34](#bib.bib34), Eq. 27\]. The noise and
speech are combined such that a [SNR](#id6) ([SNR](#id6)) of 0 dB is
obtained.

### IV-B Inhomogeneous noise field (interfering speech)

Instead of sampling a Gaussian mixture distribution as in the previous
section or in \[[21](#bib.bib21)\], we now use a setup with five

estimators $`T\textsubscript{MMSE}`$ and $`T\textsubscript{MVDR-MMSE}`$

consistent with this modeling assumption, we require the five
interfering point sources to not be Gaussian distributed or not be
simultaneously active per time-frequency bin, because otherwise the
overall noise resulting from the different interfering sources would

### IV-C Real-world CHiME3 noise

Furthermore, we investigate the performance of the nonlinear spatial
filter for real-world noise from the CHiME3 database
\[[43](#bib.bib43)\] that has been recorded in four different
environments: a cafeteria, a moving bus, next to a street, and in a
pedestrian area.

#### IV-C1 Experimental setup

The CHiME3 data has been recorded using six microphones that are
attached to a tablet computer. For this experiment, we use the simulated
training subset of the official dataset, which has been created by
mixing the recording of real-world background noise with a spatialized
version of WSJ0 utterances. A detailed description of the data
generation process can be found in \[[43](#bib.bib43)\]. We evaluate on
48 randomly selected samples that are balanced between male and female
speakers.

As before, we require an estimate of the time-varying Gaussian mixture
distribution parameters and estimate them using oracle knowledge of the
noise signal. For this, we apply the [EM](#id12) algorithm to
overlapping signal segments of length 750 ms. For both,
$`T\textsubscript{MMSE}`$ and $`T\textsubscript{MVDR-MMSE}`$, we use
$`\nu=0.25`$ and estimate the speech power $`\sigma_{s}^{2}`$ using the
cepstral smoothing technique. In addition, we need to estimate the
steering vector for the target speaker. For this, we employ oracle
knowledge of the clean speech signal and extract the steering vector
estimates as principal eigenvectors of the time-varying covariance
matrix estimates obtained by recursive smoothing.

## V Interpretation: a nonlinear spatial filter enables superior spatial selectivity

![Refer to caption](2104.11033v1/main-figure7.png)

Fig. 5: Spectrograms of an example in an inhomogeneous noise field with
five interfering sources emitting Gaussian noise bursts. The second row
visualizes the processing results obtained with
$`T\textsubscript{MVDR}`$, $`T\textsubscript{MVDR-MMSE}`$ and
$`T\textsubscript{MMSE}`$ and the top row shows the clean and noisy
spectrograms as well as close-ups of the fine-structure of a voiced
speech segment.

We assume that the performance benefit of the nonlinear spatial filter
reported in Section
[IV-B](#S4.SS2 "IV-B Inhomogeneous noise field (interfering speech) ‣ IV Evaluation of the benefit of a nonlinear spatial filter in non-Gaussian noise ‣ Nonlinear Spatial Filtering in Multichannel Speech Enhancement")
and
[IV-C](#S4.SS3 "IV-C Real-world CHiME3 noise ‣ IV Evaluation of the benefit of a nonlinear spatial filter in non-Gaussian noise ‣ Nonlinear Spatial Filtering in Multichannel Speech Enhancement")
is due to the more efficient use of spatial information by the
$`T\textsubscript{MMSE}`$ estimator. Here we support this conjecture by
an experiment that provides an insight into the functioning of the
nonlinear spatial filter.

#### V-1 Experimental setup

We use the same geometric setup as described previously in Section
[IV-B](#S4.SS2 "IV-B Inhomogeneous noise field (interfering speech) ‣ IV Evaluation of the benefit of a nonlinear spatial filter in non-Gaussian noise ‣ Nonlinear Spatial Filtering in Multichannel Speech Enhancement")
(Figure
[3](#S4.F3 "Fig. 3 ‣ IV-B1 Experimental setup ‣ IV-B Inhomogeneous noise field (interfering speech) ‣ IV Evaluation of the benefit of a nonlinear spatial filter in non-Gaussian noise ‣ Nonlinear Spatial Filtering in Multichannel Speech Enhancement"))
but replace the interfering speech sources with sources that emit
spectrally white Gaussian signals. To match the long-term
non-Gaussianity assumption, only one interfering source emits a signal
at a specific time instance. We implement this by using short (336 ms)
non-overlapping Gaussian bursts for the interfering sources. The so
created noise signal can be viewed as stationary regarding its spectral
characteristics except at the segment boundaries. By applying the
[EM](#id12) algorithm to the full-length noise signal we also model the
spatial characteristics as long-term stationary. All other experiment
settings remain unchanged as described before in Section
[IV-B](#S4.SS2 "IV-B Inhomogeneous noise field (interfering speech) ‣ IV Evaluation of the benefit of a nonlinear spatial filter in non-Gaussian noise ‣ Nonlinear Spatial Filtering in Multichannel Speech Enhancement").

#### V-2 Performance evaluation

The performance results are displayed in the second column of Table
[I](#S4.T1 "TABLE I ‣ IV-B1 Experimental setup ‣ IV-B Inhomogeneous noise field (interfering speech) ‣ IV Evaluation of the benefit of a nonlinear spatial filter in non-Gaussian noise ‣ Nonlinear Spatial Filtering in Multichannel Speech Enhancement").
For this artificial type of noise, we observe an even greater
performance difference of 9.9 dB [SI-SDR](#id15) and 2.6 [POLQA](#id9)
score. In fact, the $`T\textsubscript{MMSE}`$ estimator seems to be able
to recover the original signal almost perfectly except from minor
residual high-frequency noise while $`T\textsubscript{MVDR-MMSE}`$
suffers from clearly audible speech degradation and residual noise.
Audio examples can be found
online[1](#footnote1 "footnote 1 ‣ IV Evaluation of the benefit of a nonlinear spatial filter in non-Gaussian noise ‣ Nonlinear Spatial Filtering in Multichannel Speech Enhancement").

Figure
[5](#S5.F5 "Fig. 5 ‣ V Interpretation: a nonlinear spatial filter enables superior spatial selectivity ‣ Nonlinear Spatial Filtering in Multichannel Speech Enhancement")
depicts the spectrograms of the clean and noisy signals in the top row
and the spectrograms of the enhancement results obtained by the
$`T\textsubscript{MVDR}`$, $`T\textsubscript{MVDR-MMSE}`$, and
$`T\textsubscript{MMSE}`$ estimators in the bottom row. The uniform
green coloration of the vertical stripes in the noisy spectrogram
reflects the spectral stationarity. The vertical dark blue lines
separate segments with different spatial properties. While the spatial
diversity cannot be seen from the spectrogram, it becomes visible from
the result of the [MVDR](#id2) beamformer (first in bottom row). Here,
the [MVDR](#id2) beamformer suppresses different frequencies for signal
segments with different spatial properties as can be seen from the
displaced horizontal dark blue lines. The described differences between
the $`T\textsubscript{MVDR-MMSE}`$ (middle) and
$`T\textsubscript{MMSE}`$ (right) estimators’ results are also found in
the spectrograms. A close look reveals that the nonlinear spatial filter
preserves much more of the target signal’s fine structure. Furthermore,
a comparison with the spectrogram of the clean speech signal highlights
that it suppresses background noise much better than the
$`T\textsubscript{MVDR-MMSE}`$ estimator. Residual noise is visible in
the spectrogram only in some segments at a frequency of about 6 kHz.

#### V-3 Discussion

To explain these observations, we examine the covariance matrices
$`\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{m}`$, $`m\in\{1,...,5\}`$, of the Gaussian mixture
noise distribution estimated with the [EM](#id12) algorithm. In Figure
[6](#S5.F6 "Fig. 6 ‣ V-3 Discussion ‣ V Interpretation: a nonlinear spatial filter enables superior spatial selectivity ‣ Nonlinear Spatial Filtering in Multichannel Speech Enhancement")
we visualize their spatial structure based on the directivity pattern
\[[3](#bib.bib3), Sec. 12.5.2\] that they produce when used as noise
correlation matrix in the [MVDR](#id2) beamformer, which is denoted with
$`T_{\text{MVDR}}^{(m)}`$ in (). Furthermore, we visualize the
directivity pattern of the [MVDR](#id2) beamformer. The correlation
matrix $`\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{n}`$ required to compute
$`T\textsubscript{MVDR}`$ is related to the mixture component covariance
matrices via

|  |  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{n}`$ | $`\displaystyle=`$ | $`\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{M}c_{m}\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{m}.`$ |  | (23) |

The directivity pattern produced by $`T\textsubscript{MVDR}`$ is
displayed at the top left followed by visualizations of the five mixture
component covariance matrices. For each of these, a pronounced spatial
characteristic can be observed by means of the horizontal dark lines. On
the right side of the directivity patterns, we indicate the incidence
angles $`\theta_{i}`$ of the noise sources. We notice that each
component’s covariance matrix models one of the noise sources as
apparent from the zero placed in the respective direction by the
[MVDR](#id2) beamformer. The second horizontal line originates from the
symmetry requirements of the directivity pattern, which are determined
by the array geometry.

$`T_{\text{MVDR}}^{(m)}`$ ([MVDR](#id2) beamformer with noise

In comparison, the directivity pattern of the $`T\textsubscript{MVDR}`$
(top left) does not show zeros placed into the directions of the
interfering point sources. Instead, the weighted combination in
([23](#S5.E23 "In V-3 Discussion ‣ V Interpretation: a nonlinear spatial filter enables superior spatial selectivity ‣ Nonlinear Spatial Filtering in Multichannel Speech Enhancement"))
seems to eliminate some of the spatial information, which corresponds to
the well-known fact that a two-microphone [MVDR](#id2) beamformer can
suppress only one directional interfering source but not five of them.
As a result, only some frequencies are suppressed for each interfering
source as we observed in Figure
[5](#S5.F5 "Fig. 5 ‣ V Interpretation: a nonlinear spatial filter enables superior spatial selectivity ‣ Nonlinear Spatial Filtering in Multichannel Speech Enhancement")
before.

Figure
[6](#S5.F6 "Fig. 6 ‣ V-3 Discussion ‣ V Interpretation: a nonlinear spatial filter enables superior spatial selectivity ‣ Nonlinear Spatial Filtering in Multichannel Speech Enhancement")
reveals how much more spatial information can be utilized by
$`T\textsubscript{MMSE}`$ in comparison with
$`T\textsubscript{MVDR-MMSE}`$, whose spatial processing relies on an
estimate of $`\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{n}`$ as visualized in the top left
plot. The initial spatial filtering step using the [MVDR](#id2)
beamformer is not capable of suppressing the directional sources and the
remaining noise has to be filtered by the postfilter, which then leads
to some improvements. On the other hand, the nonlinear spatial
$`T\textsubscript{MMSE}`$ estimator can utilize the spatial information
provided by the estimates of covariance matrices
$`\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{m}`$.

One could argue that a time-varying [MVDR](#id2) beamformer
$`T_{\text{MVDR}}^{(m)}`$ with correctly chosen $`m`$ would suffice to
solve the problem on the short signal segments with noise from a single
point source and a complicated nonlinear approach is not required.
However, we must point out that the step of choosing the ‘right‘
covariance matrix is not required for the nonlinear spatial filter.
Instead, we provide the Gaussian mixture parameters reflecting the
spatial properties of the full utterance and, nevertheless, the
$`T\textsubscript{MMSE}`$ estimator is capable of suppressing five
directional noise sources with only two microphones without the need for
spatial adaptation. Note that this is an exciting finding as traditional
linear spatial filters can only suppress $`D-1`$ interfering point
sources with $`D`$ microphones without spatial adaptation
\[[26](#bib.bib26), Sec. 6.3\].

Despite the impressive performance results achieved in this experiment,
the analytic nonlinear spatial filter has some weaknesses: it requires a
very accurate estimation of the spatial and spectral characteristics of
the noise signal and is also computationally quite demanding. In
addition to the presented experiments, we carried out simulations using
measured impulse responses between the microphones and speakers and
observed a much lower benefit from using a nonlinear spatial filter for
the experiment with five interfering Gaussian sources even with access
to oracle noise data. This is because the spatial and spectral diversity
of the noise signal increase and many more mixture components would be
required to model the noise accurately which then results in a data
problem. Similarly, estimating the parameters of the Gaussian mixture
from a noisy signal is difficult. We approached this using masks to
identify time-frequency bins that are dominated by noise but did not
obtain reliable estimates this way.

Therefore, we conclude that the analytical estimators allow us to study
the potential of nonlinear spatial filters in principle, but because of
high sensitivity to parameter estimation errors and high computational
costs, practical nonlinear spatial filters may be better implemented
using modern machine learning tools like [DNN](#id18).

## VI Conclusions

In a detailed theory overview, we have revisited the fact that the
multichannel [MMSE](#id4)-optimal estimator of the clean speech signal
is in general a jointly spatial-spectral nonlinear filter. Therefore,
the state-of-the-art concatenation of a linear spatial filter and a
postfilter is [MMSE](#id4)-optimal only in the special case that the
noise follows a Gaussian distribution. The experimental section of this
paper studied the performance advantage that can be gained by replacing
the generally suboptimal sequential setup with a nonlinear spatial
filter in three different non-Gaussian noise scenarios.

First, we have shown that considerable performance improvements result
if the noise distribution deviates from a Gaussian distribution by an
increased heavy-tailedness as the nonlinear spatial filter enables a
higher noise reduction and lower speech distortions at the same time.
Second, we report a performance benefit of 4.6 dB [SI-SDR](#id15) and of
0.8 [POLQA](#id9) score for an inhomogeneous noise field created by five
interfering speech sources and, furthermore, we have observed a benefit
of about 3.2 dB [SI-SDR](#id15) and 0.6 POLQA score for the real-world
cafeteria noise recordings from the CHiME3 database. In addition, we
have performed experiments that revealed that the nonlinear spatial
filter has some notably increased spatial processing capabilities
allowing for an almost perfect elimination of five Gaussian interfering
point sources with only two microphones.

The presented findings on the performance potential of a nonlinear
spatial filter motivate further research on the implementation of
nonlinear spatial filters, e.g., using [DNN](#id18) to learn the
nonlinear spatial filter directly from data and overcome the parameter
estimation issues and other limitations of the analytic nonlinear
spatial filter that we have used for this analysis.
````
