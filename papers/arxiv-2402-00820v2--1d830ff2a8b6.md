# USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering

Zhong-Qiu Wang ^(†)^(†)thanks: Z.-Q. Wang is with the Department of
Computer Science and Engineering at Southern University of Science and
Technology, Shenzhen, Guangdong, China (e-mail:
wang.zhongqiu41@gmail.com / wangzq3@sustech.edu.cn).

###### Abstract

In reverberant conditions with a single speaker, each far-field
microphone records a reverberant version of the same speaker signal at a
different location. In over-determined conditions, where there are
multiple microphones but only one speaker, each recorded mixture signal
can be leveraged as a constraint to narrow down the solutions to target
anechoic speech and thereby reduce reverberation. Equipped with this
insight, we propose USDnet, a novel deep neural network (DNN) approach
for unsupervised speech dereverberation (USD). At each training step, we
first feed an input mixture to USDnet to produce an estimate for target
speech, and then linearly filter the DNN estimate to approximate the
multi-microphone mixture so that the constraint can be satisfied at each
microphone, thereby regularizing the DNN estimate to approximate target
anechoic speech. The linear filter can be estimated based on the mixture
and DNN estimate via neural forward filtering algorithms such as forward
convolutive prediction. We show that this novel methodology can promote
unsupervised dereverberation of single-source reverberant speech.

###### Index Terms: 

Unsupervised neural speech dereverberation

## I Introduction

Room reverberation, caused by signal reflections inside reverberant
enclosures, is very detrimental to machine perception in tasks such as
automatic speech recognition (ASR) and speaker recognition
\[[1](#bib.bib1)\]. It also dramatically degrades human hearing, leading
to much lower speech intelligibility and quality. Rereverberation
results from a linear convolution between a room impulse response and
the source signal, and speech dereverberation is known as a classic
blind deconvolution problem \[[2](#bib.bib2), [1](#bib.bib1),
[3](#bib.bib3)\], which is ill-posed in nature and difficult to be
solved as only the convolved signal is observed but the filter and
source are both unknown and need to be estimated. There are infinite
solutions to the filter and source pair, where, in each solution, their
linear-convolution result can well-approximate the observed reverberant
speech. For example, even if the source estimate is a reverberant
version of the source, there still exists a filter such that convolving
the source estimate with the filter can roughly approximate the mixture.
The key to successful speech dereverberation, we believe, is to cleverly
narrow down the infinite solutions to the source and filter so that the
models or algorithms can have a clear idea about what target signals to
predict.

A popular approach for speech dereverberation is based on inverse
filtering \[[1](#bib.bib1), [4](#bib.bib4), [5](#bib.bib5),
[6](#bib.bib6)\], mainly leveraging signal processing principles.
Representative algorithms include weighted prediction error (WPE)
\[[7](#bib.bib7), [8](#bib.bib8), [9](#bib.bib9), [10](#bib.bib10),
[11](#bib.bib11)\], where a linear filter is computed to filter past
observations with a positive prediction delay to estimate late
reverberation to remove. The rationale is that the speech signals beyond
the prediction delay in past frames is little linearly-correlated with
the target anechoic signal at the current frame. WPE leverages this
physical principle to narrow down the solutions to target speech in an
unsupervised way. It has gradually become the most popular and
successful dereverberation algorithm \[[6](#bib.bib6)\], often producing
consistent improvement for modern ASR systems. Subsequent studies along
this line of research combine WPE with beamformers in a sequential
\[[6](#bib.bib6)\] or in a jointly-optimal \[[12](#bib.bib12),
[13](#bib.bib13)\] way. It should be noted that the dereveberation
results of WPE typically contain some early reflections due to its
positive prediction delay. This is however often acceptable in many
applications. For example, early reflections are found beneficial for
speech intelligibility \[[14](#bib.bib14)\].

Another popular approach is based on supervised learning, where anechoic
and reverberant speech pairs are synthesized via room simulation and a
supervised DNN is trained to predict the anechoic speech from
reverberant speech \[[15](#bib.bib15), [16](#bib.bib16),
[17](#bib.bib17), [18](#bib.bib18), [19](#bib.bib19), [20](#bib.bib20),
[21](#bib.bib21), [22](#bib.bib22), [23](#bib.bib23),
[24](#bib.bib24)\]. This approach usually requires a large amount of
simulated training data to avoid potential issues related to data
scarcity \[[25](#bib.bib25)\]. The target anechoic speech serves as the
training label, and the DNN can learn to find the true solution to
target speech through supervised learning. Recent studies along this
line of research leverage target speech estimated by supervised DNNs to
improve signal processing based dereverberation \[[26](#bib.bib26),
[27](#bib.bib27), [28](#bib.bib28), [29](#bib.bib29), [30](#bib.bib30),
[31](#bib.bib31)\], and leverage generative modeling
\[[32](#bib.bib32)\] to improve dereverberation. This approach is often
effective as the DNN has strong capabilities at modeling speech patterns
that can be very informative for dereverberation.

In this context, we propose to investigate unsupervised neural speech
dereverberation. Different from signal processing based approaches,
which are unsupervised in nature and usually not designed to learn
speech patterns from massive training data, we leverage unsupervised
deep learning to model speech patterns. Different from supervised
learning based approaches, unsupervised neural dereverberation models
can be trained directly on real-recorded reverberant speech, avoiding
using simulated training data which could incur generalization issues
\[[33](#bib.bib33), [34](#bib.bib34), [35](#bib.bib35),
[36](#bib.bib36), [37](#bib.bib37)\].

The key to unsupervised neural speech dereverberation, we believe, is to
cleverly narrow down the infinite solutions to the source and filter so
that DNNs can receive a clear supervision on what target signals to
predict, or a clear regularization on what estimates produced by the DNN
are not good and should be avoided. This way, the strong modeling
capability of DNNs on speech signals can be sufficiently utilized for
dereverberation, rather than being wasted due to obscure supervision.
Motivated by the UNSSOR algorithm \[[38](#bib.bib38)\], which deals with
unsupervised neural speaker separation and does not perform any
dereverberation, we assume the availability of over-determined training
mixtures (i.e., multi-channel reverberant speech in single-speaker
cases), and leverage each mixture signal, during training, as a mixture
constraint to narrow down the solutions to the source and filter. This
way, the DNN can (a) receive a clear supervision about what the target
speech is by checking whether its prediction satisfies the mixture
constraints during training; and (b) be trained directly on a set of
collected reverberant signals to realize dereverberation. Our
contributions are summarized as follows:

- •
  We, for the first time, formulate unsupervised neural speech
  dereverberation as a blind deconvolution problem, which requires
  estimating both the source and the filter.
- •
  We propose USDnet, which estimates target (source) speech by modeling
  speech patterns via unsupervised deep learning. Based on the source
  estimate and input mixture, filter estimation is formulated as a
  linear regression problem, which can be solved via a neural forward
  filtering algorithm named forward convolutive prediction (FCP)
  \[[31](#bib.bib31)\] in multiple ways.
- •
  Following the blind deconvolution problem, we propose novel
  mixture-constraint loss functions. They leverage multi-microphone
  mixtures as constraints to regularize the DNN estimate. Together with
  several other regularizations (which we will introduce), they are
  found effective at leading the DNN estimate to approximate target
  speech (and realize dereverberation).

Evaluation results on two speech dereverberation tasks show that USDnet
can effectively reduce reverberation. A sound demo page is provided in
the link below.¹¹ 1 See
[https://zqwang7.github.io/demos/USDnet_demo/index.html](https://zqwang7.github.io/demos/USDnet_demo/index.html).

## II Related Work

Unsupervised neural speech separation, mainly being studied for speech
de-noising, separating mixture of multiple anechoic speakers, and
separating multi-speaker mixtures to reverberant speaker images without
any dereverberation, has attracted consistent research interests in
recent years. However, unsupervised neural dereverberation has been
largely under-explored, although dereverberation is an important task in
speech separation and enhancement.

MixIT \[[39](#bib.bib39), [36](#bib.bib36), [40](#bib.bib40)\], a
representative algorithm, first synthesizes mixtures of mixtures (MoM),
each created by mixing existing recorded mixtures, and then trains a DNN
to separate each MoM to a number of sources such that the separated
sources can be partitioned into groups and, within each group, the
separated sources can sum up to one of the mixtures used for
synthesizing the MoM. Although MixIT is shown capable of separating
mixtures of independent reverberant sources, it cannot reduce the
reverberation of each source as the reflected signals of each source are
dependent with each other. Noisy-target training \[[41](#bib.bib41),
[42](#bib.bib42)\] is an unsupervised approach for speech de-noising.
Similarly to MixIT, it synthetically mixes noisy speech with noise, and
trains DNNs to predict the noisy speech based on the synthetic mixtures.
This approach is found capable of reducing noise as speech and noise are
independent. However, whether it would be effective at dereverberation
is unknown, especially when dealing with reflection signals that are
highly dependent with each other.

Another approach requires source prior models, which can be built based
on clean anechoic speech \[[43](#bib.bib43), [44](#bib.bib44),
[45](#bib.bib45), [46](#bib.bib46)\], or anechoic and reverberant speech
with some annotations \[[47](#bib.bib47)\]. The prior models can, to
some extent, narrow down the solutions to target speech and provide a
supervision on whether DNN estimates are desired or not during training
or inference.

There are approaches leveraging pseudo-labels produced by conventional
algorithms such as spatial clustering and independent vector analysis
(IVA) for training unsupervised neural source separation models
\[[48](#bib.bib48), [49](#bib.bib49), [50](#bib.bib50),
[51](#bib.bib51)\], leveraging pseudo-labels provided by DNN-supported
WPE for training unsupervised dereverberation models
\[[52](#bib.bib52)\], or training DNNs to optimize the objective
functions of conventional separation algorithms \[[53](#bib.bib53),
[54](#bib.bib54), [55](#bib.bib55)\]. Their systems heavily rely on
conventional algorithms and their performance is often limited by
conventional algorithms.

There are studies realizing unsupervised speech de-noising via positive
and unlabeled learning \[[56](#bib.bib56)\], where noisy speech and
noise signals are assumed available so that a binary classifier can be
trained to determine whether each small patch of T-F units belongs to
noisy speech that can be maintained or noise that should be removed.
However, it is unclear whether this approach can be modified for
dereverberation.

The proposed USDnet is motivated by UNSSOR \[[38](#bib.bib38)\], which
formulates unsupervised neural speaker separation as a blind
deconvolution problem, but UNSSOR is designed to maintain the
reverberation of each speaker rather than reducing it. As we will show
in this paper, unsupervised dereverberation has unique challenges and
difficulties very different from the ones in unsupervised separation of
mixed reverberant speakers, and therefore calls for innovative
solutions.

During training, USDnet utilizes recorded mixtures as mixture
constraints to narrow down target speech and realizes unsupervised
dereverberation by solely leveraging recorded mixtures. It exploits
implicit regularization afforded by multi-microphone signals, and does
not require using synthetic data (e.g., MoM, and paired clean and
corrupted speech), source prior models, or pseudo-labels provided by
conventional algorithms. These differences distinguish USDnet from
existing unsupervised neural methods, and, meanwhile, indicate that
USDnet can complement many existing algorithms.

## III Problem Formulation

This section describes the hypothesized physical models, formulates
speech dereverberation as a blind deconvolution problem, highlights the
benefits of over-determined conditions, and overviews the proposed
USDnet.

### III-A Physical Models

For a $`P`$-microphone mixture with a single speaker in
noisy-reverberant conditions, the physical model can be formulated in
the short-time Fourier transform (STFT) domain as follows.

At a reference microphone $`q\in\{1,\dots,P\}`$, we have

|     |                              |                                                                                                                           |     |     |
| --- | ---------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle Y_{q}(t,f)`$ | $`\displaystyle=X_{q}(t,f)+\varepsilon_{q}(t,f)`$                                                                         |     |     |
|     |                              | $`\displaystyle=S_{q}(t,f)+R_{q}(t,f)+\varepsilon_{q}(t,f)`$                                                              |     |     |
|     |                              | $`\displaystyle=S_{q}(t,f)+\mathbf{g}_{q}(f)^{\mathsf{H}}\widetilde{\mathbf{S}}_{q}(t,f)+\varepsilon^{\prime}_{q}(t,f),`$ |     | (1) |

where $`t`$ indexes $`T`$ frames and $`f`$ indexes $`F`$ frequencies. In
the first row, $`Y_{q}(t,f)`$, $`X_{q}(t,f)`$, and
$`\varepsilon_{q}(t,f)`$ respectively denote the STFT coefficients of
the noisy-reverberant mixture, reverberant speaker image, and a weak
noise signal captured at time $`t`$, frequency $`f`$ and microphone
$`q`$. In the rest of this paper, when dropping indices $`t`$, $`f`$ and
$`q`$, we refer to the corresponding spectrograms. In the second row, we
decompose $`X_{q}`$ into direct-path signal $`S_{q}`$ and reverberation
$`R_{q}`$ (i.e., $`X_{q}=S_{q}+R_{q}`$). In the third row, following
narrowband approximation \[[57](#bib.bib57), [5](#bib.bib5)\] we model
reverberation $`R_{q}`$ as a linear convolution between a linear filter
$`\mathbf{g}_{q}`$ and the source $`S_{q}`$, i.e.,
$`R_{q}(t,f)\approx\mathbf{g}_{q}(f)^{\mathsf{H}}\widetilde{\mathbf{S}}_{q}(t,f)`$,
where
$`\widetilde{S}_{q}(t,f)=\big[S_{q}(t-K+1,f),\dots,S_{q}(t-\Delta,f)\big]^{\mathsf{T}}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{K-\Delta}`$
stacks a window of T-F units, $`\Delta`$ is a non-negative prediction
delay, $`\mathbf{g}_{q}(f)\in{\mathbb{C}}^{K-\Delta}`$ is a relative
transfer function relating direct-path $`S_{q}(\cdot,f)`$ to
reverberation $`R_{q}(\cdot,f)`$, $`(\cdot)^{\mathsf{H}}`$ computes
Hermitian transpose, and $`\varepsilon^{\prime}_{q}`$ absorbs the
modeling error.

At any non-reference microphone $`p\in\{1,\dots,P\}`$, where
$`p\neq q`$, we formulate the physical model as

|     |                              |                                                                                                               |     |     |
| --- | ---------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle Y_{p}(t,f)`$ | $`\displaystyle=X_{p}(t,f)+\varepsilon_{p}(t,f)`$                                                             |     |     |
|     |                              | $`\displaystyle=\mathbf{h}_{p}(f)^{\mathsf{H}}\overline{\mathbf{S}}_{q}(t,f)+\varepsilon^{\prime}_{p}(t,f),`$ |     | (2) |

where
$`\overline{\mathbf{S}}_{q}(t,f)=\big[S_{q}(t-I+1,f),\dots,S_{q}(t+J,f)\big]^{\mathsf{T}}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{I+J}`$
stacks a window of T-F units, $`\mathbf{h}_{p}(f)\in{\mathbb{C}}^{I+J}`$
is the relative transfer function relating $`S_{q}`$ at the reference
microphone $`q`$ to the reverberant image at non-reference microphone
$`p`$ (i.e., $`X_{p}`$), and $`\varepsilon^{\prime}_{p}`$ absorbs the
modeling error.

### III-B Formulating Dereverberation as Blind Deconvolution

We aim at reducing the reverberation of the target speaker in an
unsupervised way. With the physical models in
([1](#S3.E1 "In III-A Physical Models ‣ III Problem Formulation ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering"))
and
([2](#S3.E2 "In III-A Physical Models ‣ III Problem Formulation ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering")),
unsupervised dereverberation can be realized by solving, e.g., the
minimization problem below:

|     |     |                                                                                                                                    |                                                                                         |              |
| --- | --- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------ | --- | --- |
|     |     | $`\displaystyle\underset{\mathbf{g}_{\cdot}(\cdot),\mathbf{h}_{\cdot}(\cdot),S*{q}(\cdot,\cdot)}{\text{argmin}}\Big(\sum*{t,f}\Big | Y*{q}(t,f)-S*{q}(t,f)-\mathbf{g}_{q}(f)^{\mathsf{H}}\widetilde{\mathbf{S}}_{q}(t,f)\Big | ^{2}`$       |     |     |
|     |     | $`\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad+\sum*{p=1,p\neq q}^{P}\sum*{t,f}\Big                                                           | Y*{p}(t,f)-\mathbf{h}*{p}(f)^{\mathsf{H}}\overline{\mathbf{S}}\_{q}(t,f)\Big            | ^{2}\Big),`$ |     | (3) |

which finds source and filters that are most consistent with the
physical models in
([1](#S3.E1 "In III-A Physical Models ‣ III Problem Formulation ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering"))
and
([2](#S3.E2 "In III-A Physical Models ‣ III Problem Formulation ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering"))
(i.e., most satisfied with the mixture constraints). This is a blind
deconvolution problem \[[2](#bib.bib2)\], which is non-convex in nature
and is not solvable if not assuming any prior knowledge about the
filters or the source, because all of them are unknown and need to be
estimated.

### III-C Benefits of Over-determined Conditions

We point out that the more microphones we have, the more likely we could
solve the problem in
([3](#S3.E3 "In III-B Formulating Dereverberation as Blind Deconvolution ‣ III Problem Formulation ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering")).
To provide an intuitive interpretation, following UNSSOR
\[[38](#bib.bib38)\] we can view the physical models in
([1](#S3.E1 "In III-A Physical Models ‣ III Problem Formulation ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering"))
and
([2](#S3.E2 "In III-A Physical Models ‣ III Problem Formulation ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering"))
as a system of linear equations with many unknowns, where each linear
equation can be considered as a constraint to some of the unknowns. When
certain conditions (which we will describe next) are satisfied, the
equations can significantly outnumber unknowns, making it more likely to
successfully estimate the unknowns.

In detail, suppose that the modeling error $`\varepsilon^{\prime}`$ is
negligible. The linear system in
([1](#S3.E1 "In III-A Physical Models ‣ III Problem Formulation ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering"))
and
([2](#S3.E2 "In III-A Physical Models ‣ III Problem Formulation ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering"))
has in total $`P\times T\times F`$ linear equations, as we have a
mixture observation for each $`Y_{p}(t,f)`$. Meanwhile, it has
$`T\times F+(K-\Delta)\times F+(I+J)\times(P-1)\times F`$ unknowns,
where the first term, $`T\times F`$, is because we have an unknown for
each $`S_{q}(t,f)`$, the second term, $`(K-\Delta)\times F`$, is because
we have $`K-\Delta`$ unknowns for
$`\mathbf{g}_{q}(f)\in{\mathbb{C}}^{K-\Delta}`$ at the reference
microphone $`q`$ and each frequency $`f`$, and the third term,
$`(I+J)\times(P-1)\times F`$, is because we have $`I+J`$ unknowns for
each $`\mathbf{h}_{p}(f)\in{\mathbb{C}}^{I+J}`$ at each non-reference
microphone $`p`$ and each frequency $`f`$.

In many cases, the linear filters are usually shorter than one second
(meaning that $`K-\Delta`$ and $`I+J`$ can be assumed small). If the
recording is reasonably long (i.e., $`T\gg K-\Delta`$ and $`T\gg I+J`$)
and when there are multiple microphones (i.e., $`P>1`$), the number of
equations can be much larger than the number of unknowns. That is,
$`P\times T\times F\gg T\times F+(K-\Delta)\times F+(I+J)\times(P-1)\times F`$)
when $`P>1`$. In other words, we consider each mixture observation as a
constraint which the estimate of target speech has to satisfy. When
there are many more constraints than unknowns, we can more likely narrow
down the solutions to target speech.

On the other hand, if we only have one microphone (i.e., $`P=1`$), the
number of equations, $`T\times F`$, is smaller than the number of
unknowns, $`T\times F+(K-\Delta)\times F`$. In this case, it would be
more difficult to pinpoint the true solution to target speech using the
approach in
([3](#S3.E3 "In III-B Formulating Dereverberation as Blind Deconvolution ‣ III Problem Formulation ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering"))
as the number of constraints is insufficient.

Since our study assumes that there is only one target speaker, we refer
to the case when multiple microphones are available as over-determined
condition, following the conventions in source separation
\[[58](#bib.bib58)\].

### III-D Overview of USDnet

With the problem formulation in Section
[III-B](#S3.SS2 "III-B Formulating Dereverberation as Blind Deconvolution ‣ III Problem Formulation ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering")
and understandings in
[III-C](#S3.SS3 "III-C Benefits of Over-determined Conditions ‣ III Problem Formulation ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering"),
in the next section, following UNSSOR \[[38](#bib.bib38)\] we propose
USDnet, which tackles this problem by modeling speech patterns (i.e.,
source prior) via unsupervised deep learning. The high-level idea is to
leverage unsupervised deep learning to first produce a source estimate.
With the source estimated in
([3](#S3.E3 "In III-B Formulating Dereverberation as Blind Deconvolution ‣ III Problem Formulation ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering")),
filter estimation then becomes a simple linear regression problem, which
has a closed-form solution. Finally, an objective similar to
([3](#S3.E3 "In III-B Formulating Dereverberation as Blind Deconvolution ‣ III Problem Formulation ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering")),
ideally computed based on multiple microphones, can be designed to
regularize the source estimate to have it approximate $`S_{q}`$.

## IV USDnet

Fig.
[1](#S4.F1 "Fig. 1 ‣ IV-A DNN Setup ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering")
illustrates the proposed USDnet in its vanilla case. It takes as input
the mixtures at all the microphones or just at the reference microphone
$`q`$, and produces an estimate $`\hat{S}_{q}`$ at the reference
microphone $`q`$. To encourage $`\hat{S}_{q}`$ to approximate $`S_{q}`$,
we regularize it during training by linearly filtering it via FCP
\[[31](#bib.bib31)\] so that the filtered estimates can approximate the
mixtures at different microphones. This section describes the DNN setup,
loss functions, FCP filtering, an extension to monaural unsupervised
dereverberation, and the rationale of various design choices of USDnet.

### IV-A DNN Setup

The DNN estimate $`\hat{S}_{q}`$ can be produced via complex spectral
mapping \[[59](#bib.bib59), [60](#bib.bib60)\], where the real and
imaginary (RI) parts of input mixtures are stacked as input features for
the DNN to predict the RI parts of $`\hat{S}_{q}`$. Alternatively, it
can be estimated via complex ratio masking \[[61](#bib.bib61),
[62](#bib.bib62)\], where the RI parts of a complex mask $`\hat{M}_{q}`$
is predicted by the DNN and $`\hat{S}_{q}`$ is obtained by
$`\hat{S}_{q}=\hat{M}_{q}\odot Y_{q}`$, with $`\odot`$ denoting
point-wise multiplication. In supervised speech separation
\[[63](#bib.bib63)\], both masking and mapping are popular, and mapping
usually produces better separation than masking \[[64](#bib.bib64)\]. In
unsupervised dereverberation, however, we find that, for non-trivial
reasons, training USDnet with masking is clearly better. We will discuss
the reasons in Section
[IV-H](#S4.SS8 "IV-H Difficulties of Time Alignment, and Masking vs. Mapping ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering").
Other DNN configurations such as network architectures are provided
later in Section
[V-C](#S5.SS3 "V-C Miscellaneous System Setup ‣ V Experimental Setup ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering").

Fig. 1: Illustration of vanilla USDnet (described in first paragraph of
Section
[IV](#S4 "IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering")).
Several variants are described in Section
[IV](#S4 "IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering").

### IV-B Mixture-Constraint Loss

Following UNSSOR \[[38](#bib.bib38)\], we design a mixture-constraint
(MC) loss $`\mathcal{L}_{\text{MC}}`$ to regularize the DNN estimate
$`\hat{S}_{q}`$ so that it can approximate $`S_{q}`$:

|     |                                                                                                                                 |     |     |
| --- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{MC}}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{MC},q}+\alpha\times\sum_{p=1,p\neq q}^{P}\mathcal{L}_{\text{MC},p},`$ |     | (4) |

with $`\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}`$ denoting a microphone weighting term
for non-reference microphones, and $`\mathcal{L}_{\text{MC},q}`$ and
$`\mathcal{L}_{\text{MC},p}`$, defined by closely following the two
terms in
([3](#S3.E3 "In III-B Formulating Dereverberation as Blind Deconvolution ‣ III Problem Formulation ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering")),
as

|     |                                            |                                                                                                                                                         |     |     |
| --- | ------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{MC},q}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\sum_{t,f}\mathcal{F}\Big(Y_{q}(t,f),\hat{Y}_{q}(t,f)\Big)`$                                                                            |     |     |
|     |                                            | $`\displaystyle=\sum_{t,f}\mathcal{F}\Big(Y_{q}(t,f),\hat{S}_{q}(t,f)+\hat{R}_{q}(t,f)\Big)`$                                                           |     |     |
|     |                                            | $`\displaystyle=\sum_{t,f}\mathcal{F}\Big(Y_{q}(t,f),\hat{S}_{q}(t,f)+\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{q}(f)^{\mathsf{H}}\widetilde{\hat{\mathbf{S}}}_{q}(t,f)\Big),`$ |     | (5) |
|     | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{MC},p}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\sum_{t,f}\mathcal{F}\Big(Y_{p}(t,f),\hat{Y}_{p}(t,f)\Big)`$                                                                            |     |     |
|     |                                            | $`\displaystyle=\sum_{t,f}\mathcal{F}\Big(Y_{p}(t,f),\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{p}(f)^{\mathsf{H}}\overline{\hat{\mathbf{S}}}_{q}(t,f)\Big),`$                   |     | (6) |

where
$`\widetilde{\hat{\mathbf{S}}}_{q}(t,f)=\big[\hat{S}_{q}(t-K+1,f),\dots,\hat{S}_{q}(t-\Delta,f)\big]^{\mathsf{T}}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{K-\Delta}`$
stacks a window of T-F units in $`\hat{S}_{q}`$ with a non-negative
prediction delay $`\Delta`$,
$`\overline{\hat{\mathbf{S}}}_{q}(t,f)=\big[\hat{S}_{q}(t-I+1,f),\dots,\hat{S}_{q}(t+J,f)\big]^{\mathsf{T}}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{I+J}`$,
and $`\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{q}(f)\in{\mathbb{C}}^{K-\Delta}`$ and
$`\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{p}(f)\in{\mathbb{C}}^{I+J}`$ respectively denote the
corresponding estimated linear filters (to be described later). Notice
that the filter taps, $`K`$, $`\Delta`$, $`I`$ and $`J`$, are unknown in
unsupervised setups. They are hyper-parameters to tune. Their values are
shared by all the training utterances in this study.

Following \[[65](#bib.bib65), [38](#bib.bib38)\],
$`\mathcal{F}(\cdot,\cdot)`$ computes a loss on the estimated RI
components and their magnitude:

|     |                                                                    |                               |                                                           |                          |
| --- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------- | ---- | -------- | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\Big(Y_{a}(t,f),\hat{Y}_{a}(t,f)\Big)=`$ |                               |                                                           |                          |
|     | $`\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sum\nolimits\_{t^{\prime},f^{\prime}}     | Y\_{a}(t^{\prime},f^{\prime}) | }`$                                                       | $`\displaystyle\Big(\Big | \mathcal{R}(Y*{a}(t,f))-\mathcal{R}(\hat{Y}*{a}(t,f))\Big | `$                |      |          |
|     |                                                                    | $`\displaystyle+\Big          | \mathcal{I}(Y*{a}(t,f))-\mathcal{I}(\hat{Y}*{a}(t,f))\Big | `$                       |                                                           |                   |
|     |                                                                    | $`\displaystyle+\Big          |                                                           | Y\_{a}(t,f)              | -                                                         | \hat{Y}\_{a}(t,f) | \Big | \Big),`$ |     | (7) |

where $`a`$ indexes all the microphones, $`|\cdot|`$ computes magnitude,
$`\mathcal{R}(\cdot)`$ and $`\mathcal{I}(\cdot)`$ respectively extract
RI components, and the normalization term balances the losses at
different microphones and across training mixtures.

To compute $`\mathcal{L}_{\text{MC}}`$ and enforce the above-mentioned
regularizations to $`\hat{S}_{q}`$, we need to first estimate the
filters, $`\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{q}(f)`$ and $`\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{p}(f)`$. We
describe the method in the next subsection.

In our experiments, we observe that minimizing
$`\mathcal{L}_{\text{MC}}`$ can promote unsupervised dereverberation. A
plot of its loss curve is described later in Section
[VI-B](#S6.SS2 "VI-B Illustration of Loss Curve of ℒ*"MC" ‣ VI Evaluation Results ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering")
and shown later in Fig.
[2](#S6.F2 "Fig. 2 ‣ VI-B Illustration of Loss Curve of ℒ*"MC" ‣ VI Evaluation Results ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering").

Notice that we want $`\hat{S}_{q}`$ to approximate direct-path
$`S_{q}`$, and
$`\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{q}(f)^{\mathsf{H}}\widetilde{\hat{\mathbf{S}}}_{q}(t,f)`$
to approximate reverberation $`R_{q}(t,f)`$. To achieve this, in
([5](#S4.E5 "In IV-B Mixture-Constraint Loss ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering"))
we constrain their summation to approximate the mixture (i.e.,
$`\hat{Y}_{q}(t,f)=\hat{S}_{q}(t,f)+\hat{R}_{q}(t,f)=\hat{S}_{q}(t,f)+\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{q}(f)^{\mathsf{H}}\widetilde{\hat{\mathbf{S}}}_{q}(t,f)`$),
and in
([6](#S4.E6 "In IV-B Mixture-Constraint Loss ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering"))
we constrain $`\hat{S}_{q}`$ such that it can be linearly filtered to
approximate the mixture at another microphone (i.e.,
$`\hat{Y}_{p}(t,f)=\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{p}(f)^{\mathsf{H}}\overline{\hat{\mathbf{S}}}_{q}(t,f)`$).
However, naively using
([5](#S4.E5 "In IV-B Mixture-Constraint Loss ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering"))
and
([6](#S4.E6 "In IV-B Mixture-Constraint Loss ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering"))
alone is not sufficiently effective at leading $`\hat{S}_{q}`$ to
approximate $`S_{q}`$, as the loss is defined on the reconstructed
mixture rather than on $`\hat{S}_{q}`$ itself. We will propose more
regularizations later in Section
[IV-E](#S4.SS5 "IV-E Alternative FCP Filtering at Reference Microphone ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering"),
[IV-G](#S4.SS7 "IV-G Necessity of Over-determined Training Mixtures ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering"),
[IV-H](#S4.SS8 "IV-H Difficulties of Time Alignment, and Masking vs. Mapping ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering")
and
[IV-I](#S4.SS9 "IV-I Discussion on Why Ŝ_q Could Approximate S_q ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering")
so that even if the loss is defined on the reconstructed mixtures, the
resulting $`\hat{S}_{q}`$ would still approximate $`S_{q}`$.

### IV-C FCP for Filter Estimation

Following UNSSOR \[[38](#bib.bib38)\], we compute the relative transfer
functions $`\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{q}(f)`$ and $`\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{p}(f)`$
via forward convolutive prediction (FCP) \[[31](#bib.bib31),
[66](#bib.bib66)\], a neural forward filtering algorithm that linearly
filters the DNN estimate $`\hat{S}_{q}`$ to approximate the mixture:

|     |     |                                                                                                                |                                                                                                         |                                   |
| --- | --- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------- | --- | --- |
|     |     | $`\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{q}(f)=\underset{\mathbf{g}_{q}(f)}{{\text{argmin}}}\sum\limits\_{t}\frac{\Big | Y*{q}(t,f)-\hat{S}*{q}(t,f)-\mathbf{g}_{q}(f)^{{\mathsf{H}}}\ \widetilde{\hat{\mathbf{S}}}_{q}(t,f)\Big | ^{2}}{\hat{\lambda}\_{q}(t,f)},`$ |     | (8) |
|     |     | $`\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{p}(f)=\underset{\mathbf{h}_{p}(f)}{{\text{argmin}}}\sum\_{t}\frac{\Big        | Y*{p}(t,f)-\mathbf{h}*{p}(f)^{{\mathsf{H}}}\ \overline{\hat{\mathbf{S}}}\_{q}(t,f)\Big                  | ^{2}}{\hat{\lambda}\_{p}(t,f)},`$ |     | (9) |

where $`\hat{\lambda}_{a}`$, with $`a`$ indexing all the microphones, is
a weighting term balancing the importance of various T-F units.
Following \[[31](#bib.bib31), [66](#bib.bib66), [38](#bib.bib38)\], we
define it as

|     |                                                                 |             |                                                         |
| --- | --------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------- | ------------------------------------------------------- | ------ | ------------ | --- | ---- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\hat{\lambda}_{a}(t,f)=\frac{1}{P}\sum_{p=1}^{P} | Y\_{p}(t,f) | ^{2}+\xi\times\text{max}\big(\frac{1}{P}\sum\_{p=1}^{P} | Y\_{p} | ^{2}\big),`$ |     | (10) |

where $`\xi`$ (tuned to $`10^{-4}`$ in this paper) floors the weighting
term to avoid placing too much weights on low-energy T-F units, and
$`\text{max}(\cdot)`$ extracts the maximum value of a spectrogram.

Both
([8](#S4.E8 "In IV-C FCP for Filter Estimation ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering"))
and
([9](#S4.E9 "In IV-C FCP for Filter Estimation ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering"))
are weighted linear regression problems, where closed-form solutions can
be readily computed. For
([9](#S4.E9 "In IV-C FCP for Filter Estimation ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering")),
the closed-form solution is

|     |                                          |                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                  |     |
| --- | ---------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{p}(f)`$ | $`\displaystyle=`$                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                               |     |
|     |                                          | $`\displaystyle\Big(\sum\limits_{t}\frac{\overline{\hat{\mathbf{S}}}_{q}(t,f)\overline{\hat{\mathbf{S}}}_{q}(t,f)^{{\mathsf{H}}}}{\hat{\lambda}_{p}(t,f)}\Big)^{-1}\sum\limits_{t}\frac{\overline{\hat{\mathbf{S}}}_{q}(t,f)\big(Y_{p}(t,f)\big)^{*}}{\hat{\lambda}_{p}(t,f)},`$ |     |

where $`(\cdot)^{*}`$ computes complex conjugate. For
([8](#S4.E8 "In IV-C FCP for Filter Estimation ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering")),
it is

|     |     |                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                      |     |
| --- | --- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- |
|     |     | $`\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{q}(f)=`$                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                            |     |
|     |     | $`\displaystyle\Big(\sum\limits_{t}\frac{\widetilde{\hat{\mathbf{S}}}_{q}(t,f)\widetilde{\hat{\mathbf{S}}}_{q}(t,f)^{{\mathsf{H}}}}{\hat{\lambda}_{q}(t,f)}\Big)^{-1}\sum\limits_{t}\frac{\widetilde{\hat{\mathbf{S}}}_{q}(t,f)\big(Y_{q}(t,f)-\hat{S}_{q}(t,f)\big)^{*}}{\hat{\lambda}_{q}(t,f)}.`$ |     |

We then plug the closed-form solutions to
([5](#S4.E5 "In IV-B Mixture-Constraint Loss ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering"))
and
([6](#S4.E6 "In IV-B Mixture-Constraint Loss ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering")),
compute the $`\mathcal{L}_{\text{MC}}`$ loss in
([4](#S4.E4 "In IV-B Mixture-Constraint Loss ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering")),
and optimize the DNN.

### IV-D Interpretation of MC Loss and FCP Filtering

To facilitate understanding, we give an interpretation of the proposed
$`\mathcal{L}_{\text{MC}}`$ loss.

Suppose that $`\mathcal{F}(\cdot,\cdot)`$ is defined as
$`\mathcal{F}\big(Y_{r}(t,f),\hat{Y}_{r}(t,f)\big)=|Y_{r}(t,f)-\hat{Y}_{r}(t,f)|^{2}`$
(rather than as the one in
([7](#S4.Ex8 "In IV-B Mixture-Constraint Loss ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering")),
no weighting is used in FCP (i.e., $`\hat{\lambda}_{a}(t,f)=1`$), and
$`\alpha=1`$, $`\mathcal{L}_{\text{MC}}`$ in
([4](#S4.E4 "In IV-B Mixture-Constraint Loss ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering"))
can be alternatively formulated as

|     |     |                                                                                                      |                                                                                                     |         |
| --- | --- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- | --- | ---- |
|     |     | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{M}}{}_{\text{C,alt}}=`$                                            |                                                                                                     |         |
|     |     | $`\displaystyle\sum*{f}\underset{\mathbf{g}*{q}(f)}{{\text{min}}}\sum\_{t}\Big                       | Y*{q}(t,f)-\hat{S}*{q}(t,f)-\mathbf{g}_{q}(f)^{\mathsf{H}}\widetilde{\hat{\mathbf{S}}}_{q}(t,f)\Big | ^{2}`$  |     |      |
|     |     | $`\displaystyle+\sum*{p=1,p\neq q}^{P}\sum*{f}\underset{\mathbf{h}_{p}(f)}{{\text{min}}}\sum_{t}\Big | Y*{p}(t,f)-\mathbf{h}*{p}(f)^{\mathsf{H}}\overline{\hat{\mathbf{S}}}\_{q}(t,f)\Big                  | ^{2},`$ |     | (11) |

which, given a DNN estimate $`\hat{S}_{q}`$, first computes the relative
transfer functions $`\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{q}(f)`$ and
$`\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{p}(f)`$ that best relate the DNN estimate
$`\hat{S}_{q}`$ to the mixtures (i.e., best satisfy the physical mixture
constraints) by minimizing a quadratic objective per frequency at each
microphone, and then uses the minimum as the loss for DNN training. The
proposed approach can be considered as a minimizing the minimum
approach. This echoes the idea of permutation invariant training
\[[67](#bib.bib67)\] in supervised speech separation, where the minimum
to minimize is first computed by finding the best speaker permutation
and then used as the loss to train DNNs.

In our study, we use $`\mathcal{L}_{\text{MC}}`$ in
([4](#S4.E4 "In IV-B Mixture-Constraint Loss ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering"))
rather than $`\mathcal{L}_{\text{MC,alt}}`$ for DNN training, as we find
that using $`\mathcal{F}(\cdot,\cdot)`$ defined in
([7](#S4.Ex8 "In IV-B Mixture-Constraint Loss ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering"))
and the weighting term in
([10](#S4.E10 "In IV-C FCP for Filter Estimation ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering"))
produces better performance.

### IV-E Alternative FCP Filtering at Reference Microphone

An alternative way to compute $`\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{q}(f)`$ is as follows:

|     |                                                                                                                |                                                                                         |                                   |
| --- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------- | --- | ---- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{q}(f)=\underset{\mathbf{g}_{q}(f)}{{\text{argmin}}}\sum\limits\_{t}\frac{\Big | Y*{q}(t,f)-\mathbf{g}*{q}(f)^{{\mathsf{H}}}\ \widetilde{\hat{\mathbf{S}}}\_{q}(t,f)\Big | ^{2}}{\hat{\lambda}\_{q}(t,f)},`$ |     | (12) |

where the difference from
([8](#S4.E8 "In IV-C FCP for Filter Estimation ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering"))
is that, in the numerator, $`\hat{S}_{q}`$ is not subtracted from
$`Y_{q}`$. Although this change appears minor, its benefits are
non-trivial. We find it a useful regularization that can (a) help USDnet
avoid the trivial solution of copying the input mixture as the output
(i.e., $`\hat{S}_{q}=Y_{q}`$); (b) enable USDnet to be trained on
monaural mixtures where only one microphone can be used in loss
computation; and (c) lead to an $`\hat{S}_{q}`$ that better approximates
$`S_{q}`$ and a
$`\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{q}(f)^{{\mathsf{H}}}\ \widetilde{\hat{\mathbf{S}}}_{q}(t,f)`$
that better approximates $`R_{q}`$, rather than an $`\hat{S}_{q}`$ and a
$`\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{q}(f)^{{\mathsf{H}}}\ \widetilde{\hat{\mathbf{S}}}_{q}(t,f)`$
whose summation in
([5](#S4.E5 "In IV-B Mixture-Constraint Loss ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering"))
can better approximate the mixture $`Y_{q}`$ (and hence better minimize
$`\mathcal{L}_{\text{MC},q}`$) but not respectively better approximating
$`S_{q}`$ and $`R_{q}`$. We will later detail the first two benefits in
Section
[IV-G](#S4.SS7 "IV-G Necessity of Over-determined Training Mixtures ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering")
and the third in
[IV-I](#S4.SS9 "IV-I Discussion on Why Ŝ_q Could Approximate S_q ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering"),
after describing the input to USDnet in the next subsection.

### IV-F Number of Microphones in Input and Loss

We can use a subset of microphones in the input or loss to train USDnet.
This section describes three cases.

#### IV-F1 Multi-channel input and loss

So far, we have mainly presented the loss functions of USDnet, which are
computed based on multiple microphones. On the input side, we can feed
all the microphone mixtures (i.e., same as the ones used in loss
computation) to USDnet. The resulting trained USDnet performs
multi-channel unsupervised dereverberation at run time.

#### IV-F2 Single-channel input and multi-channel loss

USDnet can be trained to realize monaural dereverberation at run time by
only feeding in the mixture at the reference microphone (i.e.,
$`Y_{q}`$) to the DNN while still optimizing $`\mathcal{L}_{\text{MC}}`$
computed based on multi-channel mixtures. Fig.
[1](#S4.F1 "Fig. 1 ‣ IV-A DNN Setup ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering")
shows the idea. The loss computed on multiple microphones could guide
USDnet to model the spectro-temporal patterns in reverberant speech and
realize monaural unsupervised dereverberation. Notice that multi-channel
mixtures are only required for training. At run time, the trained model
performs monaural dereverberation.

#### IV-F3 Single-channel input and loss

If the training mixtures are all monaural, we find that we can still
train USDnet (although with very limited improvement) if the FCP
filtering in
([12](#S4.E12 "In IV-E Alternative FCP Filtering at Reference Microphone ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering"))
is used, but the training would fail if
([8](#S4.E8 "In IV-C FCP for Filter Estimation ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering"))
is used for filter estimation. We will describe the rationale in Section
[IV-G](#S4.SS7 "IV-G Necessity of Over-determined Training Mixtures ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering").

### IV-G Necessity of Over-determined Training Mixtures

This subsection discusses the necessity of over-determined training
mixtures, depending on whether $`\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{q}(f)`$ is computed
using
([8](#S4.E8 "In IV-C FCP for Filter Estimation ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering"))
or
([12](#S4.E12 "In IV-E Alternative FCP Filtering at Reference Microphone ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering")).

#### IV-G1 When $`\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{q}(f)`$ is computed using ([8](#S4.E8 "In IV-C FCP for Filter Estimation ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering"))

In this case, the mixtures for training USDnet need to be
over-determined. If the training mixtures are instead all monaural
(i.e., using single-channel input and loss), $`\mathcal{L}_{\text{MC}}`$
would only contain the first term $`\mathcal{L}_{\text{MC},q}`$, which
can be optimized to zero by the DNN by simply copying the input mixture
as the output (i.e., $`\hat{S}_{q}=Y_{q}`$ and, in this case,
$`\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{q}(f)`$ computed via
([8](#S4.E8 "In IV-C FCP for Filter Estimation ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering"))
would be all-zero since in the numerator $`Y_{q}-\hat{S}_{q}=0`$). This
trivial solution would not yield any dereverberation. By leveraging
extra microphone recordings as constraints, the second term in
$`\mathcal{L}_{\text{MC}}`$ defined in
([4](#S4.E4 "In IV-B Mixture-Constraint Loss ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering"))
could help avoid the trivial solution.

#### IV-G2 When $`\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{q}(f)`$ is computed using ([12](#S4.E12 "In IV-E Alternative FCP Filtering at Reference Microphone ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering"))

In this case, we find that the mixtures for training USDnet can also be
monaural (i.e., by using single-channel input and loss). Different from
the previous case, in this case if the DNN simply copies the input as
output (i.e., $`\hat{S}_{q}=Y_{q}`$),
([12](#S4.E12 "In IV-E Alternative FCP Filtering at Reference Microphone ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering"))
becomes identical to the optimization problem in WPE \[[9](#bib.bib9)\],
a classic dereverberation algorithm. Following WPE, the filtering result
$`\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{q}(f)^{{\mathsf{H}}}\ \widetilde{\hat{\mathbf{S}}}_{q}(t,f)=\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{q}(f)^{{\mathsf{H}}}\ \widetilde{Y}_{q}(t,f)`$
would inevitably, and largely only, approximate late reverberation if
the prediction delay $`\Delta`$ used in defining
$`\widetilde{\hat{\mathbf{S}}}_{q}(t,f)`$ is reasonably large²² 2 This
is because when the prediction delay $`\Delta`$ is set reasonably large,
the target speech in the current T-F unit becomes much less
linearly-correlated with the T-F units that are beyond $`\Delta`$ frames
in the past \[[9](#bib.bib9)\].. As a result, the reconstructed mixture
used for computing $`\mathcal{L}_{\text{MC},q}`$ in
([5](#S4.E5 "In IV-B Mixture-Constraint Loss ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering")),
$`\hat{Y}_{q}(t,f)=\hat{S}_{q}(t,f)+\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{q}(f)^{{\mathsf{H}}}\ \widetilde{\hat{\mathbf{S}}}_{q}(t,f)=Y_{q}(t,f)+\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{q}(f)^{{\mathsf{H}}}\ \widetilde{Y}_{q}(t,f)`$,
would produce a large loss, due to the approximated late reverberation
$`\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{q}(f)^{{\mathsf{H}}}\ \widetilde{Y}_{q}(t,f)`$.
Therefore, using
([12](#S4.E12 "In IV-E Alternative FCP Filtering at Reference Microphone ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering"))
and minimizing $`\mathcal{L}_{\text{MC},q}`$ could help USDnet avoid the
trivial solution. For similar reasons, using
([12](#S4.E12 "In IV-E Alternative FCP Filtering at Reference Microphone ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering"))
could yield better dereverberation than
([8](#S4.E8 "In IV-C FCP for Filter Estimation ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering"))
when multiple microphones are used in loss computation.

### IV-H Difficulties of Time Alignment, and Masking vs. Mapping

In supervised speech separation, the availability of target speech
signals can provide a strong supervision at each sample for DNNs to
learn to produce an estimate that is strictly time-aligned with target
speech. In WPE, a time-aligned estimate can be obtained by only removing
estimated late reverberation. In unsupervised neural dereverberation,
however, we find it particularly difficult to produce an estimate
time-aligned with target speech, simply due to the lack of an explicit
sample-level supervision (e.g., oracle target speech). For
$`\mathcal{L}_{\text{MC},q}`$ in
([5](#S4.E5 "In IV-B Mixture-Constraint Loss ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering")),
even if $`\hat{S}_{q}`$ produced by the DNN is not time-aligned with the
oracle target speech $`S_{q}`$, $`\mathcal{L}_{\text{MC},q}`$ can still
be small because the filter $`\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{q}(f)`$, which is
multi-tap, could compensate the time-misalignment through multi-frame
filtering. This compensation can also happen when we compute
$`\mathcal{L}_{\text{MC},p}`$ in
([6](#S4.E6 "In IV-B Mixture-Constraint Loss ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering")).

To partially address this issue, we propose to use complex masking
instead of mapping to obtain target estimate $`\hat{S}_{q}`$, and
observe that masking obtains better performance. This could be because,
in masking, $`\hat{S}_{q}`$ is produced by masking the mixture
$`Y_{q}`$, while, in mapping, $`\hat{S}_{q}`$ is produced directly by
the DNN and could hence more likely be misaligned with target speech.
For example, when using complex mapping, at each T-F unit the DNN could
just output an estimated signal not time- and gain-aligned with the
mixture, e.g., $`\hat{S}_{q}(t,f)=\beta\times Y_{q}(t+\Delta,f)`$ where
$`\beta\in{\mathbb{C}}`$ denotes an arbitrary scaling factor with
$`|\beta|`$ being small. In this case, in
([12](#S4.E12 "In IV-E Alternative FCP Filtering at Reference Microphone ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering")),
$`\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{q}(f)^{{\mathsf{H}}}\ \widetilde{\hat{\mathbf{S}}}_{q}(t,f)`$
can perfectly approximate $`Y_{q}(t,f)`$ since now
$`\widetilde{\hat{\mathbf{S}}}_{q}(t,f)=\big[\hat{S}_{q}(t-K,f),\dots,\hat{S}_{q}(t-\Delta,f)\big]^{\mathsf{T}}=\beta\times\big[Y_{q}(t+\Delta-K,f),\dots,Y_{q}(t,f)\big]^{\mathsf{T}}`$
has $`Y_{q}(t,f)`$ in the last element; and in
([5](#S4.E5 "In IV-B Mixture-Constraint Loss ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering")),
the resulting reconstructed mixture
$`\hat{Y}_{q}(t,f)=\hat{S}_{q}(t,f)+\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{q}(f)^{\mathsf{H}}\widetilde{\hat{\mathbf{S}}}_{q}(t,f)=\beta\times Y_{q}(t+\Delta,f)+Y_{q}(t,f)`$
would be very close to the mixture $`Y_{q}(t,f)`$ if $`|\beta|`$ is
small, yielding a very small $`\mathcal{L}_{\text{MC},q}`$ even though
the solution is trivial. By using masking, this trivial solution could
be avoided, as masking applies a complex-valued filter to the mixture at
each T-F unit and it is difficult to modify the phase and magnitude of
the mixture in a way such that the resulting estimates would be multiple
frames ahead or behind.

### IV-I Discussion on Why $`\hat{S}_{q}`$ Could Approximate $`S_{q}`$

So far, we have described the benefits of over-determined conditions in
Section
[III-C](#S3.SS3 "III-C Benefits of Over-determined Conditions ‣ III Problem Formulation ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering"),
and various regularizations such as using
([12](#S4.E12 "In IV-E Alternative FCP Filtering at Reference Microphone ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering"))
rather than
([8](#S4.E8 "In IV-C FCP for Filter Estimation ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering"))
for FCP filtering and using masking rather than mapping to obtain target
estimate $`\hat{S}_{q}`$. One more important regularization we have not
discussed is the non-negative prediction delay $`\Delta`$. We find that
these regularizations combined can lead $`\hat{S}_{q}`$ to approximate
$`S_{q}`$. This subsection provides more intuitions and discussions.

In
([5](#S4.E5 "In IV-B Mixture-Constraint Loss ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering")),
although the loss is defined on the reconstructed mixture
$`\hat{Y}_{q}(t,f)=\hat{S}_{q}(t,f)+\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{q}(f)^{\mathsf{H}}\widetilde{\hat{\mathbf{S}}}_{q}(t,f)`$,
our goal is to have $`\hat{S}_{q}`$ approximate direct-path $`S_{q}`$
and
$`\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{q}(f)^{\mathsf{H}}\widetilde{\hat{\mathbf{S}}}_{q}(t,f)`$
approximate reverberation $`R_{q}(t,f)`$. In this case, the prediction
delay $`\Delta`$ has to be positive. If it is zero, $`\hat{S}_{q}`$ can
be absorbed into
$`\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{q}(f)^{\mathsf{H}}\widetilde{\hat{\mathbf{S}}}_{q}(t,f)`$,
and
([5](#S4.E5 "In IV-B Mixture-Constraint Loss ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering"))
would have the same form as
([6](#S4.E6 "In IV-B Mixture-Constraint Loss ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering")).
This would not lead $`\hat{S}_{q}`$ to approximate $`S_{q}`$, as there
are infinite solutions to the estimated filter $`\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{q}`$
and source $`\hat{S}_{q}`$ that can minimize the losses in
([5](#S4.E5 "In IV-B Mixture-Constraint Loss ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering"))
and
([6](#S4.E6 "In IV-B Mixture-Constraint Loss ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering")).

Suppose that the prediction delay $`\Delta`$ is positive, masking is
used, and noise $`\varepsilon`$ is negligible. The loss in
([5](#S4.E5 "In IV-B Mixture-Constraint Loss ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering"))
would encourage $`\hat{S}_{q}`$ to approximate $`S_{q}`$. Our
interpretation consists of three parts:

- •
  When masking is used, $`\hat{S}_{q}`$ would be constrained to be
  approximately time-aligned with $`S_{q}`$ (see our discussion in
  Section
  [IV-H](#S4.SS8 "IV-H Difficulties of Time Alignment, and Masking vs. Mapping ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering")).
- •
  Due to the positive prediction delay,
  $`\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{q}(f)^{\mathsf{H}}\widetilde{\hat{\mathbf{S}}}_{q}(t,f)`$,
  with $`\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{q}(f)`$ computed via
  ([12](#S4.E12 "In IV-E Alternative FCP Filtering at Reference Microphone ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering")),
  largely can only approximate late reverberation.
- •
  To minimize
  ([5](#S4.E5 "In IV-B Mixture-Constraint Loss ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering")),
  $`\hat{S}_{q}`$ and
  $`\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{q}(f)^{\mathsf{H}}\widetilde{\hat{\mathbf{S}}}_{q}(t,f)`$
  need to add up to the mixture. Since
  $`\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{q}(f)^{\mathsf{H}}\widetilde{\hat{\mathbf{S}}}_{q}(t,f)`$
  can only approximate (or explain) the late reverberation component of
  the mixture, $`\hat{S}_{q}`$ has to explain the rest components, which
  mainly include (a) the direct-path signal $`S_{q}`$; and (b) some
  early reflections that cannot be explained by
  $`\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{q}(f)^{\mathsf{H}}\widetilde{\hat{\mathbf{S}}}_{q}(t,f)`$
  when the prediction delay $`\Delta`$ is set to a large positive value.

This interpretation highlights the importance of using masking together
with a non-negative prediction delay $`\Delta`$. If mapping is used
instead, it could predict multiple frames ahead and break the
regularizations enforced by the positive prediction delay.

The third point above suggests that $`\hat{S}_{q}`$ would inevitably
contain some early reflections due to the positive prediction delay.
Similarly to that in WPE \[[9](#bib.bib9)\], this could be fine in many
applications. For example, including some early reflections can improve
speech intelligibility \[[14](#bib.bib14)\].

On the other hand, the third point also suggests that we may need a
noise estimate (besides the speech estimate) to explain the noise
component in the mixture. We will describe this later in Section
[IV-K](#S4.SS11 "IV-K Introducing Garbage Sources ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering").

### IV-J Input Microphone Dropout

When multiple microphone signals are used as the network input and in
loss computation, we find that USDnet often optimizes the loss well but
the dereverberation result is poor. This could be because USDnet sees,
from the input, exactly the same mixtures used in loss computation and,
therefore, could figure out a way to optimize the loss well but not
dereverberate well.

To deal with this issue, we propose to apply dropout
\[[68](#bib.bib68)\] to input non-reference microphone signals, where,
during training, each non-reference microphone signal in the input is
entirely dropped out with a probability of $`\theta`$.

### IV-K Introducing Garbage Sources

To model environmental noises (assumed weak) and the modeling error
caused by narrow-band approximation (i.e., $`\varepsilon^{\prime}`$ in
([1](#S3.E1 "In III-A Physical Models ‣ III Problem Formulation ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering"))
and
([2](#S3.E2 "In III-A Physical Models ‣ III Problem Formulation ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering"))),
we propose to use a garbage source to absorb them.

We modify $`\mathcal{L}_{\text{MC},q}`$ in
([5](#S4.E5 "In IV-B Mixture-Constraint Loss ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering"))
and $`\mathcal{L}_{\text{MC},p}`$ in
([6](#S4.E6 "In IV-B Mixture-Constraint Loss ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering"))
as follows:

| --- | ------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | ---- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{MC},q}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\sum_{t,f}\mathcal{F}\Big(Y_{q}(t,f),`$                                                                                                                                 |     |      |
|     |                                            | $`\displaystyle\hat{S}_{q}(t,f)+\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{q}(f)^{\mathsf{H}}\widetilde{\hat{\mathbf{S}}}_{q}(t,f)+\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{q}(f)^{\mathsf{H}}\breve{\hat{\mathbf{V}}}_{q}(t,f)\Big),`$ |     | (13) |

|     |                                                                       |                                                                                                                                                                       |     |      |
| --- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | ---- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{MC},p}=\sum_{t,f}\mathcal{F}\Big(`$ | $`\displaystyle Y_{p}(t,f),`$                                                                                                                                         |     |      |
|     |                                                                       | $`\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{p}(f)^{\mathsf{H}}\overline{\hat{\mathbf{S}}}_{q}(t,f)+\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{p}(f)^{\mathsf{H}}\breve{\hat{\mathbf{V}}}_{q}(t,f)\Big),`$ |     | (14) |

where the DNN is trained to output one more spectrogram $`\hat{V}_{q}`$
in addition to $`\hat{S}_{q}`$,
$`\breve{\hat{\mathbf{V}}}_{q}(t,f)=\big[\hat{V}_{q}(t-L,f),\dots,\hat{V}_{q}(t+L,f)\big]^{\mathsf{T}}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{L+1+L}`$
stacks a window of $`L+1+L`$ T-F units in $`\hat{V}_{q}`$, and
$`\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{q}(f)`$ and $`\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{p}(f)`$ are
respectively computed in the same ways as in
([8](#S4.E8 "In IV-C FCP for Filter Estimation ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering"))
or
([12](#S4.E12 "In IV-E Alternative FCP Filtering at Reference Microphone ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering")),
and
([9](#S4.E9 "In IV-C FCP for Filter Estimation ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering")).
$`\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{q}(f)`$ and $`\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{p}(f)`$ are computed
as follows, similarly to
([9](#S4.E9 "In IV-C FCP for Filter Estimation ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering")):

|     |                                                                                                         |                                                                                     |                                   |
| --- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------- | --- | ---- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{a}(f)=\underset{\mathbf{w}_{a}(f)}{{\text{argmin}}}\sum\_{t}\frac{\Big | Y*{a}(t,f)-\mathbf{w}*{a}(f)^{{\mathsf{H}}}\ \breve{\hat{\mathbf{V}}}\_{q}(t,f)\Big | ^{2}}{\hat{\lambda}\_{a}(t,f)},`$ |     | (15) |

where $`a`$ indexes all the microphones.

We point out that using garbage sources to absorb modeling errors is a
widely-adopted technique in conventional separation algorithms such as
IVA \[[58](#bib.bib58)\], and CAGMM- and MESSL-based spatial clustering
\[[69](#bib.bib69), [70](#bib.bib70)\]. Our novelty here is adapting
this idea for DNN-based unsupervised dereverberation.

### IV-L Run-Time Inference

At run time, we use $`\hat{S}_{q}`$ as the final prediction. We just
need to run feed-forwarding for inference, without needing to compute
FCP filters.

In other words, USDnet is trained to optimize the
$`\mathcal{L}_{\text{MC}}`$ loss in
([4](#S4.E4 "In IV-B Mixture-Constraint Loss ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering"))
so that it can learn to model signal patterns in reverberant speech for
dereverberation. At run time, it can be used to dereverberate signals in
the same way as models trained in a supervised way. This novel design
makes USDnet possible to be readily integrated with semi-supervised
learning, where, for example, (a) USDnet trained on massive unlabeled
data can be fine-tuned on labeled target-domain data via supervised
learning; and (b) pre-trained supervised models can be adapted in an
unsupervised way on target-domain unlabeled data by initializing USDnet
with the pre-trained model.

Following WPE \[[9](#bib.bib9)\], we can alternatively use
$`Y_{q}(t,f)-\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{q}(f)^{{\mathsf{H}}}\ \widetilde{\hat{\mathbf{S}}}_{q}(t,f)`$
as the final prediction, where the subtracted term is obtained by
filtering past DNN estimates with a prediction delay $`\Delta`$ (in
$`\widetilde{\hat{\mathbf{S}}}_{q}(t,f)`$) and hence can be viewed as an
estimate of reverberation to remove. However, in our experiments the
performance is worse than directly using $`\hat{S}_{q}`$.

## V Experimental Setup

We train USDnet using an existing speech dereverberation dataset, and
evaluate it on its simulated test set and the REVERB dataset. This
section describes the datasets, miscellaneous setup, baseline systems,
and evaluation metrics.

### V-A WSJ0CAM-DEREVERB Dataset

WSJ0CAM-DEREVERB is simulated and has been used in several supervised
dereverberation studies \[[71](#bib.bib71), [31](#bib.bib31),
[64](#bib.bib64)\], which reported strong results. The dry source
signals are from the WSJ0CAM corpus, which has $`7,861`$, $`742`$ and
$`1,088`$ utterances respectively in its training, validation and test
sets. Based on them, $`39,293`$ ($`\sim`$$`77.7`$ h), $`2,968`$
($`\sim`$$`5.6`$ h) and $`3,262`$ ($`\sim`$$`6.4`$ h) noisy-reverberant
mixtures are respectively simulated as the training, validation and test
sets. For each utterance, a room with random room characteristics and
speaker and microphone locations is sampled. The simulated microphone
array has $`8`$ microphones uniformly placed on a circle with a diameter
of $`20`$ cm. The speaker-to-array distance is drawn from the range
$`[0.75,2.5]`$ m and the reverberation time (T60) from $`[0.2,1.3]`$ s.
For each utterance, an $`8`$-channel diffuse air-conditioning noise is
sampled from the REVERB dataset \[[72](#bib.bib72)\], and added to the
reverberant speech at an SNR (between the direct-path signal and the
noise) sampled from the range $`[5,25]`$ dB. The sampling rate is $`16`$
kHz.

### V-B REVERB Dataset

We evaluate USDnet, trained on WSJ0CAM-DEREVERB, directly on the REVERB
dataset \[[72](#bib.bib72)\], which has real near- and far-field
reverberant speech recorded in rooms with air-conditioning noises by an
8-microphone circular array with a diameter of $`20`$ cm. The rooms have
a T60 at around $`0.7`$ s and the speaker to microphone distances are
around $`1`$ m in the near-field case and $`2.5`$ m in the far-field
case. We feed dereverberated signals produced by USDnet to an ASR
backend for recognition. The ASR backend is trained by using the default
recipe in Kaldi³³ 3 commit 61637e6c8ab01d3b4c54a50d9b20781a0aa12a59,
which trains a TDNN-based acoustic model based on the default
multi-condition training data consisting of the noisy-reverberant speech
of REVERB. The sampling rate is $`16`$ kHz.

We emphasize that the purpose of this evaluation is to show the
effectiveness of USDnet on real-recorded data and compare its
performance with WPE, not to obtain state-of-the-art ASR performance on
REVERB.

### V-C Miscellaneous System Setup

The STFT window size is $`32`$ ms, hop size $`8`$ ms, and the square
root of Hann window is used as the analysis window. We use $`512`$-point
discrete Fourier transform to extract $`257`$-dimensional complex
spectra at each frame.

We employ TF-GridNet \[[64](#bib.bib64)\] as the DNN architecture. Using
symbols defined in Table I of \[[64](#bib.bib64)\], we set its
hyper-parameters to $`D=48`$, $`B=4`$, $`I=4`$, $`J=4`$, $`H=192`$,
$`L=4`$ and $`E=2`$. Please do not confuse the symbols of TF-GridNet
with the ones in this paper. When the DNN is trained for complex ratio
masking, we truncate the RI parts of the estimated masks into the range
$`[-5,5]`$ before multiplying it with the mixture.

We consider $`1`$-, $`2`$-, $`4`$- and $`8`$-microphone dereverberation.
The first microphone is always considered as the referenece microphone.
For $`1`$-channel dereverberation, we use the mixture signal captured by
microphone $`1`$; for $`2`$-channel dereverberation, we use microphone
$`1`$ and $`4`$; for $`4`$-channel dereverberation, we use microphone
$`1`$, $`3`$, $`5`$ and $`7`$; and for $`8`$-channel dereverberation,
all the $`8`$ microphones are used.

### V-D Baseline Systems

We consider WPE \[[9](#bib.bib9)\] as the baseline. It is unsupervised
and is so far the most popular and successful dereverberation algorithm.
We use the implementation in the nara*wpe toolkit \[[73](#bib.bib73)\].
The STFT configuration for WPE is the same as that in USDnet. Following
\[[73](#bib.bib73), [12](#bib.bib12)\], the filter tap is tuned to
$`37`$ in monaural cases, to $`10`$ in $`4`$-channel cases, to $`5`$ in
$`8`$-channel cases, the prediction delay is $`3`$, and $`3`$ iterations
is performed. Building upon the configurations of WPE, we additionally
provide the results of DNN-WPE \[[26](#bib.bib26)\], where the target
power spectral density used in WPE is provided by a supervised
dereverberation DNN. The DNN has the same architecture as that in
USDnet. It is trained via complex masking in the same way as USDnet, but
the loss is defined on the RI components and magnitude of estimated
target speech $`\hat{S}*{q}`$ and is weighted by the summation of
mixture magnitude, following the distance metric in
([7](#S4.Ex8 "In IV-B Mixture-Constraint Loss ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering")).

We considered comparing USDnet with unsupervised neural dereverberation
models such as \[[44](#bib.bib44), [46](#bib.bib46), [47](#bib.bib47)\].
However, they only deal with monaural cases and require separate source
prior models trained on anechoic speech or DNN-based metric models (such
as DNSMOS) trained on annotated anechoic and reverberant speech, and
cannot be directly trained solely on a set of reverberant signals.
Differently, USDnet exploits implicit regularizations afforded by
multiple microphones and can be trained directly on a set of reverberant
signals. In other words, they utilize very different signal principles,
which can be likely integrated with USDnet in future studies. We
therefore do not consider them as baselines for comparison, and mainly
focus on showing the effectiveness of leveraging mixture constraints in
this paper.

### V-E Evaluation Metrics

For evaluation on the test set of WSJ0CAM-DEREVERB, we use the
direct-path signal at the first microphone (designated as the reference
microphone) as the reference signal for metric computation. We report
scores of perceptual evaluation of speech quality (PESQ)
\[[74](#bib.bib74)\] and extended short-time objective intelligibility
(eSTOI) \[[75](#bib.bib75)\]. They are widely-adopted objective metrics
of speech quality and intelligibility. We use the python-pesq toolkit to
report narrow-band MOS-LQO scores for PESQ, and the pystoi toolkit for
eSTOI. Additionally, we report scale-invariant signal-to-distortion
ratio (SI-SDR) \[[76](#bib.bib76)\] but do not consider it as a primary
evaluation metric due to the difficulties, and in many applications
unnecessities, of time alignment. For the ASR evaluation on REVERB, we
report word error rates (WER).

All of these metrics, except WER, favor estimated signals that are
time-aligned with reference signals.

### VI-B Illustration of Loss Curve of $`\mathcal{L}_{\text{MC}}`$

To show that minimizing $`\mathcal{L}_{\text{MC}}`$ can promote
unsupervised dereverberation, we plot the loss curve of
$`\mathcal{L}_{\text{MC}}`$ in Fig.
[2](#S6.F2 "Fig. 2 ‣ VI-B Illustration of Loss Curve of ℒ*"MC" ‣ VI Evaluation Results ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering"),
by using a simulated noisy-reverberant speech signal sampled from
WSJ0CAM-DEREVERB. Given the simulated direct-path RIR
$`o*{d}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{M*{d}}`$ and reverberant-speech RIR
$`o*{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{M*{i}}`$, both at the reference microphone
$`q`$, we first compute the relative RIR $`o*{r}`$ relating the
direct-path signal to reverberant speech as follows:

|     |                                                                                                                                    |     |      |
| --- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | ---- |
|     | $`\displaystyle o_{r}=\text{iFFT}\Big(\frac{\text{FFT}(o_{i},M_{r})}{\text{FFT}(o_{d},M_{r})},M_{r}\Big)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{M_{r}},`$ |     | (16) |

where $`M_{r}=M_{i}+M_{d}-1`$, $`\text{FFT}(\cdot,M_{r})`$ computes an
$`M_{r}`$-point fast Fourier transform (FFT) of the RIR, and
$`\text{iFFT}(\cdot,M_{r})`$ computes an $`M_{r}`$-point inverse FFT
(iFFT). We then compute a hypothesized dereverberation result as

|     |                                                                                             |     |      |
| --- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | ---- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\hat{S}_{q}=\text{STFT}\big(s_{q}*\operatorname{T}_{0}^{\tau}(o_{r})\big),`$ |     | (17) |

where $`s_{q}`$ is the time-domain direct-path signal corresponding to
$`S_{q}`$, the operator $`*`$ denotes linear convolution, and
$`\operatorname{T}_{0}^{\tau}(\cdot)`$ truncates the RIR to length
$`\tau`$ so that we can control how reverberant $`\hat{S}_{q}`$ is. We
then use $`\hat{S}_{q}`$ to compute $`\mathcal{L}_{\text{MC}}`$ based on
the hyper-parameters shown in row 2d of Table
[II](#S6.T2 "TABLE II ‣ VI-A Effects of Using More Microphones in Loss and Input ‣ VI Evaluation Results ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering"),
and plot the loss curve against $`\tau`$. From Fig.
[2](#S6.F2 "Fig. 2 ‣ VI-B Illustration of Loss Curve of ℒ*"MC" ‣ VI Evaluation Results ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering"),
we observe much smaller $`\mathcal{L}*{\text{MC}}`$ when $`\hat{S}_{q}`$
is configured less reverberant. This partially supports our claim that
minimizing $`\mathcal{L}_{\text{MC}}`$ can promote dereverberation.

Fig. 2: Loss curve of $`\mathcal{L}_{\text{MC}}`$ against truncated
length of relative RIR, $`\tau`$. For this example utterance, the T60 is

for more details.

### VI-C Effects of Masking vs. Mapping

In row 4a-4c of Table
[II](#S6.T2 "TABLE II ‣ VI-A Effects of Using More Microphones in Loss and Input ‣ VI Evaluation Results ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering"),
we use complex spectral mapping instead of complex masking. The results
are clearly worse than those in 2a, 2d and 3d, respectively, and, in
particular, the SI-SDR scores are much lower. This is because, in
complex spectral mapping, the DNN has fewer restrictions in producing
target estimates, and this would result in trivial solutions where
reverberation is not reduced (see our discussions in Section
[IV-H](#S4.SS8 "IV-H Difficulties of Time Alignment, and Masking vs. Mapping ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering")).
During training, we indeed observe that, when using mapping, the
training loss often suddenly decreases to values very close to zero.

In the rest of this paper, we use masking in default.

### VI-E Effects of Non-Negative Prediction Delay $`\Delta`$

The prediction delay $`\Delta`$ in defining
$`\widetilde{\hat{\mathbf{S}}}_{q}(t,f)`$ (see the text below
([6](#S4.E6 "In IV-B Mixture-Constraint Loss ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering")))
is a hyper-parameter to tune. Ideally, for $`\mathcal{L}_{\text{MC},q}`$
in
([5](#S4.E5 "In IV-B Mixture-Constraint Loss ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering")),
we want $`\hat{S}_{q}(t,f)`$ to approximate the direct-path signal and
$`\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{q}(f)^{\mathsf{H}}\widetilde{\hat{\mathbf{S}}}_{q}(t,f)`$
to approximate all the reflections so that their summation can add up to
the mixture $`Y_{q}`$. When using
([12](#S4.E12 "In IV-E Alternative FCP Filtering at Reference Microphone ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering"))
to compute $`\mathbf{g}_{q}(f)`$, a large $`\Delta`$ would make
$`\mathbf{g}_{q}(f)^{{\mathsf{H}}}\ \widetilde{\hat{\mathbf{S}}}_{q}(t,f)`$
in the numerator not capable of approximating early reflections, and a
small $`\Delta`$ could result in a
$`\mathbf{g}_{q}(f)^{{\mathsf{H}}}\ \widetilde{\hat{\mathbf{S}}}_{q}(t,f)`$
that cancels out target direct-path speech. In Table
[IV](#S6.T4 "TABLE IV ‣ VI-F Effects of Filter Length K and I ‣ VI Evaluation Results ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering"),
we sweep $`\Delta`$ based on the set of $`\{1,2,3,4\}`$, and observe
that setting $`\Delta`$ to $`3`$ or $`2`$ results in better performance.
However, the performance differences are not very large.

## VII Miscellaneous Discussions

This section discusses USDnet in aspects other than dereverberation
performance, putting USDnet is a broader context.

#### VII-1 Algorithmic Complexity

As described in Section
[IV-L](#S4.SS12 "IV-L Run-Time Inference ‣ IV USDnet ‣ USDnet: Unsupervised Speech Dereverberation via Neural Forward Filtering"),
at run time the trained model performs inference in the same way as
existing supervised dereverberation algorithms (i.e., just running a
forward pass to obtain target estimate $`\hat{S}_{q}`$). During
training, the extra amount of computation compared to supervised
algorithms stems from estimating FCP filters and computing the
$`\mathcal{L}_{\text{MC}}`$ loss, which are typically much less costly
than modern DNN modules.

#### VII-2 Amenability to Online Dereverberation

USDnet can be easily configured for online derevebreration. We only need
to change the DNN to be causal. We leave this investigation to future
research.

#### VII-3 Array-geometry-invariant Modeling

USDnet can be modified to be trained on mixtures recorded by arrays with
diverse microphone geometries. Clearly, the $`\mathcal{L}_{\text{MC}}`$
loss is invariant to microphone geometry. We only need to use a DNN
architecture that can model mixtures with a diverse number of input
microphones, by following, e.g., the VarArray approach
\[[77](#bib.bib77)\]. We leave this to future research.

## VIII Limitations

A limitation of USDnet stems from the assumption that there is only one
target speaker source and the considered noises are weak and stationary.
In realistic conditions (such as in conversational applications), there
could be an unknown number of target speaker sources and an unknown
number of directional and diffuse sources, and we need to address
simultaneous separation, dereverberation and enhancement. Future
research will modify USDnet for these more challenging cases. Our recent
studies such as SuperM2M \[[78](#bib.bib78)\] and cross-talk reduction
\[[79](#bib.bib79)\] have made initial investigation along this
direction.

Another limitation is that, in our experiments, multi-channel USDnet
trained with, e.g., eight-channel input and loss shows worse performance
than single-channel USDnet trained with, e.g, single-channel input and
eight-channel loss. This issue is likely because in the case of
eight-channel input and loss, USDnet sees the same mixtures used in loss
computation from the input, and hence could figure out a way to optimize
the loss well but not dereverberate well. This issue needs to be
addressed in future research to build better multi-channel models.

## IX Conclusion

We have formulated unsupervised neural speech dereverberation as a blind
deconvolution problem, which requires estimating both the source and
filter, and proposed a novel algorithm named USDnet, which leverages
over-determined training mixtures as constraints to narrow down the
solutions to target speech. Evaluation results on two dereverberation
tasks show that USDnet, even if only trained on a set of reverberant
mixtures in an unsupervised way, can learn to effectively reduce
reverberation. Future research will modify USDnet and evaluate it on
real-recorded conversational speech dereverberation and separation tasks
with multiple sound sources.

A contribution of this paper, we highlight, is our finding that DNNs can
be trained directly on a set of reverberant mixtures, in an unsupervised
way, to reduce reverberation, by solely exploiting mixture constraints
afforded by multi-microphone mixtures. The novel way of modeling
reverberation and the novel concept of leveraging mixture constraints
exploit signal cues and physical principles very different from the ones
utilized by existing algorithms. The proposed algorithms, hence, we
think, would in many ways complement existing dereverberation and
separation algorithms, and motivate new ones.

In closing, another contribution of this paper, we point out, is our
unsupervised deep learning based approach for solving blind
deconvolution problems, which exist not only in speech dereverberation
but also in many other engineering applications. By modeling source
priors in an unsupervised way, filter estimation becomes a
differentiable operation given a source estimate. This enables us to
train DNNs on signal patterns in a discriminative way to optimize
mixture-constraint loss and realize unsupervised deconvolution. This
novel methodological contribution, we think, could generate broader
impact beyond speech dereverberation.
