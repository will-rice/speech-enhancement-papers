# End-to-End Multi-speaker ASR with Independent Vector Analysis

Robin Scheibler    Wangyou Zhang    Xuankai Chang    Shinji Watanabe   
Yanmin Qian

###### Abstract

We develop an end-to-end system for multi-channel, multi-speaker
automatic speech recognition. We propose a frontend for joint source
separation and dereverberation based on the independent vector analysis
(IVA) paradigm. It uses the fast and stable iterative source steering
algorithm together with a neural source model. The parameters from the
ASR module and the neural source model are optimized jointly from the
ASR loss itself. We demonstrate competitive performance with previous
systems using neural beamforming frontends. First, we explore the
trade-offs when using various number of channels for training and
testing. Second, we demonstrate that the proposed IVA frontend performs
well on noisy data, even when trained on clean mixtures only.
Furthermore, it extends without retraining to the separation of more
speakers, which is demonstrated on mixtures of three and four speakers.

## 1 Introduction

Automatic speech recognition (ASR) technology provides a natural
interface for human-to-machine communication \[[1](#bib.bib1)\]. Despite
tremendous progress in the last decade, ASR systems are still severely
challenged by reverberation, overlapped speech, and noise
\[[2](#bib.bib2)\]. Microphone arrays are a powerful tool to fight these
degradations. In particular, linear spatial filtering, i.e.,
beamforming, has been shown to reliably decrease the word error rate
(WER) of ASR systems \[[2](#bib.bib2)\]. While optimal beamforming
formulations exist \[[3](#bib.bib3)\], e.g. the famous minimum variance
distortionless response (MVDR) beamformer, their use has been
traditionally limited by the difficulty of estimating the target and
noise statistics. These limitations have been recently practically
solved by using trained neural networks to estimate these statistics
\[[4](#bib.bib4)\]. The resulting neural beamformers are highly
effective \[[5](#bib.bib5)\]. However, training these networks requires
a large amount of parallel speech data, e.g. reverberant mixtures and
the isolated anechoic sources they contain. Such data is notoriously
difficult to collect. Instead, most works rely on simulation
\[[6](#bib.bib6), [7](#bib.bib7)\]. However, the simulation is often
insufficient, and some fully unsupervised approaches have been proposed
\[[8](#bib.bib8), [9](#bib.bib9)\].

The situation for ASR systems is much different since transcripts of
actual recordings may be collected by skilled annotators
\[[1](#bib.bib1)\]. Indeed, a large amount of annotated speech data has
been collected for academic and commercial purposes
\[[10](#bib.bib10)\]. One can thus bypass the necessity of parallel
speech data by concatenating enhancement and ASR systems, and training
directly from the ASR loss \[[11](#bib.bib11), [12](#bib.bib12)\].
Building on this approach, an end-to-end (E2E) paradigm for
multi-channel, multi-speaker ASR called MIMO-speech \[[13](#bib.bib13)\]
has been proposed. This approach has demonstrated not only competitive
ASR, but also decent separation performance, trained from the ASR loss
only. It has been extended to include several advanced joint
dereverberation and beamforming methods \[[14](#bib.bib14),
[15](#bib.bib15)\]. Despite all these progresses, the challenge of
domain mismatch remains. Neural beamformers for separation typically
rely on estimating multiple sources, usually two, from a single
spectrogram. If the test data is sufficiently different from the input
data, this stage may fail, impeding the beamforming performance.

An alternative line of research builds upon independent vector analysis
(IVA) \[[16](#bib.bib16), [17](#bib.bib17)\]. In addition to a
statistical model of the sources, their mutual statistical independence
is leveraged to help the separation. Vanilla IVA is a blind method,
requiring no training data, that can be solved iteratively
\[[18](#bib.bib18), [19](#bib.bib19)\], and rivals sophisticated neural
beamformers \[[20](#bib.bib20)\]. Extensions to joint dereverberation
and separation have been proposed \[[21](#bib.bib21)\]. In particular,
time-decorrelation iterative source steering (T-ISS)
\[[22](#bib.bib22)\] is a stable and fast algorithm that avoids matrix
inversion. Recently, combining IVA with a neural source model has
attracted attention \[[23](#bib.bib23), [24](#bib.bib24)\]. One
particular approach proposes to train a neural source model end-to-end
through T-ISS \[[19](#bib.bib19), [25](#bib.bib25)\]. Unlike in neural
beamforming, the network models a single source. This allows to maintain
the high performance of neural beamforming while being agnostic to the
number of sources and channels, and robust to a fair amount of data
mismatch \[[25](#bib.bib25)\].

In this work, we investigate the use of a T-ISS frontend for MIMO-speech
E2E ASR. We extend T-ISS to the overdetermined case, where more channels
than sources are present \[[26](#bib.bib26)\] We implement our model in
ESPnet \[[27](#bib.bib27)\] with an E2E transformer-based ASR backend
\[[28](#bib.bib28)\]. The whole system is trained E2E by joint
CTC/attention loss \[[29](#bib.bib29)\]. We use variations of the
spatialized WSJ1 \[[13](#bib.bib13)\] dataset, clean, and corrupted with
several kinds of noise. We explore how the number of channels at
training affects test performance (spoiler: more is better). We
demonstrate the robustness of T-ISS to mismatch with training in terms
of noise and number of sources.

## 2 Background

We use the following notation. Bold lower and upper case letters are for
vector and matrices, respectively. Furthermore,
$`\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}`$ and $`\boldsymbol{A}^{H}`$ denote the
transpose and conjugate transpose, respectively, of matrix
$`\boldsymbol{A}`$. The norm of vector $`\boldsymbol{v}`$ is
$`\|\boldsymbol{v}\|=(\boldsymbol{v}^{H}\boldsymbol{v})^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}`$.

Figure 1: Illustration of the MIMO-speech E2E system for $`K`$ source
and $`M`$ microphones.

### 2.1 MIMO-Speech and Its Extensions

We are concerned with multi-channel, multi-speaker ASR systems. The
MIMO-Speech method \[[13](#bib.bib13), [30](#bib.bib30)\] proposes a
fully end-to-end framework that jointly optimizes the entire system with
only the final ASR criterion. The model consists of a beamforming-based
frontend for speech separation and an E2E ASR backend. It takes $`M`$
channels, as inputs, and outputs $`K`$ text hypotheses, corresponding to
$`K`$ concurrent speakers. First, the frontend extracts $`K`$ source
signals from the input mixture. Second, each of the extracted signal is
processed by the same E2E ASR backend in parallel. This produces $`K`$
text hypotheses that are evaluated against the $`K`$ reference
transcripts with a permutation invariant training (PIT) loss
\[[31](#bib.bib31)\]. The process is illustrated in Fig.
[1](#S2.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 2 Background ‣ End-to-End Multi-speaker ASR with Independent Vector Analysis").

### 2.2 Multichannel Speech Separation and Dereverberation

Physically, the signals from the $`K`$ sources propagate and reflect on
the walls of a room, and mix additively with various amplitudes and time
delays at the $`M`$ microphones. This process can be approximated in the
short-time Fourier transform (STFT) domain as follows,

|     |                                                                                                                                                                    |     |     |
| --- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}_{fn}=\boldsymbol{A}_{f}\boldsymbol{s}_{fn}+\boldsymbol{Z}_{f}\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{fn}+\boldsymbol{b}_{fn},\quad\in\mathbb{C}^{M},`$ |     | (1) |

where $`\boldsymbol{s}_{fn}\in\mathbb{C}^{K}`$ is a vector containing
the source signals. The matrix
$`\boldsymbol{A}_{f}\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times K}`$ contains the transfer
functions from sources to microphones in its entries. The term
$`\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{fn}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{x}_{f,n-D}^{\top},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{f,n-D-L}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}`$
accounts for reflections from the room mixed by the matrix
$`\boldsymbol{Z}_{f}\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times ML}`$. The reverberation
length is given by $`L`$, and $`D`$ is a delay necessary due to the
overlap of the STFT. Finally, $`\boldsymbol{b}_{fn}\in\mathbb{C}^{M}`$
is a noise term The indices $`f`$ and $`n`$ are for frequency bins and
STFT frames, and run from 1 to $`F`$ and $`N`$, respectively. The role
of the frontend is to reduce the second and third terms, as much as
possible, and invert $`\boldsymbol{A}_{f}`$, if feasible.

#### 2.2.1 Neural Dereverberation and Beamforming

Conventionally, dereverberation and beamforming are done in distinct
steps. While several dereverberation methods exist, WPE
\[[32](#bib.bib32)\] has been widely adopted for ASR. Ignoring the noise
term $`\boldsymbol{b}_{fn}`$, the dereverberated mixture
$`\boldsymbol{A}_{f}\boldsymbol{s}_{fn}`$ can be obtained if we know
$`\boldsymbol{Z}_{f}`$. Provided with a neural network producing a mask
$`u^{\text{WPE}}_{fn}`$ hiding the target signal from the spectrogram,
the dereverberation filters are given by the minimizer of
$`\sum_{n}u_{fn}^{\text{WPE}}\|\boldsymbol{x}_{fn}-\boldsymbol{Z}_{f}\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{fn}\|^{2}`$.
The beamforming filters are computed from the spatial covariance
matrices of target speech and noise. Since these are typically not
available, a neural network is trained to estimate masks that extract
these signals power from a spectrogram. Let $`u_{fn}`$ be one of these
masks. Then, the corresponding spatial covariance matrix is
$`{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}_{f}=\nicefrac{{1}}{{N}}\sum_{n}u_{fn}\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{fn}\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{fn}^{H}`$,
where $`\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{fn}`$ is the dereverberated input
signal. For the separation of multiple sources, the neural network is
trained to output masks for each of the sources. Several ways of
combining or sharing masks between steps have been proposed and give
rise to different neural beamformers such as MVDR, WPD
\[[33](#bib.bib33)\], and wMPDR \[[34](#bib.bib34)\]. See
\[[14](#bib.bib14)\] for the details. We emphasize here that after the
masks are estimated, the beamforming filters are estimated
independently. Thus, if the mask estimation fails, it cannot be
corrected during the filter computation step.

#### 2.2.2 Independence-based Dereverberation and Separation

This approach builds upon independent vector analysis (IVA)
\[[16](#bib.bib16), [17](#bib.bib17)\]. Unlike, the neural beamforming
approach, the foundational hypothesis of IVA is that multiple
statistically independent sources are present. The approach has been
extended to jointly optimize for dereverberation \[[21](#bib.bib21),
[22](#bib.bib22)\] and include a trainable neural source model
\[[19](#bib.bib19), [25](#bib.bib25)\]. We define the demixing and
dereverberation matrices as
$`\boldsymbol{W}_{f}\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times M}`$ and
$`\boldsymbol{U}_{f}\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times ML}`$, respectively. For
convenience, we concatenate them into a unified dereverberation and
separation matrix
$`\boldsymbol{P}_{f}=[\boldsymbol{W}_{f}\ \boldsymbol{U}_{f}]`$. Further
let $`\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{fn}`$ be the concatenation of
$`\boldsymbol{x}_{fn}`$ and $`\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{fn}`$. The $`k`$th
row of $`\boldsymbol{P}_{f}`$, i.e. $`\boldsymbol{p}_{kf}^{H}`$,
extracts the $`k`$th target as
$`y_{kfn}=\boldsymbol{p}_{kf}^{H}\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{fn}`$. Maximum
likelihood estimation of $`\boldsymbol{P}_{f}`$ can be done by
iteratively minimizing the cost function

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{P}_{f})=\sum_{kfn}u_{fn}(\hat{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{k})|\boldsymbol{p}_{kf}^{H}\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{fn}|^{2}-2\log|\det\boldsymbol{W}_{f}|
``` |  | (2) |

where $`\boldsymbol{p}_{kf}^{H}`$ is the $`k`$th row of
$`\boldsymbol{P}_{f}`$. The $`F\times N`$ matrix
$`\hat{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{k}`$ is the current estimate of source $`k`$
with entries $`(\hat{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{k})_{fn}=\hat{y}_{kfn}`$. The cost
function
([2](#S2.E2 "In 2.2.2 Independence-based Dereverberation and Separation ‣ 2.2 Multichannel Speech Separation and Dereverberation ‣ 2 Background ‣ End-to-End Multi-speaker ASR with Independent Vector Analysis"))
is derived from the likelihood function of the observed data
$`\boldsymbol{x}_{fn}`$ \[[21](#bib.bib21)\]. The change of variable
from $`\boldsymbol{x}_{fn}`$ to $`\boldsymbol{Y}_{k}`$ allows to work on
the source signals, rather than the mixture, but introduces the
log-determinant term. Then, since the sources are independent, the joint
probability density function (pdf) is the product of the marginals.
Finally, we assume the log-pdf may be majorized by that of the Normal
distribution to obtain
([2](#S2.E2 "In 2.2.2 Independence-based Dereverberation and Separation ‣ 2.2 Multichannel Speech Separation and Dereverberation ‣ 2 Background ‣ End-to-End Multi-speaker ASR with Independent Vector Analysis"))
\[[18](#bib.bib18)\]. The function $`u_{fn}(\boldsymbol{Y})`$ is derived
from the majorization step, and guarantees decrease of
([2](#S2.E2 "In 2.2.2 Independence-based Dereverberation and Separation ‣ 2.2 Multichannel Speech Separation and Dereverberation ‣ 2 Background ‣ End-to-End Multi-speaker ASR with Independent Vector Analysis")).
In \[[19](#bib.bib19), [25](#bib.bib25)\], this exact derivation is
abandoned, together with the guarantees, and $`u_{fn}(\boldsymbol{Y})`$
is replaced by a trainable neural network.

While minimization of
([2](#S2.E2 "In 2.2.2 Independence-based Dereverberation and Separation ‣ 2.2 Multichannel Speech Separation and Dereverberation ‣ 2 Background ‣ End-to-End Multi-speaker ASR with Independent Vector Analysis"))
does not have a closed-form solution, algorithms to efficiently decrease
its value exist \[[21](#bib.bib21), [22](#bib.bib22)\]. T-ISS
\[[22](#bib.bib22)\] is particularly suitable for use in E2E training
because of low computational cost and lack of matrix inversion. The
algorithm proceeds by finding a sequence of $`\ell=1,\ldots,M(L+1)`$
optimal rank-1 updates of the form

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\boldsymbol{P}_{f}\leftarrow\boldsymbol{P}_{f}-\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell}\boldsymbol{p}_{\ell f}^{H},\quad\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell}=\underset{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{C}^{M}}{\arg\min}\ \mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{P}_{f}-\boldsymbol{v}\boldsymbol{p}_{\ell f}^{H}).`$ |  | (3) |

For $`\ell>M`$, we define
$`\boldsymbol{p}_{\ell f}=\boldsymbol{e}_{\ell}`$, i.e., the vector with
all zeros but a one at position $`\ell`$. The closed form solution for
$`\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell}`$ in
([3](#S2.E3 "In 2.2.2 Independence-based Dereverberation and Separation ‣ 2.2 Multichannel Speech Separation and Dereverberation ‣ 2 Background ‣ End-to-End Multi-speaker ASR with Independent Vector Analysis"))
is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell})_{q}=\begin{cases}1-\left(\sum_{n}\frac{u_{fn}(\bm{Y}_{\ell})}{N}|y_{\ell fn}|^{2}\right)^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}},&\text{if $q=\ell$,}\\
\frac{\sum_{n}u_{fn}(\bm{Y}_{q})y_{qfn}y^{*}_{\ell fn}}{\sum_{n}u_{fn}(\bm{Y}_{q})|y_{\ell fn}|^{2}},&\text{else}.\end{cases}`$ |  | (4) |

where $`y_{qfn}=\boldsymbol{p}_{qf}^{H}\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{fn}`$ for
$`q\leq M`$, and
$`y_{qfn}=\boldsymbol{e}_{\ell}^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{fn}`$
else. In contrast to the neural beamformer in \[[14](#bib.bib14),
[15](#bib.bib15)\], spatial cues are taken into account when estimating
the source masks. Because the neural network models a single source, the
algorithm is easily extended to different numbers of sources. It was
also shown to be robust to domain mismatch \[[25](#bib.bib25)\].

## 3 Proposed End-to-end Architecture

![Refer to caption](2204.00218v1/algorithm_structure.png)

Figure 2: Block diagram of the proposed frontend and neural source
model.

Our proposed system builds upon the latest methodology of MIMO-Speech
\[[15](#bib.bib15)\]. We replace the WPD beamforming frontend by an
IVA-based one that performs joint dereverberation and separation. During
training, multiple iterations of T-ISS are run to obtain the separation
matrix. The same single source mask network is used for all iterations
and separation output. The proposed frontend is illustrated in Fig.
[2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3 Proposed End-to-end Architecture ‣ End-to-End Multi-speaker ASR with Independent Vector Analysis").
The system is trained E2E from the ASR loss as illustrated in Fig.
[1](#S2.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 2 Background ‣ End-to-End Multi-speaker ASR with Independent Vector Analysis").

### 3.1 ASR Transformer Model

We adopt the joint connectionist temporal classification
(CTC)/attention-based encoder-decoder \[[29](#bib.bib29)\] as the ASR
backend, which consists of four submodules: feature extraction, encoder,
CTC, and attention-decoder. For each separated stream
$`\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_{k}`$ from the frontend, 80-dimensional log-Mel
filterbank features $`\mathbf{O}_{k}`$ are firstly extracted via the
feature extraction module $`\operatorname{MVN-LMF}`$. The extracted
feature is then fed into the ASR encoder to obtain hidden
representations $`\mathbf{H}_{k}`$, which are used in both CTC and
attention-decoder submodules for recognition. The ASR procedure is
summarized as follows:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathbf{O}_{k}=\operatorname{MVN-LMF}(\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_{k}),&\mathbf{H}_{k}=\operatorname{Enc}(\mathbf{O}_{k}),\\
\hat{\mathbf{R}}^{\text{(ctc)}}_{k}=\operatorname{CTC}(\mathbf{H}_{k}),&\hat{\mathbf{R}}^{\text{(dec)}}_{k}=\operatorname{AttentionDec}(\mathbf{H}_{k}),\end{array}`$ |  |

where $`\operatorname{MVN-LMF}`$ denotes extracting log-Mel filterbank
features and applying mean-variance normalization.
$`\hat{\mathbf{R}}^{\text{(ctc)}}_{k}`$ and
$`\hat{\mathbf{R}}^{\text{(dec)}}_{k}`$ are recognition results from CTC
and attention-decoder submodules, respectively. The ASR loss function is
constructed based on multi-task learning:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{asr}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\alpha\mathcal{L}_{ctc}+(1-\alpha)\mathcal{L}_{dec},`$ |  | (7) |

where $`0<\alpha<1`$ is an interpolation factor. Note that the
permutation invariant training (PIT) \[[31](#bib.bib31)\] method is
applied in the CTC submodule as in \[[13](#bib.bib13),
[30](#bib.bib30)\] to solve the label permutation problem when
processing multiple separated streams.

### 3.2 Overdetermined T-ISS Frontend

Our independence-based frontend is a new extension of the T-ISS
\[[22](#bib.bib22)\] algorithm, described in this sub-section, that can
be used when more channels are available than there are sources. When
there are more channels than sources, i.e., $`K<M`$, the separation
matrix $`\boldsymbol{W}_{f}`$ is not square anymore and the algorithm of
Section
[2.2.2](#S2.SS2.SSS2 "2.2.2 Independence-based Dereverberation and Separation ‣ 2.2 Multichannel Speech Separation and Dereverberation ‣ 2 Background ‣ End-to-End Multi-speaker ASR with Independent Vector Analysis")
is not sufficient anymore. An extension of ISS to the overdetermined
case has been proposed, but for separation only \[[26](#bib.bib26)\]. We
extend it here to include dereverberation.

First, we write the overdetermined dereverberation and separation
operation as a determined system, i.e., square,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{y}_{fn}\\
\boldsymbol{z}_{fn}\\
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{fn}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{W}_{f}&\boldsymbol{U}_{f}\\
\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{J}_{f}&-\boldsymbol{I}_{M-N}\end{bmatrix}&\boldsymbol{0}\\
\boldsymbol{0}&\boldsymbol{I}_{ML}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{x}_{fn}\\
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{fn}\end{bmatrix}.`$ |  | (8) |

The separated target sources are
$`\boldsymbol{y}_{fn}\in\mathbb{C}^{K}`$, and
$`\boldsymbol{z}_{fn}\in\mathbb{C}^{M-K}`$ is a vector of background
noise sources to make the system determined. The top part contains
$`\boldsymbol{P}_{f}=[\boldsymbol{W}_{f}\ \boldsymbol{U}_{f}]`$, of
Section
[2.2.2](#S2.SS2.SSS2 "2.2.2 Independence-based Dereverberation and Separation ‣ 2.2 Multichannel Speech Separation and Dereverberation ‣ 2 Background ‣ End-to-End Multi-speaker ASR with Independent Vector Analysis"),
but with $`K`$ rows, since we only wish to extract so many sources. To
complete the separation matrix, we add the strict minimum of parameters
$`\boldsymbol{J}_{f}\in\mathbb{C}^{M-K\times K}`$. The zeros on the
right of the middle block reflect that we do not need to dereverberate
the background noise. Previous work \[[35](#bib.bib35),
[36](#bib.bib36), [37](#bib.bib37)\] has shown that a necessary
condition for optimality is that the target sources and the noise vector
be orthogonal, i.e.,
$`\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{y}_{fn}\boldsymbol{z}_{fn}^{H}\right]=\boldsymbol{0}`$.
From this and
([8](#S3.E8 "In 3.2 Overdetermined T-ISS Frontend ‣ 3 Proposed End-to-end Architecture ‣ End-to-End Multi-speaker ASR with Independent Vector Analysis")),
we obtain an equation for $`\boldsymbol{J}`$,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle((\boldsymbol{W}_{f}{\rm\bf R}_{f}+\boldsymbol{U}\overline{\boldsymbol{C}}_{f})\boldsymbol{E}_{1})\boldsymbol{J}^{H}=((\boldsymbol{W}_{f}{\rm\bf R}_{f}+\boldsymbol{U}\overline{\boldsymbol{C}}_{f})\boldsymbol{E}_{2}),`$ |  | (9) |

where
$`{\rm\bf R}_{f}=\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{x}_{fn}\boldsymbol{x}_{fn}^{H}\right]`$,
$`\overline{\boldsymbol{C}}_{f}=\mathbb{E}\left[\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{fn}\boldsymbol{x}_{fn}^{H}\right]`$,
and $`\boldsymbol{E}_{1}`$, $`\boldsymbol{E}_{2}`$ are of the
appropriate shape, and such that
$`[\boldsymbol{E}_{1}\ \boldsymbol{E}_{2}]=\boldsymbol{I}`$. Following
the methodology of \[[26](#bib.bib26)\], we update
$`\boldsymbol{P}_{f}=[\boldsymbol{W}_{f}\ \boldsymbol{U}_{f}]`$ and
$`\boldsymbol{J}_{f}`$ in two steps.

1.  1.
    We update
    $`\boldsymbol{P}_{f}=[\boldsymbol{W}_{f}\boldsymbol{U}_{f}]`$ with
    ([3](#S2.E3 "In 2.2.2 Independence-based Dereverberation and Separation ‣ 2.2 Multichannel Speech Separation and Dereverberation ‣ 2 Background ‣ End-to-End Multi-speaker ASR with Independent Vector Analysis"))
    and
    ([4](#S2.E4 "In 2.2.2 Independence-based Dereverberation and Separation ‣ 2.2 Multichannel Speech Separation and Dereverberation ‣ 2 Background ‣ End-to-End Multi-speaker ASR with Independent Vector Analysis")),
    but limit the size of $`\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell}`$ to $`K`$ to match
    the size of $`\boldsymbol{W}_{f}`$, $`\boldsymbol{U}_{f}`$ in
    ([8](#S3.E8 "In 3.2 Overdetermined T-ISS Frontend ‣ 3 Proposed End-to-end Architecture ‣ End-to-End Multi-speaker ASR with Independent Vector Analysis")).
2.  2.
    Update $`\boldsymbol{J}`$ by solving
    ([9](#S3.E9 "In 3.2 Overdetermined T-ISS Frontend ‣ 3 Proposed End-to-end Architecture ‣ End-to-End Multi-speaker ASR with Independent Vector Analysis")).

The resulting algorithm maintains the low-complexity of T-ISS while
allowing to use more channels for increased separation power. We note
that one matrix inverse is introduced in step 2. However, the size of
the matrix to invert is only $`K\times K`$, with e.g., $`K=2`$ for two
sources. Despite this small size, we observed some stability issues. The
system matrix is not Hermitian symmetric, and its eigenvalues not always
positive. Thus, straight diagonal loading, as in \[[15](#bib.bib15)\],
does not guarantee stability. Our solution is to replace the
$`d\times d`$ system $`\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{b}`$ by

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{A}^{H}\boldsymbol{D}^{-1}\boldsymbol{A}+\epsilon\boldsymbol{I})\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{A}^{H}\boldsymbol{D}^{-1}\boldsymbol{b},`$ |  | (10) |

where $`\boldsymbol{D}`$ is a diagonal matrix containing the square
norms of the rows of $`\boldsymbol{A}`$. The system matrix is now
guaranteed positive definite. Clearly, if $`\epsilon=0`$, the solution
is the same as the original system. Furthermore, normalizing the rows of
$`\boldsymbol{A}`$ with $`\boldsymbol{D}`$ ensures the sum of the
eigenvalues of the system matrix is $`d`$. This allows a numerically
sensible choice of $`\epsilon`$.

## 4 Experiments

We conducted several experiments to assess the performance of the
proposed method. We investigate the impact of the number of channels,
iterations, and the presence of noise.

### 4.1 Experimental Conditions

We evaluate the proposed method on three datasets derived from the WSJ1
corpus \[[38](#bib.bib38)\]. For training and test on clean and noisy
mixtures, we use wsj1_spatialized \[[13](#bib.bib13)\], and a similar
dataset \[[19](#bib.bib19)\] that includes noise from the CHiME3
challenge \[[39](#bib.bib39)\]. These two datasets have 8 and 6
channels, respectively. For noisy training, we concatenate the clean and
noisy training sets. The third dataset is used for mismatched testing
and remixes the clean test mixtures with simulated diffuse noise
\[[6](#bib.bib6)\] created from the TUT environmental sound database
\[[40](#bib.bib40)\] with SNR of
$`5\text{\,}\mathrm{dB}15\text{\,}\mathrm{dB}`$. Unlike previous work
\[[14](#bib.bib14), [15](#bib.bib15)\], we did not do multi-condition
training and used only the reverberant mixtures. All the input speech is
sampled at $`16\text{\,}\mathrm{kHz}`$. The STFT uses a
$`25\text{\,}\mathrm{ms}`$ long Hann window with
$`10\text{\,}\mathrm{ms}`$ shifts. The FFT is zero padded to length
$`512`$ producing $`257`$-dimensional spectral feature vectors. After
the frontend, spectrograms are converted to 80-dimensional log
Mel-filterbank features. The training was conducted on an NVidia V100
graphical processing unit (GPU) with $`32\text{\,}\mathrm{GB}`$ RAM.

All the models are implemented in ESPnet \[[27](#bib.bib27)\] using the
PyTorch \[[41](#bib.bib41)\] backend. For the baseline, we use the WPD
model described in \[[14](#bib.bib14)\]. It uses a bidirectional
long-short term memory (BLSTM) network with 600 cells in each direction
followed by an output layer producing three masks per target speaker,
i.e. 6 in our case. WPE is configured with $`L=5`$ taps and delay
$`D=3`$, and runs for single iteration. The neural source model for
T-ISS is the same as in \[[19](#bib.bib19)\]. It has three convolutional
layers, with batch-norm, max pooling, and GLU activations. It has a 256
hidden dimension and dropout set to $`0.2`$. For the models trained on
clean data, we preprocessed the input with 5 iterations of AuxIVA-ISS
with a non-trainable source model \[[42](#bib.bib42)\]. This is followed
by 10 iterations of T-ISS with the neural source model. After
separation, the scale and phase are aligned to a reference channel by
projection back \[[43](#bib.bib43)\]. As it did not seem very effective,
we did not apply this preprocessing when training on the noisy dataset.
Instead we ran 15 iterations of T-ISS straight. We used demixing matrix
checkpointing \[[25](#bib.bib25)\] to allow the model to fit on GPU
during training.

We used the Adam optimizer with warmup set to 25000 and 50000 steps on
the clean and noisy datasets, respectively, and initial learning rate of
$`1`$. The WPD baseline was always trained with maximum $`M=8`$
channels. We trained multiple T-ISS models on the clean dataset using
$`M\in\{2,4,8\}`$ channels. On the noisy dataset, we only trained on
$`M=4`$ channels due to time constraints. At test time, the number of
T-ISS iterations was adjusted to achieve better performance. An external
word-level recurrent neural network language model (RNNLM)
\[[44](#bib.bib44)\] is applied as shallow fusion in the decoding stage.

separation stage for two channels. Similarly, T-ISS models trained on

training and testing. We thus further tested on the mismatched noisy
mixture dataset (labeled TUT in the table). For WPD, the noisy training
was effective at improving the robustness, and the WER did not increase
as much as before. The T-ISS model trained on noisy data performed much
worse on the mismatched noise. We conjecture that this may be due to the
lack of a proper noise model, and overfitting to the noise in the source
model. It was the T-ISS model trained on noiseless data only that

## 5 Conclusions

We have proposed the joint training of a MIMO-speech ASR system with an
independent vector analysis frontend using the T-ISS algorithm. T-ISS is
an iterative procedure performing joint separation and dereverberation
with the help of a neural source model. We demonstrate that E2E training
of this system, through the iterations, yields an ASR robust to data
mismatch. The T-ISS frontend trained on clean data only, did best, or at
least well enough, on all our test sets. In contrast, the neural
beamformer baseline required noisy data in the training set in order to
avoid a large performance drop.

Future work should concentrate on the inclusion of a noise model in
T-ISS, e.g. \[[45](#bib.bib45)\]. Multi-condition training and
curriculum learning are also promising research avenues.
````
