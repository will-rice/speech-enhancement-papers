###### Abstract

Dynamical variational autoencoders (DVAEs) are a class of deep
generative models with latent variables, dedicated to model time series
of high-dimensional data. DVAEs can be considered as extensions of the
variational autoencoder (VAE) that include temporal dependencies between
successive observed and/or latent vectors. Previous work has shown the
interest of using DVAEs over the VAE for speech spectrograms modeling.
Independently, the VAE has been successfully applied to speech
enhancement in noise, in an unsupervised noise-agnostic set-up that
requires neither noise samples nor noisy speech samples at training
time, but only requires clean speech signals. In this paper, we extend
these works to DVAE-based single-channel unsupervised speech
enhancement, hence exploiting both speech signals unsupervised
representation learning and dynamics modeling. We propose an
unsupervised speech enhancement algorithm that combines a DVAE speech
prior pre-trained on clean speech signals with a noise model based on
nonnegative matrix factorization, and we derive a variational
expectation-maximization (VEM) algorithm to perform speech enhancement.
The algorithm is presented with the most general DVAE formulation and is
then applied with three specific DVAE models to illustrate the
versatility of the framework. Experimental results show that the
proposed DVAE-based approach outperforms its VAE-based counterpart, as
well as several supervised and unsupervised noise-dependent baselines,
especially when the noise type is unseen during training.

###### Index Terms: 

Speech enhancement, dynamical variational autoencoders, nonnegative
matrix factorization, variational inference.

## I Introduction

Speech enhancement is a classical and fundamental problem in speech
processing \[[1](#bib.bib1), [2](#bib.bib2)\], which aims to recover the
clean speech signal from a noisy recording. Classical
signal-processing-based solutions include spectral subtraction
\[[3](#bib.bib3)\] and Wiener filtering \[[4](#bib.bib4)\] (which use
noise and clean speech power spectral density estimates obtained from
the noisy signal), and the short-time spectral amplitude estimator
\[[5](#bib.bib5)\].

Recently, the advances in deep learning (DL) have opened new
possibilities to tackle this task. The most widely studied approach to
DL-based speech enhancement is that of a regression problem in the
time-frequency (TF) domain where a deep neural network (DNN) is trained
to map an input noisy speech signal into an output clean speech signal
or into a denoising TF mask that is applied on the noisy signal (see a
review in \[[6](#bib.bib6)\]). We can refer to this general approach as
a noisy-to-clean mapping (N2C). Recent works have considered N2C
directly in the time domain instead of the TF domain \[[7](#bib.bib7)\]
or leveraging generative adversarial networks (GANs) \[[8](#bib.bib8),
[9](#bib.bib9), [10](#bib.bib10)\].

In the N2C approach, model training is typically done in a supervised
manner using a parallel noisy-clean dataset, i.e., with noisy and clean
versions of the same speech signal. Such parallel dataset must be
prepared beforehand, the noisy version being obtained by summing the
clean speech signal with noise. With a large amount of training data,
DNNs can efficiently learn the N2C denoising mapping \[[11](#bib.bib11),
[6](#bib.bib6)\]. However, supervised methods, whether they work in the
TF domain or in the time domain, tend to have difficulties for
generalizing to noise types and acoustic conditions that were not seen
during training. And it is difficult, not to say impossible, to generate
a dataset that includes all possible types and levels of noise (e.g.,
urban noise vs. office noise) and all possible acoustic conditions
(e.g., different recording equipments, varying mouth-to-microphone
distance and orientation, different reverberation characteristics,
etc.).

Recent works in DL-based speech enhancement have tried to relax the
constraints regarding the degree of supervision to ease the design of
datasets and/or improve the generalization capability of the models. In
the present speech enhancement context, relaxing the degree of
supervision means that we go from methods using carefully aligned
parallel noisy-clean data to methods using _non-parallel_ noisy-clean
data (i.e., the noisy examples are not the noisy version of the clean
examples), which are easier to prepare, or noisy-only data, which are
both easy to record and prepare, or clean-only data, which are not so
easy to record, but which can lead to good generalization capabilities,
as seen below. For example, the GANs employed in \[[12](#bib.bib12)\]
and \[[13](#bib.bib13)\] use non-parallel noisy and clean speech
examples. In the present context, such methods that do not require a
parallel noisy-clean dataset, are referred to as _unsupervised_. They
can be divided into two groups.

_Unsupervised noise-dependent_ methods use noise examples or noisy
speech examples only (they do not use clean speech signals). For
example, a noisy-to-noisy (N2N) mapping approach, originally proposed
for image denoising in \[[14](#bib.bib14)\], was applied to speech
enhancement in \[[15](#bib.bib15), [16](#bib.bib16)\]. In this approach,
the DNN input is still a noisy signal but the output clean signal is
replaced with another noisy version of the same clean signal. This is
supported by theoretical considerations: If the noises in the noisy
input and output are zero-mean and uncorrelated, and an infinite number
of examples is provided to the DNN, the latter will learn to output an
average denoised version of the signal. The motivation for adopting this
approach for speech enhancement is that clean audio signals are
difficult and expensive to record (in studio condition) compared to
noisy speech signals. However, the required assumptions are not met for
audio signals. Not only the different channels of multichannel
recordings do not contain the exact same clean speech signal, but they
also contain correlated noise \[[17](#bib.bib17), [18](#bib.bib18),
[19](#bib.bib19), [20](#bib.bib20)\]. Moreover, in their experiments,
the authors of \[[15](#bib.bib15), [16](#bib.bib16)\] have to rely on
simulated clean speech plus noise signals, which questions the interest
of the N2N approach compared to the conventional N2C mapping. However,
the N2N approach inspired the more realistic noisy-target training
method (NyTT) \[[21](#bib.bib21)\], in which noisy speech and extra
noise is used. In the training step, the NyTT input is noisy speech
further corrupted by an additional noise and the network is trained to
recover the noisy speech at the output. Then in the test step, the
network is supposed to recover the clean speech signal from a noisy
speech input. Although NyTT lacks theoretical support, it was shown to
obtain good results in practice \[[21](#bib.bib21)\]. In a different
spirit, another approach not using clean speech signals is the
MetricGAN-U method proposed by Fu et al. \[[22](#bib.bib22)\], an
unsupervised version of their previous model MetricGAN \[[9](#bib.bib9),
[10](#bib.bib10)\]. MetricGAN-U relies on the non-intrusive speech
quality metric DNSMOS \[[23](#bib.bib23)\], which does not require using
the clean speech signal, in contrast to the intrusive PESQ metric
\[[24](#bib.bib24)\] used in MetricGAN. One problem with the
unsupervised noise-dependent methods in general is that they learn the
noise characteristics and acoustic conditions, and thus may generalize
poorly to unseen noise and acoustic conditions, just like supervised N2C
methods.

Alternatively, _unsupervised noise-agnostic_ methods are based on a
(deep) model of clean speech signals and do not learn the noise
characteristics during training. Instead, the latter are estimated at
test time on each speech sequence to denoise, hence conceptually letting
the speech enhancement method the potential to adapt to any kind of
noise. This setting was originally referred to as semi-supervised in the
audio source separation literature \[[25](#bib.bib25), [26](#bib.bib26),
[27](#bib.bib27)\], because it exploits a dataset of isolated signals
for one of the sources in the mixture. This dataset is thus labeled with
the class of the sound source, e.g., clean speech for speech
enhancement. In the present paper, we choose to call this setting
unsupervised because in the machine learning literature, semi-supervised
refers to methods that are trained from both labeled and unlabeled
datasets (e.g., \[[28](#bib.bib28)\]). In this context, a
semi-supervised speech enhancement method would be trained from both a
labeled dataset of noisy and clean speech signal pairs, and an unlabeled
dataset containing only noisy or clean speech. While very interesting,
this setting is not considered in this paper.

Examples of unsupervised noise-agnostic methods include that of Bando et
al. \[[29](#bib.bib29)\], who proposed to use a variational autoencoder
(VAE) \[[30](#bib.bib30), [31](#bib.bib31)\] to learn a prior
distribution of the clean speech signals. At test time, the noise signal
is modeled with Bayesian nonnegative matrix factorization (NMF)
\[[32](#bib.bib32)\] whose parameters, as well as the VAE latent
variables, are estimated with a Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC)
algorithm, including a sampling of the NMF parameters and the VAE latent
variables. The same general approach was considered and extended in
\[[33](#bib.bib33)\] within an expectation-maximization optimization
framework, in \[[34](#bib.bib34)\] with an alpha-stable noise model, in
\[[35](#bib.bib35)\] with efficient inference and learning algorithms,
and in \[[36](#bib.bib36), [37](#bib.bib37), [38](#bib.bib38)\] for a
multi-channel configuration. More recently, a guided VAE was proposed in
\[[39](#bib.bib39)\], where the clean speech prior is defined
conditionally on a voice activity detection or an ideal binary mask.
This guiding information is provided by a supervised classifier,
separately trained on noisy speech signals. Other supervised extensions
of the speech enhancement framework combining a VAE clean speech model
and an NMF noise model include \[[40](#bib.bib40)\] and
\[[41](#bib.bib41)\].

Most of the above VAE-based unsupervised noise-agnostic speech
enhancement methods focused on exploiting different distributions and
algorithms. Very few works dealt with the inherent limitation of the VAE
to handle sequential data correlated in time, as is the case of speech
data. To the best of our knowledge, only two papers proposed generative
approaches to speech enhancement based on VAE variants that can learn
temporal dependencies: A recurrent VAE (RVAE) based on recurrent neural
networks (RNNs) was proposed in \[[42](#bib.bib42)\] and stochastic
temporal convolutional networks (TCNs) \[[43](#bib.bib43),
[44](#bib.bib44)\] were used in \[[45](#bib.bib45)\], allowing the
latent variables to have both hierarchical and temporal dependencies.
Yet, a series of works have focused on developing extensions of the
original VAE for time series (completely independently of the speech
enhancement problem). The deep Kalman filter (DKF) \[[46](#bib.bib46),
[47](#bib.bib47)\] is a DNN-based state-space model that combines a VAE
with a non-linear first-order Markov model on the latent vectors. The
variational RNN (VRNN) \[[48](#bib.bib48)\] and the stochastic RNN
(SRNN) \[[49](#bib.bib49)\] are other temporal extensions of the VAE,
with more complex temporal dependencies between the observed and latent
data sequences, implemented with DNNs and RNNs. Actually, RVAE, DKF,
VRNN, SRNN, and several other models \[[50](#bib.bib50),
[51](#bib.bib51), [52](#bib.bib52), [53](#bib.bib53)\] can all be seen
as particular instances of a general class of models called dynamical
variational autoencoders (DVAEs), which have been recently reviewed in
\[[54](#bib.bib54)\]. In the present paper, we propose an unsupervised
noise-agnostic speech enhancement algorithm based on the modeling of the
clean speech signal with a DVAE and the use of the variational inference
methodology \[[55](#bib.bib55), [56](#bib.bib56), [57](#bib.bib57)\]. We
present this algorithm in the general context of the DVAE class of
models, and then we apply it on three specific DVAE models in our
experiments: RVAE (extending our preliminary work in
\[[42](#bib.bib42)\]), DKF, and SRNN. To our knowledge, the present
paper is the first in-depth study on the use of DVAEs, as a general
class of models, for unsupervised speech enhancement.

## II DVAE and Speech Modeling

In this section, we first review the standard VAE and its extensions to
temporal models, which are referred to as DVAEs. Then, we briefly
introduce speech modeling using DVAE models. In the end of this section,
we describe the practical implementation of three typical DVAE models.

### II-A VAEs and DVAEs

In a VAE \[[30](#bib.bib30), [31](#bib.bib31)\], an observed variable
$`\mathbf{s}`$ of high dimension $`F`$ is assumed to be generated from
an unobserved, or latent, random variable $`\mathbf{z}`$ of low
dimension $`L\ll F`$. Let
$`p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{s}\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{z})=p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{s}}}(\mathbf{s}|\mathbf{z})p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{z}}}(\mathbf{z})`$
be the parametric generative model of their joint distribution, where
$`\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{s}}\cup\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{z}}`$
denotes the set of parameters. In general, the latent vector
$`\mathbf{z}`$ is assumed to be generated from a very simple prior
distribution, typically the multivariate standard Gaussian distribution
$`p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{z}}}(\mathbf{z})=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{z};\mathbf{0}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathbf{I})`$
(in that case, $`\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{z}}=\emptyset`$). The
parameters of
$`p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{s}}}(\mathbf{s}|\mathbf{z})`$ are
provided by a complex nonlinear function of $`\mathbf{z}`$, implemented
with a deep neural network (DNN) (and
$`\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{s}}`$ is the set of parameters of this
DNN).

Given a dataset $`\mathbf{S}=\{\mathbf{s}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}`$ of $`N`$
i.i.d. samples of $`\mathbf{s}`$, a probabilistic model is traditionally
optimized by maximizing the log-marginal likelihood (also called
evidence),
$`\log p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{S})=\textstyle\sum_{n=1}^{N}\log p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{s}_{n})`$,
over the parameter set $`\boldsymbol{\theta}`$. In the VAE case, the
complexity of
$`p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{s}}}(\mathbf{s}|\mathbf{z})`$ makes
the marginalization over the latent variable, and thus the computation
of $`\log p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{S})`$, intractable, and the
same for the posterior distribution
$`p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{s})`$. Therefore, instead
of directly maximizing $`\log p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{S})`$, an
inference model
$`q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{s})\approx p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{s})`$
is introduced, which is also defined by a DNN (of parameters
$`\boldsymbol{\phi}`$). Then, the following evidence lower bound (ELBO)
is computed:

|     |     |                                                                                                                                                                        |                                                                                                                                                       |                         |
| --- | --- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------- | -------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------- | --- | --- |
|     |     | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\boldsymbol{\phi};\mathbf{S})=\sum*{n=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}*{q*{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\mathbf{z}*{n} | \mathbf{s}_{n})}\big[\log p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{s}_{n}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathbf{z}_{n})-\log q*{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\mathbf{z}*{n} | \mathbf{s}\_{n})\big]`$ |                                        |                                           |
|     |     | $`\displaystyle\qquad\quad=\log p*{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{S})-\sum*{n=1}^{N}D*{\text{KL}}\big(q*{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\mathbf{z}\_{n}                              | \mathbf{s}\_{n})                                                                                                                                      |                         | p*{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{z}*{n} | \mathbf{s}\_{n})\big)\mathchar 59\relax`$ |     | (1) |

where $`D_{\text{KL}}(.)`$ denotes the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence,
which is always non-negative. The generative model
$`p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{s}}}(\mathbf{s}|\mathbf{z})`$ and the
inference model $`q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{s})`$ are
jointly trained by maximizing the ELBO with respect to
$`\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{s}}`$ and $`\boldsymbol{\phi}`$, using
stochastic gradient descent combined with sampling \[[30](#bib.bib30),
[31](#bib.bib31)\].

While the vanilla VAE assumes statistical independence among observation
vectors, DVAEs can be seen as an extension of the VAE for modeling
sequential data correlated in time \[[54](#bib.bib54)\]. A DVAE keeps
the global encoder-decoder philosophy of the VAE, but considers a
sequence of (high-dimensional) observed random vectors
$`\mathbf{s}_{1:T}=\{\mathbf{s}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}`$ and a corresponding
sequence of (low-dimensional) latent vectors
$`\mathbf{z}_{1:T}=\{\mathbf{z}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}`$. A DVAE is thus
defined by the joint probability density function (pdf) of observed and
latent sequences
$`p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{s}_{1:T}\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{z}_{1:T})`$,
which can be factorized using the chain rule:¹¹ 1 Here and in all the
following, we take the convention that
$`\mathbf{s}_{1:0}=\mathbf{z}_{1:0}=\emptyset`$. For $`t=1`$ the first
term of the product in
([2](#S2.E2 "In II-A VAEs and DVAEs ‣ II DVAE and Speech Modeling ‣ Unsupervised Speech Enhancement using Dynamical Variational Autoencoders"))
and
([3](#S2.E3 "In II-A VAEs and DVAEs ‣ II DVAE and Speech Modeling ‣ Unsupervised Speech Enhancement using Dynamical Variational Autoencoders"))
is thus
$`p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{s}_{1}\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{z}_{1})`$
and
$`p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{s}_{1}|\mathbf{z}_{1})p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{z}_{1})`$,
respectively.

|     |                                                                                               |                                                                                                       |                                                                                                                     |                                                                      |
| --- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{s}_{1:T}\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{z}_{1:T})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\prod*{t=1}^{T}p*{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{s}_{t}\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{z}_{t} | \mathbf{s}_{1:t-1}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathbf{z}_{1:t-1})`$                                                 |                                                                      | (2) |

As for the VAE, the exact posterior distribution
$`p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{z}_{1:T}|\mathbf{x}_{1:T})`$ is not
analytically tractable. Consequently, an approximate posterior
distribution
$`q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\mathbf{z}_{1:T}|\mathbf{x}_{1:T})`$ is
introduced and it can be factorized using the chain rule as:

|     |                                                        |                      |                                                                      |                                                                    |
| --- | ------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle q*{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\mathbf{z}*{1:T} | \mathbf{s}\_{1:T})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\prod*{t=1}^{T}q*{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\mathbf{z}\_{t} | \mathbf{z}_{1:t-1}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathbf{s}_{1:T}).`$ |     | (4) |

````math
When writing a joint distribution as a product of conditional
distributions using the chain rule, a specific ordering of the variables
has to be chosen. Among different possibilities, we chose a *causal*
ordering to write the factorization in
([2](#S2.E2 "In II-A VAEs and DVAEs ‣ II DVAE and Speech Modeling ‣ Unsupervised Speech Enhancement using Dynamical Variational Autoencoders"))
and

The generation of $`\mathbf{s}_{t}`$ and $`\mathbf{z}_{t}`$ uses their
past values $`\mathbf{s}_{1:t-1}`$ and $`\mathbf{z}_{1:t-1}`$ (plus
$`\mathbf{z}_{t}`$ for generating $`\mathbf{x}_{t}`$). In the DVAE
literature, almost all models are causal \[[54](#bib.bib54)\]. Each of
them can be seen as a special case of the general expression
([3](#S2.E3 "In II-A VAEs and DVAEs ‣ II DVAE and Speech Modeling ‣ Unsupervised Speech Enhancement using Dynamical Variational Autoencoders"))
where the dependencies in

various types of DNNs, see \[[54](#bib.bib54)\] for an extensive
discussion on this topic.

### II-B Speech modeling using DVAEs

![Refer to caption](2106.12271v3/AnalysisResynthesis.png)

Fig. 1: Speech analysis-resynthesis with a DVAE model in the STFT
domain. The speech power spectrogram is used as input to the DVAE and
the output is the variance of a complex Gaussian model. The audio
waveform is reconstructed by inverse STFT using the phase of the
original signal.

The VAE and the DVAE class of models have been used to model different
kinds of data. In this subsection, we discuss the use of DVAEs for
modeling speech signals in the short-term Fourier transform (STFT)
domain.
Fig. [1](#S2.F1 "Fig. 1 ‣ II-B Speech modeling using DVAEs ‣ II DVAE and Speech Modeling ‣ Unsupervised Speech Enhancement using Dynamical Variational Autoencoders")
illustrates this process. Let
$`\mathbf{s}_{1:T}=\{\mathbf{s}_{t}\in\mathbb{C}^{F}\}_{t=1}^{T}`$
denote an $`F\times T`$ sequence of complex-valued STFT frames, where
$`t`$ is the time-frame index. Each vector
$`\mathbf{s}_{t}=\{s_{ft}\in\mathbb{C}\}_{f=1}^{F}`$ represents the
speech short-term spectrum at time index $`t`$, and $`f`$ is the
frequency bin. As indicated above, $`\mathbf{s}_{1:T}`$ is associated
with an $`L\times T`$ sequence of latent variables
$`\mathbf{z}_{1:T}=\{\mathbf{z}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{L}\}_{t=1}^{T}`$, with
$`L\ll F`$.

In speech and audio processing, the Fourier coefficients in
$`\mathbf{s}_{t}\in\mathbb{C}^{F}`$ are usually assumed to be
independent and distributed according to a complex Gaussian circularly
symmetric distribution \[[58](#bib.bib58)\] (denoted below by
$`\mathcal{N}_{c}`$), whose variance vary over time and frequency
\[[5](#bib.bib5), [59](#bib.bib59)\] (the circularly symmetric
assumption means that the phase follows a uniform distribution in
$`[0\mathchar 59\relax 2\pi)`$). Thus, for all time frames
$`t\in\{1\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\dots\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T\}`$,
the DVAE generative model of speech signal is defined as follows:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  |  | $`\displaystyle p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{s}}}(\mathbf{s}_{t}|\mathbf{s}_{1:t-1}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathbf{z}_{1:t})=\mathcal{N}_{c}\left(\mathbf{s}_{t};\mathbf{0}\mathchar 59\relax\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{s}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t}\right)\mathchar 59\relax`$ |  | (6) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{z}}}(\mathbf{z}_{t}|\mathbf{s}_{1:t-1}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathbf{z}_{1:t-1})=\mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{z}_{t};\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{z}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t}\mathchar 59\relax\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{z}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t}\right)\mathchar 59\relax`$ |  | (7) |

where the diagonal covariance matrix
$`\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{s}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t}=\diag\{\boldsymbol{v}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{s}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t}\}`$
is provided by a DNN that takes as input the conditioning variables in
([6](#S2.E6 "In II-B Speech modeling using DVAEs ‣ II DVAE and Speech Modeling ‣ Unsupervised Speech Enhancement using Dynamical Variational Autoencoders")),
namely $`(\mathbf{s}_{1:t-1}\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{z}_{1:t})`$.
Similarly,
$`\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{z}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t}`$
and
$`\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{z}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t}=\diag\{\boldsymbol{v}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{z}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t}\}`$
are provided by a DNN that takes as input the conditioning variables in
([7](#S2.E7 "In II-B Speech modeling using DVAEs ‣ II DVAE and Speech Modeling ‣ Unsupervised Speech Enhancement using Dynamical Variational Autoencoders")),
namely $`(\mathbf{s}_{1:t-1}\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{z}_{1:t-1})`$.²² 2
It is important to note that, in DVAEs, a parameter of a distribution is
always a function of the variables that come after the conditioning bar.
In the rest of the paper, we will generally omit to rewrite these
variables in the right-hand-side of the probabilistic modeling
equations, for concision, but we may punctually make these dependencies
explicit when it eases the understanding. We denote by
$`\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{s}}\cup\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{z}}`$
the parameters of the DNNs involved in
([6](#S2.E6 "In II-B Speech modeling using DVAEs ‣ II DVAE and Speech Modeling ‣ Unsupervised Speech Enhancement using Dynamical Variational Autoencoders"))
and
([7](#S2.E7 "In II-B Speech modeling using DVAEs ‣ II DVAE and Speech Modeling ‣ Unsupervised Speech Enhancement using Dynamical Variational Autoencoders")).

As for the inference model, it is given by:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\mathbf{z}_{t}|\mathbf{z}_{1:t-1}\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{s}_{1:T})=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{z}_{t};\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t}\mathchar 59\relax\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t})\mathchar 59\relax
``` |  | (8) |

where
$`\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t}`$
and
$`\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t}=\diag\{\boldsymbol{v}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t}\}`$
are provided by a DNN taking $`(\mathbf{z}_{1:t-1}`$,
$`\mathbf{s}_{1:T})`$ as input and whose parameters are denoted by
$`\boldsymbol{\phi}`$.

Even if the complex-valued vector sequence $`\mathbf{s}_{1:t-1}`$ or
$`\mathbf{s}_{1:T}`$ is used as a conditioning variable in
([6](#S2.E6 "In II-B Speech modeling using DVAEs ‣ II DVAE and Speech Modeling ‣ Unsupervised Speech Enhancement using Dynamical Variational Autoencoders"))-([8](#S2.E8 "In II-B Speech modeling using DVAEs ‣ II DVAE and Speech Modeling ‣ Unsupervised Speech Enhancement using Dynamical Variational Autoencoders")),
in practice we use the modulus-squared values of these variables at the
encoder and decoder input. In other words, the DVAE encoder and decoder
distribution parameters are computed using sequences of vectors with
entries equal to $`|s_{ft}|^{2}`$, as illustrated in
Fig. [1](#S2.F1 "Fig. 1 ‣ II-B Speech modeling using DVAEs ‣ II DVAE and Speech Modeling ‣ Unsupervised Speech Enhancement using Dynamical Variational Autoencoders").
Note that the modulus-squared of data is homogeneous with the decoder
output (the variance vector
$`\boldsymbol{v}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{s}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t}`$).

Given the generative model
([6](#S2.E6 "In II-B Speech modeling using DVAEs ‣ II DVAE and Speech Modeling ‣ Unsupervised Speech Enhancement using Dynamical Variational Autoencoders")),
([7](#S2.E7 "In II-B Speech modeling using DVAEs ‣ II DVAE and Speech Modeling ‣ Unsupervised Speech Enhancement using Dynamical Variational Autoencoders"))
and the inference model
([8](#S2.E8 "In II-B Speech modeling using DVAEs ‣ II DVAE and Speech Modeling ‣ Unsupervised Speech Enhancement using Dynamical Variational Autoencoders")),
we can develop the ELBO in
([5](#S2.E5 "In II-A VAEs and DVAEs ‣ II DVAE and Speech Modeling ‣ Unsupervised Speech Enhancement using Dynamical Variational Autoencoders"))
as follows (for one data sequence):

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\boldsymbol{\phi};\mathbf{s}_{1:T})\overset{c}{=}-\sum_{f\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t=1}^{F\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T}\mathbb{E}_{q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\mathbf{z}_{1:t}|\mathbf{s}_{1:T})}\left[d_{\text{IS}}(|s_{ft}|^{2}\mathchar 59\relax v_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{s}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}ft})\right]`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{l\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t=1}^{L\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T}\Bigg[\ln\frac{v_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}lt}}{v_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{z}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}lt}}-\frac{v_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}lt}+({\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}lt}-{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{z}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}lt})^{2}}{v_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{z}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}lt}}\Bigg]\mathchar 59\relax`$ |  | (9) |

where $`\overset{c}{=}`$ denotes equality up to an additive constant
w.r.t. $`\boldsymbol{\theta}`$ and $`\boldsymbol{\phi}`$,
$`d_{\text{IS}}(q\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}p)=q/p-\ln(q/p)-1`$ is the
Itakura-Saito (IS) divergence \[[32](#bib.bib32)\],
$`v_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{s}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}ft}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}`$
is the $`f`$-th entry of
$`\boldsymbol{v}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{s}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t}`$,
and
$`\{{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}lt}\in\mathbb{R}\mathchar 59\relax{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{z}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}lt}\in\mathbb{R}\mathchar 59\relax v_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}lt}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\mathchar 59\relax v_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{z}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}lt}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\}`$
are the $`l`$-th entry of
$`\{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t}\mathchar 59\relax\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{z}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t}\mathchar 59\relax\boldsymbol{v}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t}\mathchar 59\relax\boldsymbol{v}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{z}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t}\}`$,
respectively.

### II-C Three representative DVAE models

In this subsection, we briefly present three DVAE models, namely DKF,
RVAE, and SRNN, which are representative of the versatility of the DVAE
class (see the review in \[[54](#bib.bib54)\]) and which we used in
practice in our speech enhancement method, with results reported in
Section [IV](#S4 "IV Experiments ‣ Unsupervised Speech Enhancement using Dynamical Variational Autoencoders").

#### II-C1 DKF

As briefly stated in the introduction, DKF \[[46](#bib.bib46),
[47](#bib.bib47)\] is the simplest DVAE model, following the structure
of a basic state-space model with a first-order Markov model on the
latent vectors, i.e., $`\mathbf{z}_{t}`$ is generated from
$`\mathbf{z}_{t-1}`$, and an instantaneous observation model, i.e.,
$`\mathbf{s}_{t}`$ is generated from $`\mathbf{z}_{t}`$:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{s}_{1:T}\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{z}_{1:T})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\prod_{t=1}^{T}p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{s}}}(\mathbf{s}_{t}|\mathbf{z}_{t})p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{z}}}(\mathbf{z}_{t}|\mathbf{z}_{t-1}).`$ |  | (10) |

The inference model is defined by:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\mathbf{z}_{1:T}|\mathbf{s}_{1:T})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\prod_{t=1}^{T}q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\mathbf{z}_{t}|\mathbf{z}_{t-1}\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{s}_{t:T}).`$ |  | (11) |

#### II-C2 RVAE

As opposed to DKF, RVAE \[[42](#bib.bib42)\] does not consider any
dynamical model for the latent vectors, which are assumed i.i.d., with
$`p(\mathbf{z}_{t})=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{z}_{t};\mathbf{0}\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{I)}`$.
This prior therefore lacks parameters and does not involve a neural
network, as in standard VAEs. However, the observation model in RVAE is
more complex than in DKF, as $`\mathbf{s}_{t}`$ is generated from the
current and previous latent variables $`\mathbf{z}_{1:t}`$. Formally,
the RVAE generative and inference models are defined by:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{s}_{1:T}\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{z}_{1:T})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\prod_{t=1}^{T}p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{s}}}(\mathbf{s}_{t}|\mathbf{z}_{1:t})p(\mathbf{z}_{t})\mathchar 59\relax`$ |  | (12) |
|  | $`\displaystyle q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\mathbf{z}_{1:T}|\mathbf{s}_{1:T})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\prod_{t=1}^{T}q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\mathbf{z}_{t}|\mathbf{z}_{1:t-1}\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{s}_{t:T}).`$ |  | (13) |

RVAE is the only model that was presented in the literature with both a
causal and non-causal form \[[42](#bib.bib42), [54](#bib.bib54)\]. The
non-causal form is obtained by simply replacing $`\mathbf{z}_{1:t}`$
with $`\mathbf{z}_{1:T}`$ in
([12](#S2.E12 "In II-C2 RVAE ‣ II-C Three representative DVAE models ‣ II DVAE and Speech Modeling ‣ Unsupervised Speech Enhancement using Dynamical Variational Autoencoders"))
and by replacing $`\mathbf{s}_{t:T}`$ with $`\mathbf{s}_{1:T}`$ in
([13](#S2.E13 "In II-C2 RVAE ‣ II-C Three representative DVAE models ‣ II DVAE and Speech Modeling ‣ Unsupervised Speech Enhancement using Dynamical Variational Autoencoders")).

#### II-C3 SRNN

In SRNN \[[49](#bib.bib49)\], the latent vector $`\mathbf{z}_{t}`$ is
generated not only from $`\mathbf{z}_{t-1}`$ (as in DKF), but also from
$`\mathbf{s}_{1:t-1}`$. And $`\mathbf{s}_{t}`$ is generated not only
from $`\mathbf{z}_{t}`$ (as in DKF) but also from
$`\mathbf{s}_{1:t-1}`$, so that SRNN actually corresponds to an
autoregressive model. The generative and inference models are defined
by:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{s}_{1:T}\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{z}_{1:T})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\prod_{t=1}^{T}p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{s}}}(\mathbf{s}_{t}|\mathbf{z}_{t}\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{s}_{1:t-1})p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{z}}}(\mathbf{z}_{t}|\mathbf{z}_{t-1}\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{s}_{1:t-1})\mathchar 59\relax`$ |  | (14) |
|  | $`\displaystyle q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\mathbf{z}_{1:T}|\mathbf{s}_{1:T})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\prod_{t=1}^{T}q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\mathbf{z}_{t}|\mathbf{z}_{t-1}\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{s}_{1:T}).`$ |  | (15) |

## III DVAE for Speech Enhancement

This section presents the proposed unsupervised noise-agnostic
DVAE-based speech enhancement algorithm, where the clean speech signal
is modeled with a DVAE and the noise is modeled with nonnegative matrix
factorization (NMF) \[[32](#bib.bib32)\]. It is an extended version of
the algorithm proposed in \[[42](#bib.bib42)\] for the RVAE model, with
a more general formulation applicable to any other DVAE model. The
proposed method is illustrated in Fig.
[2](#S2.F2 "Fig. 2 ‣ II-B Speech modeling using DVAEs ‣ II DVAE and Speech Modeling ‣ Unsupervised Speech Enhancement using Dynamical Variational Autoencoders").
We assume that the DVAE-based clean speech generative and inference
models defined in
([6](#S2.E6 "In II-B Speech modeling using DVAEs ‣ II DVAE and Speech Modeling ‣ Unsupervised Speech Enhancement using Dynamical Variational Autoencoders"))-([7](#S2.E7 "In II-B Speech modeling using DVAEs ‣ II DVAE and Speech Modeling ‣ Unsupervised Speech Enhancement using Dynamical Variational Autoencoders"))
and
([8](#S2.E8 "In II-B Speech modeling using DVAEs ‣ II DVAE and Speech Modeling ‣ Unsupervised Speech Enhancement using Dynamical Variational Autoencoders")),
respectively, have been learned, i.e., the DNN parameters
$`\boldsymbol{\theta}`$ and $`\boldsymbol{\phi}`$ have been estimated
from a dataset of clean speech signals during an independent training
stage (see
Section [II-B](#S2.SS2 "II-B Speech modeling using DVAEs ‣ II DVAE and Speech Modeling ‣ Unsupervised Speech Enhancement using Dynamical Variational Autoencoders")).
The objective of speech enhancement is to use this pre-trained DVAE
model to estimate the clean speech signal when only the noisy mixture is
observed. This is done with a variational expectation-maximization (VEM)
algorithm \[[55](#bib.bib55), [56](#bib.bib56), [57](#bib.bib57)\]. We
recall that this method is unsupervised, since no pair of clean and
noisy speech examples are used. Moreover it is noise-agnostic since it
does not make any assumption on the noise type, except that it can be
modeled with an NMF model, and the noise model NMF parameters are
estimated independently for each noisy sequence to process.

In the rest of this section, we first introduce the noise and mixture
models, then we develop the general strategy to estimate the clean
speech signal modeled by a DVAE when only the mixture signal is
available, and finally we present the VEM algorithm used to estimate the
remaining unknown model parameters. Throughout this section,
$`\mathbf{s}_{1:T}=\{\mathbf{s}_{t}\in\mathbb{C}^{F}\}_{t=1}^{T}`$,
$`\mathbf{b}_{1:T}=\{\mathbf{b}_{t}\in\mathbb{C}^{F}\}_{t=1}^{T}`$, and
$`\mathbf{x}_{1:T}=\{\mathbf{x}_{t}\in\mathbb{C}^{F}\}_{t=1}^{T}`$
respectively denote the STFT of the clean speech signal, the noise
signal, and the noisy speech signal.

### III-A Noise and mixture models

As in \[[33](#bib.bib33), [42](#bib.bib42)\], we consider a Gaussian
noise model with NMF parameterization of the variance
\[[32](#bib.bib32)\]. Independently for all time frames
$`t\in\{1\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}...\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T\}`$,
we define:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
p(\mathbf{b}_{t})=\mathcal{N}_{c}(\mathbf{b}_{t};\mathbf{0}\mathchar 59\relax\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{b}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t})\mathchar 59\relax
``` |  | (16) |

where
$`\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{b}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t}=\diag\{(\mathbf{W}_{b}\mathbf{H}_{b})_{:\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t}\}`$
with $`\mathbf{W}_{b}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{F\times K}`$ and
$`\mathbf{H}_{b}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{K\times T}`$. The rank of the
factorization $`K`$ is usually chosen such that $`K(F+T)\ll FT`$.

We consider that the noisy speech is a mixture of the noise defined in
([16](#S3.E16 "In III-A Noise and mixture models ‣ III DVAE for Speech Enhancement ‣ Unsupervised Speech Enhancement using Dynamical Variational Autoencoders"))
and the clean speech defined in
([6](#S2.E6 "In II-B Speech modeling using DVAEs ‣ II DVAE and Speech Modeling ‣ Unsupervised Speech Enhancement using Dynamical Variational Autoencoders"))
and
([7](#S2.E7 "In II-B Speech modeling using DVAEs ‣ II DVAE and Speech Modeling ‣ Unsupervised Speech Enhancement using Dynamical Variational Autoencoders")):

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbf{x}_{t}=\sqrt{g_{t}}\mathbf{s}_{t}+\mathbf{b}_{t}\mathchar 59\relax
``` |  | (17) |

where $`g_{t}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}`$ is a frame-dependent
frequency-independent gain parameter scaling the speech signal level at
each time frame. This parameter enables to take into account the
potentially different loudness between the clean speech training
examples used to learn the DVAE model and the speech signal in the test
noisy sequence we have to denoise \[[33](#bib.bib33)\].

From
([6](#S2.E6 "In II-B Speech modeling using DVAEs ‣ II DVAE and Speech Modeling ‣ Unsupervised Speech Enhancement using Dynamical Variational Autoencoders")),
([16](#S3.E16 "In III-A Noise and mixture models ‣ III DVAE for Speech Enhancement ‣ Unsupervised Speech Enhancement using Dynamical Variational Autoencoders"))
and
([17](#S3.E17 "In III-A Noise and mixture models ‣ III DVAE for Speech Enhancement ‣ Unsupervised Speech Enhancement using Dynamical Variational Autoencoders")),
and by assuming the independence of the speech and noise signals, we
have for all
$`t\in\{1\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}...\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T\}`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{x}}}(\mathbf{x}_{t}\mid\mathbf{s}_{1:t-1}\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{z}_{1:t})=\mathcal{N}_{c}\left(\mathbf{x}_{t};\mathbf{0}\mathchar 59\relax\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{x}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t}\right)\mathchar 59\relax
``` |  | (18) |

where
$`\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{x}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t}=\diag\{g_{t}\boldsymbol{v}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{s}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t}+(\mathbf{W}_{b}\mathbf{H}_{b})_{:\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t}\}`$
and $`\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{x}}`$ is the union of the speech
generative model parameters $`\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{s}}`$ and the
mixture model parameters
$`\boldsymbol{\varphi}=\{\boldsymbol{g}=[g_{1}\mathchar 59\relax\dots\mathchar 59\relax g_{T}]^{T}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathbf{W}_{b}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathbf{H}_{b}\}`$.
As already mentioned in
Section [II-B](#S2.SS2 "II-B Speech modeling using DVAEs ‣ II DVAE and Speech Modeling ‣ Unsupervised Speech Enhancement using Dynamical Variational Autoencoders"),
$`\boldsymbol{v}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{s}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t}`$
is actually a function of
$`(\mathbf{s}_{1:t-1}\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{z}_{1:t})`$. Note that it
is clear from
([16](#S3.E16 "In III-A Noise and mixture models ‣ III DVAE for Speech Enhancement ‣ Unsupervised Speech Enhancement using Dynamical Variational Autoencoders"))
and
([17](#S3.E17 "In III-A Noise and mixture models ‣ III DVAE for Speech Enhancement ‣ Unsupervised Speech Enhancement using Dynamical Variational Autoencoders"))
that given the clean speech frame $`\mathbf{s}_{t}`$, the noisy speech
frame $`\mathbf{x}_{t}`$ is characterized by:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
p_{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}(\mathbf{x}_{t}\mid\mathbf{s}_{t})=\mathcal{N}_{c}\left(\mathbf{x}_{t};\sqrt{g_{t}}\mathbf{s}_{t}\mathchar 59\relax\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{b}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t}\right).
``` |  | (19) |

### III-B Speech reconstruction

Now we consider the problem of reconstructing the clean speech signal
from the observed mixture signal, which consists in computing the
following posterior mean vector:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\hat{\mathbf{s}}_{t}=\mathbb{E}_{p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{s}_{t}|\mathbf{x}_{1:T})}[\mathbf{s}_{t}].
``` |  | (20) |

However, we cannot write the posterior
$`p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{s}_{t}|\mathbf{x}_{1:T})`$
analytically, which makes the above expectation intractable. However,
leveraging the speech model defined previously, we can approximate it by
introducing random variables that are then marginalized.

#### III-B1 Introducing the past and current latent variables

We start from marginalizing with respect to $`\mathbf{z}_{1:t}`$:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{s}_{t}|\mathbf{x}_{1:T})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\int p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{s}_{t}|\mathbf{z}_{1:t}\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{x}_{1:T})p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{z}_{1:t}|\mathbf{x}_{1:T})d\mathbf{z}_{1:t}`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{z}_{1:t}|\mathbf{x}_{1:T})}[p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{s}_{t}|\mathbf{z}_{1:t}\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{x}_{1:T})].`$ |  | (21) |

Using
([21](#S3.E21 "In III-B1 Introducing the past and current latent variables ‣ III-B Speech reconstruction ‣ III DVAE for Speech Enhancement ‣ Unsupervised Speech Enhancement using Dynamical Variational Autoencoders"))
to rewrite
([20](#S3.E20 "In III-B Speech reconstruction ‣ III DVAE for Speech Enhancement ‣ Unsupervised Speech Enhancement using Dynamical Variational Autoencoders")),
the estimate of the clean speech signal at time $`t`$ is given by:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\hat{\mathbf{s}}_{t}=\mathbb{E}_{p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{z}_{1:t}|\mathbf{x}_{1:T})}\left[\mathbb{E}_{p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{s}_{t}|\mathbf{z}_{1:t}\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{x}_{1:T})}[\mathbf{s}_{t}]\right].
``` |  | (22) |

Let us now focus on the inner expectation, taken with respect to
$`p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{s}_{t}|\mathbf{z}_{1:t}\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{x}_{1:T})`$.
We will come back later on the outer expectation taken with respect to
$`p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{z}_{1:t}|\mathbf{x}_{1:T})`$. Using
Bayes rule, we have:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{s}_{t}|\mathbf{z}_{1:t}\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{x}_{1:T})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{1:T}|\mathbf{s}_{t}\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{z}_{1:t})p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{s}_{t}|\mathbf{z}_{1:t})p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{z}_{1:t})}{p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{z}_{1:t}\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{x}_{1:T})}`$ |  | (23) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\propto p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{1:T}|\mathbf{s}_{t}\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{z}_{1:t})p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{s}_{t}|\mathbf{z}_{1:t})`$ |  | (24) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\approx p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{s}_{t})p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{s}_{t}|\mathbf{z}_{1:t}).`$ |  | (25) |

The exact computation of
$`p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{1:T}|\mathbf{s}_{t}\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{z}_{1:t})`$
requires the marginalisation of
$`p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{1:T}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathbf{s}_{1:t-1\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t+1:T}\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{z}_{t+1:T}|\mathbf{s}_{t}\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{z}_{1:t})`$
w.r.t. the undesired variables. This would require not only
marginalising from future latent codes, but also from past and future
clean speech, which is clearly not feasible. Instead, we approximate
([24](#S3.E24 "In III-B1 Introducing the past and current latent variables ‣ III-B Speech reconstruction ‣ III DVAE for Speech Enhancement ‣ Unsupervised Speech Enhancement using Dynamical Variational Autoencoders"))
with
([25](#S3.E25 "In III-B1 Introducing the past and current latent variables ‣ III-B Speech reconstruction ‣ III DVAE for Speech Enhancement ‣ Unsupervised Speech Enhancement using Dynamical Variational Autoencoders"))
by considering only the signal mixture model
$`p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{s}_{t})`$, as defined
in
([19](#S3.E19 "In III-A Noise and mixture models ‣ III DVAE for Speech Enhancement ‣ Unsupervised Speech Enhancement using Dynamical Variational Autoencoders")).

#### III-B2 Introducing the past speech vectors

Then, it comes to estimating
$`p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{s}_{t}|\mathbf{z}_{1:t})`$ in
([25](#S3.E25 "In III-B1 Introducing the past and current latent variables ‣ III-B Speech reconstruction ‣ III DVAE for Speech Enhancement ‣ Unsupervised Speech Enhancement using Dynamical Variational Autoencoders")).
To do so, we introduce and then marginalize the past speech vectors
$`\mathbf{s}_{1:t-1}`$:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}`$ | $`\displaystyle(\mathbf{s}_{t}|\mathbf{z}_{1:t})=\int p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{s}_{t}|\mathbf{s}_{1:t-1}\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{z}_{1:t})p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{s}_{1:t-1}|\mathbf{z}_{1:t})d\mathbf{s}_{1:t-1}`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\int p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{s}_{t}|\mathbf{s}_{1:t-1}\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{z}_{1:t})\left[\prod_{\tau=1}^{t-1}p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{s}_{\tau}|\mathbf{s}_{1:\tau-1}\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{z}_{1:\tau})\right]d\mathbf{s}_{1:t-1}`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\prod_{\tau=1}^{t-1}p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{s}_{\tau}|\mathbf{s}_{1:\tau-1}\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{z}_{1:\tau})}\left[\,p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{s}_{t}|\mathbf{s}_{1:t-1}\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{z}_{1:t})\,\right]\mathchar 59\relax`$ |  | (26) |

where in the second line we used the fact that $`\mathbf{s}_{\tau}`$ is
conditionally independent of $`\mathbf{z}_{\tau+1:t}`$.

When computing
([26](#S3.E26 "In III-B2 Introducing the past speech vectors ‣ III-B Speech reconstruction ‣ III DVAE for Speech Enhancement ‣ Unsupervised Speech Enhancement using Dynamical Variational Autoencoders")),
we are facing two problems: First, the expectation is intractable; and
second, in a speech enhancement framework, we do not have access to the
past ground-truth clean speech vectors $`\mathbf{s}_{1:t-1}`$ (as
opposed to the DVAE training procedure which is done using sequences of
clean speech signals). Therefore, we approximate
$`p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{s}_{t}|\mathbf{z}_{1:t})`$ as follows:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\begin{split}p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{s}_{t}|\mathbf{z}_{1:t})&\approx p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{s}_{t}|\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_{1:t-1}\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{z}_{1:t})\\
&=\mathcal{N}_{c}\big(\mathbf{s}_{t};\mathbf{0}\mathchar 59\relax\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{s}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t}(\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_{1:t-1}\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{z}_{1:t})\big)\mathchar 59\relax\end{split}
``` |  | (27) |

where
$`\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{s}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t}(\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_{1:t-1}\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{z}_{1:t})=\diag\{\boldsymbol{v}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{s}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t}(\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_{1:t-1}\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{z}_{1:t})\}`$
and $`\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_{t}`$ is computed recursively as
$`\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_{t}=\boldsymbol{v}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{s}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t}(\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_{1:t-1}\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{z}_{1:t})`$.³³
3 Here we explicitly write the dependency of the covariance matrix on
$`\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_{1:t-1}`$ and $`\mathbf{z}_{1:t}`$, to make the use
of the DVAE model clear. In the following we will omit it again for
concision of presentation. In practice, the decoder output at time frame
$`t-1`$ is re-injected at the decoder input at the next time frame
$`t`$. This part of the process is necessary only for SRNN, and more
generally for any autoregressive DVAE. For non-autoregressive DVAE
models, such as RVAE and DKF,
$`\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{s}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t}`$
is only computed from the sequence of latent vectors.

#### III-B3 Computing the conditional posterior

Substituting
([19](#S3.E19 "In III-A Noise and mixture models ‣ III DVAE for Speech Enhancement ‣ Unsupervised Speech Enhancement using Dynamical Variational Autoencoders"))
and
([27](#S3.E27 "In III-B2 Introducing the past speech vectors ‣ III-B Speech reconstruction ‣ III DVAE for Speech Enhancement ‣ Unsupervised Speech Enhancement using Dynamical Variational Autoencoders"))
into
([25](#S3.E25 "In III-B1 Introducing the past and current latent variables ‣ III-B Speech reconstruction ‣ III DVAE for Speech Enhancement ‣ Unsupervised Speech Enhancement using Dynamical Variational Autoencoders")),
we have:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{s}_{t}|\mathbf{z}_{1:t}\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{x}_{1:T})`$ | $`\displaystyle\approx\mathcal{N}_{c}\left(\mathbf{x}_{t};\sqrt{g_{t}}\mathbf{s}_{t}\mathchar 59\relax\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{b}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t}\right)\mathcal{N}_{c}\left(\mathbf{s}_{t};\mathbf{0}\mathchar 59\relax\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{s}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t}\right)`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}_{c}\left(\mathbf{s}_{t};\mathbf{m}_{\mathbf{s}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t}\mathchar 59\relax\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{s}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t}\right)\mathchar 59\relax`$ |  | (28) |

where

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\mathbf{m}_{\mathbf{s}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t}=\sqrt{g_{t}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{s}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t}\left(g_{t}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{s}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{b}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathchar 59\relax`$ |  | (29) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{s}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{s}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{b}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t}(g_{t}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{s}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{b}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t})^{-1}.`$ |  | (30) |

Finally, from
([22](#S3.E22 "In III-B1 Introducing the past and current latent variables ‣ III-B Speech reconstruction ‣ III DVAE for Speech Enhancement ‣ Unsupervised Speech Enhancement using Dynamical Variational Autoencoders")),
([28](#S3.E28 "In III-B3 Computing the conditional posterior ‣ III-B Speech reconstruction ‣ III DVAE for Speech Enhancement ‣ Unsupervised Speech Enhancement using Dynamical Variational Autoencoders"))
and
([29](#S3.E29 "In III-B3 Computing the conditional posterior ‣ III-B Speech reconstruction ‣ III DVAE for Speech Enhancement ‣ Unsupervised Speech Enhancement using Dynamical Variational Autoencoders")),
the estimate of the clean speech signal is given by:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{s}}_{t}`$ | $`\displaystyle\approx\mathbb{E}_{p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{z}_{1:t}|\mathbf{x}_{1:T})}\left[\sqrt{g_{t}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{s}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t}\left(g_{t}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{s}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{b}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t}\right)^{-1}\right]\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathchar 59\relax`$ |  | (31) |

where we recall that
$`\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{s}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t}`$
is actually a function of
$`(\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_{1:t-1}\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{z}_{1:t})`$. This
speech signal estimate can be seen as the output of a “probabilistic”
Wiener filter, i.e., a Wiener filter averaged over all possible
realizations of the latent variables according to their posterior
distribution
$`p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{z}_{1:t}|\mathbf{x}_{1:T})`$.

The expectation in
([31](#S3.E31 "In III-B3 Computing the conditional posterior ‣ III-B Speech reconstruction ‣ III DVAE for Speech Enhancement ‣ Unsupervised Speech Enhancement using Dynamical Variational Autoencoders"))
is intractable, but similarly as before we can approximate it by

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\hat{\mathbf{s}}_{t}\approx\sqrt{g_{t}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{s}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t}\left(g_{t}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{s}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{b}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{x}_{t}\mathchar 59\relax
``` |  | (32) |

where
$`\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{s}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{s}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t}(\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_{1:t-1}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{1:t})`$
and $`\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{1:t}`$ is sampled from
$`p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{z}_{1:t}|\mathbf{x}_{1:T})`$. This
posterior distribution is also intractable. We thus propose to use
instead a variational approximation
$`q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{z}_{1:T}|\mathbf{x}_{1:T})`$
whose parameters $`\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime}`$ need to be jointly
estimated together with the noisy mixture model parameters
$`\boldsymbol{\varphi}`$. In the next section, we propose a VEM
algorithm to do that. This generalizes the algorithm developed for RVAE
in \[[42](#bib.bib42)\] to the whole class of DVAE models.

### III-C VEM algorithm for model parameters estimation

Now that we have an expression for the clean speech signal estimate,
what remains to be estimated is the set of mixture model parameters
$`\boldsymbol{\varphi}`$ (the NMF noise model parameters and the gains)
and the parameters $`\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime}`$ of the variational
distribution
$`q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{z}_{1:T}|\mathbf{x}_{1:T})`$.
Using a VEM algorithm, we will maximize the following ELBO defined for
the noisy speech observations $`\mathbf{x}_{1:T}`$:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\boldsymbol{\varphi};\mathbf{x}_{1:T})=\mathbb{E}_{q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{z}_{1:T}|\mathbf{x}_{1:T})}[`$ | $`\displaystyle\ln p_{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}(\mathbf{x}_{1:T}\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{z}_{1:T})`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle-\ln q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{z}_{1:T}|\mathbf{x}_{1:T})].`$ |  | (33) |

It can be shown that this corresponds to (i) maximizing with respect to
$`\boldsymbol{\varphi}`$ a lower bound of the intractable log-marginal
likelihood $`\ln p_{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})`$, and (ii)
minimizing with respect to $`\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime}`$ the KL
divergence between the variational distribution
$`q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{z}_{1:T}|\mathbf{x}_{1:T})`$
and the intractable exact posterior
$`p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{z}_{1:T}|\mathbf{x}_{1:T})`$
\[[55](#bib.bib55)\]. The proposed VEM algorithm thus consists in
iterating between the following variational E and M steps.

#### III-C1 Variational E-step

We consider a variational distribution of the same form as the DVAE
inference model:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{z}_{1:T}|\mathbf{x}_{1:T})=\prod_{t=1}^{T}q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{z}_{t}|\mathbf{z}_{1:t-1}\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{x}_{1:T})\mathchar 59\relax
``` |  | (34) |

where
$`q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{z}_{t}|\mathbf{z}_{1:t-1}\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{x}_{1:T})`$
is defined as in
([8](#S2.E8 "In II-B Speech modeling using DVAEs ‣ II DVAE and Speech Modeling ‣ Unsupervised Speech Enhancement using Dynamical Variational Autoencoders")),
except that $`\mathbf{s}_{t}`$ is replaced by $`\mathbf{x}_{t}`$. The
noisy speech frames can be considered as out-of-sample data for the DVAE
model trained on clean speech signals \[[60](#bib.bib60)\]. Therefore,
similarly as in \[[42](#bib.bib42)\], we can fine-tune the pre-trained
DVAE inference network on the noisy speech test signal, by maximizing
the ELBO in
([33](#S3.E33 "In III-C VEM algorithm for model parameters estimation ‣ III DVAE for Speech Enhancement ‣ Unsupervised Speech Enhancement using Dynamical Variational Autoencoders"))
w.r.t. $`\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime}`$. This objective function can be
developed by marginalizing and sampling, similarly to what was done in
Section [III-B](#S3.SS2 "III-B Speech reconstruction ‣ III DVAE for Speech Enhancement ‣ Unsupervised Speech Enhancement using Dynamical Variational Autoencoders").
This leads to the following expression:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\begin{split}&\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{\star};\mathbf{x}_{1:T})\overset{c}{=}\,-\sum_{f=1}^{F}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime}}}\left[\ln v_{\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{\star}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}ft}+\frac{|x_{ft}|^{2}}{v_{\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{\star}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}ft}}\right]+\\
&\frac{1}{2}\sum_{l=1}^{L}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Bigg[\ln v_{\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}lt}-\ln v_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{z}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}lt}-\frac{v_{\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}lt}+({\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}lt}-{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{z}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}lt})^{2}}{v_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{z}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}lt}}\Bigg]\mathchar 59\relax\end{split}
``` |  | (35) |

where $`x_{ft}`$ denotes the $`f`$-th entry of $`\mathbf{x}_{t}`$,
$`\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{\star}`$ denotes the current estimate of the
mixture model parameters, and
$`v_{\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{\star}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}ft}`$ is
the $`f`$-th diagonal entry of
$`\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{x}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t}`$,
whose expectation is intractable and is approximated with:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
v_{\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{\star}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}ft}=g_{t}v_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{s}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}ft}(\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_{1:t-1}\mathchar 59\relax\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{1:t})+(\mathbf{W}_{b}\mathbf{H}_{b})_{ft}.
``` |  | (36) |

We remind that $`\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_{1:t-1}`$ is computed recursively
from the output of the decoder network, as explained after equation
([27](#S3.E27 "In III-B2 Introducing the past speech vectors ‣ III-B Speech reconstruction ‣ III DVAE for Speech Enhancement ‣ Unsupervised Speech Enhancement using Dynamical Variational Autoencoders")),
and $`\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{1:t}`$ is recursively sampled from
$`q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{z}_{1:t}|\mathbf{x}_{1:T})=\prod_{\tau=1}^{t}q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{z}_{\tau}|\mathbf{z}_{1:\tau-1}\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{x}_{1:T})`$,
as defined in
([34](#S3.E34 "In III-C1 Variational E-step ‣ III-C VEM algorithm for model parameters estimation ‣ III DVAE for Speech Enhancement ‣ Unsupervised Speech Enhancement using Dynamical Variational Autoencoders")).
During the variational E-step, the parameters
$`\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime}`$ are updated with a gradient ascent
technique, and we denote by $`\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime\star}`$ the
resulting parameters that will be fixed in the M-step.

We recall that the recursive computation of $`\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_{t}`$
is required only for SRNN (actually for the DVAE models with an
autoregressive form). DKF and RVAE, as non-autoregressive models, do not
require estimating these quantities. They only require the sampling of
the latent variables $`\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{1:t}`$.

#### III-C2 M-step

The M-step consists in maximizing
$`\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime\star}\mathchar 59\relax\boldsymbol{\varphi})`$
w.r.t $`\boldsymbol{\varphi}`$ under a non-negativity constraint.
Replacing the intractable expectation in
([35](#S3.E35 "In III-C1 Variational E-step ‣ III-C VEM algorithm for model parameters estimation ‣ III DVAE for Speech Enhancement ‣ Unsupervised Speech Enhancement using Dynamical Variational Autoencoders"))
with a Monte Carlo estimate (using one single sample), the M-step can be
recast as minimizing the following criterion \[[33](#bib.bib33)\]:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(\boldsymbol{\varphi})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{f=1}^{F}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}d_{\text{IS}}\left(|x_{ft}|^{2}\mathchar 59\relax v_{\boldsymbol{\varphi}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}ft}\right)\mathchar 59\relax`$ |  | (37) |

where $`v_{\boldsymbol{\varphi}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}ft}`$ is
defined in
([36](#S3.E36 "In III-C1 Variational E-step ‣ III-C VEM algorithm for model parameters estimation ‣ III DVAE for Speech Enhancement ‣ Unsupervised Speech Enhancement using Dynamical Variational Autoencoders")).
This optimization problem can be tackled using a majorize-minimize
approach \[[61](#bib.bib61)\], which leads to the multiplicative update
rules derived in \[[33](#bib.bib33)\] using the methodology proposed in
\[[62](#bib.bib62)\]:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbf{H}_{b}\leftarrow\mathbf{H}_{b}\odot\left[\frac{\mathbf{W}_{b}^{\top}\left(|\mathbf{X}|^{\odot 2}\odot\left(\mathbf{V}_{\mathbf{x}}\right)^{\odot-2}\right)}{\mathbf{W}_{b}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{V}_{\mathbf{x}}\right)^{\odot-1}}\right]^{\odot 1/2}\mathchar 59\relax
``` |  | (38) |

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbf{W}_{b}\leftarrow\mathbf{W}_{b}\odot\left[\frac{\left(|\mathbf{X}|^{\odot 2}\odot\left(\mathbf{V}_{\mathbf{x}}\right)^{\odot-2}\right)\mathbf{H}_{b}^{\top}}{\left(\mathbf{V}_{\mathbf{x}}\right)^{\odot-1}\mathbf{H}_{b}^{\top}}\right]^{\odot 1/2}\mathchar 59\relax
``` |  | (39) |

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbf{g}^{\top}\leftarrow\mathbf{g}^{\top}\odot\left[\frac{\mathbf{1}^{\top}\left[|\mathbf{X}|^{\odot 2}\odot\left(\mathbf{V}_{\mathbf{s}}\odot\left(\mathbf{V}_{\mathbf{x}}\right)^{\odot-2}\right)\right]}{\mathbf{1}^{\top}\left[\left(\mathbf{V}_{\mathbf{s}}\odot\left(\mathbf{V}_{\mathbf{x}}\right)^{\odot-1}\right)\right]}\right]^{\odot 1/2}\mathchar 59\relax
``` |  | (40) |

where $`\odot`$ denotes element-wise multiplication and exponentiation,
and matrix division is also element-wise,
$`\mathbf{V}_{\mathbf{s}}\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{V}_{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{F\times T}`$
are the matrices of entries
$`v_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{s}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}ft}`$
and $`v_{\boldsymbol{\varphi}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}ft}`$
respectively, $`\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{C}^{F\times T}`$ is the matrix of
entries $`x_{ft}`$ and $`\mathbf{1}`$ is an all-ones column vector of
dimension $`F`$. Note that non-negativity is ensured provided that these
parameters are initialized with non-negative values.

the posterior

the mean of the resulting approximate distribution (i.e., the Wiener
filter output). The estimation of the involved parameters is made with
the VEM algorithm, which consists in iteratively fine-tuning the
inference network of the pre-trained DVAE (E-step) and updating the
mixture model parameters $`\boldsymbol{\varphi}`$ (M-step). The complete
proposed speech enhancement method is summarized in Algorithm

## IV Experiments

### IV-A Datasets

We use the WSJ0-QUT dataset and the VoiceBank-DEMAND (VB-DMD) dataset,
described below. Each dataset has a “clean” version used to pre-train
the DVAE models and a “noisy” version used to test the proposed speech
enhancement algorithm and the reference methods. The clean and noisy
versions are actually used together to compute the speech enhancement
objective performance measures (see
Section [IV-B](#S4.SS2 "IV-B Evaluation metrics ‣ IV Experiments ‣ Unsupervised Speech Enhancement using Dynamical Variational Autoencoders"))
and for the training of the supervised reference methods. When using
only the clean version, we refer to it as WSJ0 or VB.

#### IV-A1 WSJ0-QUT

We used the Wall Street Journal dataset (WSJ0) \[[63](#bib.bib63)\],
which is composed of 16kHz clean speech signals (read Wall Street
Journal news). WSJ0-QUT is the noisy version already presented and used
in \[[42](#bib.bib42)\]. It was obtained by mixing clean speech signals
from WSJ0 with various types of noise signals from the QUT-NOISE dataset
\[[64](#bib.bib64)\], with three signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) values:
$`-5`$, $`0`$, and $`5`$ dB. The full description of the dataset,
including training/test splits and noise types, can be found in
\[[42](#bib.bib42)\]. Note that we mixed the speech and noise signals
using the ITU-R BS.1770-4 protocol \[[65](#bib.bib65)\]. An SNR computed
with this protocol is $`2.5`$ dB lower (in average) than that computed
with sums of squared signal samples.

#### IV-A2 VB-DMD

We also used the publicly available VB-DMD dataset \[[66](#bib.bib66)\].
This dataset contains a training set with
$`11\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}572`$ utterances performed by $`28`$
speakers and a test set with $`824`$ utterances performed by $`2`$
speakers, different from the training set. The noisy train set consists
of mixture signals mixed at four different SNRs, namely $`15`$, $`10`$,
$`5`$, and $`0`$ dB, whereas the noisy test speech signals are corrupted
with $`17.5`$, $`12.5`$, $`7.5`$, and $`2.5`$ dB SNR and different noise
types. The full description of the dataset, including training/test
splits and noise types, can be found in \[[66](#bib.bib66)\]. Following
\[[22](#bib.bib22)\], we also selected two speakers (p226 and p287) from
the clean training set as the validation set for the training of the
DVAEs.

#### IV-A3 Data preprocessing

In all our experiments, the STFT was computed with a $`64`$-ms sine
window ($`1\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}024`$ samples) and a
$`75`$%-overlap ($`256`$ samples hop length), resulting in a sequence of
$`513`$-dimensional discrete Fourier coefficients (for positive
frequencies). The DVAEs were trained with STFT power spectrograms of
clean speech signals extracted from either WSJ0 or VB, and obtained with
the following preprocessing. We first removed the silence at the
beginning and ending of the files, using an energy-based voice activity
detection threshold of $`-30`$ dB. The waveform signal was then
normalized by its maximum absolute value. We set $`T=50`$, meaning that
speech segments of $`0.8`$s were used to train the DVAE models. In
summary, each training data sequence is a $`513\times 50`$ STFT power
spectrogram. For WSJ0, this data preprocessing resulted in a set of
$`N_{\rm{tr}}=93\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}393`$ training sequences
(representing about $`20.8`$ hours of speech signal) and
$`N_{\rm{val}}=7\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}775`$ validation sequences
(about $`1.7`$ hours). For VB, we obtained
$`N_{\rm{tr}}=29\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}389`$ training sequences
($`6.5`$ hours) and $`N_{\rm{val}}=2\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}152`$
validation sequences ($`0.5`$ hour). For the evaluation of the speech
enhancement methods, we used the STFT spectrogram of each complete noisy
test sequence (with normalization), which can be of variable length,
most often larger than $`2`$s. For WSJ0-QUT, the total duration of the
test dataset is $`1.5`$ hours, and for VB-DMD it is $`0.6`$ hour.

### IV-B Evaluation metrics

We used three metrics to evaluate the quality of the estimated speech
signals: The scale-invariant signal-to-distortion ratio (SI-SDR) in dB
\[[67](#bib.bib67)\], the perceptual evaluation of speech quality (PESQ)
score \[[24](#bib.bib24)\], and the extended short-time objective
intelligibility (ESTOI) score (in $`[0\mathchar 59\relax 1]`$)
\[[68](#bib.bib68)\]. The PESQ measure is declined in three different
variants, depending on different protocols:⁴⁴ 4 See the explanation for
‘pesq’ at
[https://github.com/ludlows/python-pesq](https://github.com/ludlows/python-pesq)
and for ‘pypesq’ and
[https://github.com/vBaiCai/python-pesq](https://github.com/vBaiCai/python-pesq).
the narrow-band PESQ MOS value (PESQ MOS, in
$`[-0.5\mathchar 59\relax 4.5]`$), the narrow-band PESQ LQ0 value (PESQ
NB, in $`[1\mathchar 59\relax 5]`$), and the wide-band PESQ LQ0 value
(PESQ WB, in $`[1\mathchar 59\relax 5]`$). We report all of them in the
following experiments. For all measures, a higher value indicates a
better result.

### IV-C Models implementation

Here we present the implementation of the three example DVAEs that we
used in practice in the proposed DVAE-based speech enhancement
algorithm, namely DKF, RVAE, and SRNN. As indicated in
\[[54](#bib.bib54)\], we can have various implementations for each DVAE
model, thus we only present the model configurations that showed the
best performance in our experiments (for the latent space dimension
selected below).

#### IV-C1 Dimension of the latent space

In the present experiments, we set $`L=16`$. We recall that the data
dimension is $`F=513`$. We also recall that $`\mathbf{z}_{t}`$ is a
real-valued vector that is modeled by a Gaussian distribution, so the
DNNs modeling $`\mathbf{z}_{t}`$ has to output two $`L`$-dimensional
vectors, the mean and variance vectors, for both inference and
generation (except for RVAE since $`\mathbf{z}_{t}`$ is assumed i.i.d.
with a standard Gaussian distribution and no DNN is used for its
generation). In contrast, $`\mathbf{s}_{t}`$ is a complex-valued vector
modeled by a circular complex Gaussian distribution, which only leaves
one $`F`$-dimensional variance vector to be provided by the decoder DNN.
To guarantee the positivity of the entries of this output variance
vector, we used log-parameterization (the output is the log-variance in
$`\mathbb{R}`$, which is then converted to variance by taking the
exponential). The last layer predicting the mean and log-variance
parameters is always a linear layer, with a dimension corresponding to
that of $`\mathbf{z}_{t}`$ (16) or $`\mathbf{s}_{t}`$ (513). We omit
this in the following description for simplicity.

#### IV-C2 DKF

Fig. [3](#S4.F3 "Fig. 3 ‣ IV-A3 Data preprocessing ‣ IV-A Datasets ‣ IV Experiments ‣ Unsupervised Speech Enhancement using Dynamical Variational Autoencoders")(a)
summarizes our implementation of DKF. The layers providing the
parameters of the inference and generative models of $`\mathbf{z}_{t}`$
are respectively implemented with the specific combiner function and
gated transition function described in \[[47](#bib.bib47)\]. For the
inference model, we used a backward long short-term memory (LSTM)
\[[69](#bib.bib69)\] layer with an internal state of dimension $`128`$
to accumulate the information from $`\mathbf{s}_{t:T}`$ in
([11](#S2.E11 "In II-C1 DKF ‣ II-C Three representative DVAE models ‣ II DVAE and Speech Modeling ‣ Unsupervised Speech Enhancement using Dynamical Variational Autoencoders")).
Before being fed into the recurrent layer, each vector
$`\mathbf{s}_{t}`$ passes through a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) with
one hidden layer of dimension $`256`$ and a $`\tanh`$ activation. The
variance parameters of the generative model of $`\mathbf{s}_{t}`$ are
provided by an MLP with 4 hidden layers of dimension $`32`$, $`64`$,
$`128`$ and $`256`$, with a $`\tanh`$ activation function.

#### IV-C3 RVAE

We implemented the non-causal version of RVAE as schematized in
Fig. [3](#S4.F3 "Fig. 3 ‣ IV-A3 Data preprocessing ‣ IV-A Datasets ‣ IV Experiments ‣ Unsupervised Speech Enhancement using Dynamical Variational Autoencoders")(b).
The inference model includes a bidirectional LSTM (BLSTM) layer with an
internal state of dimension $`128`$ to process the complete sequence
$`\mathbf{s}_{1:T}`$ and an LSTM layer to process the sampled past
latent vectors sequence $`\mathbf{z}_{1:t-1}`$. The output of these two
layers are then concatenated and mapped into the parameters of the
inference model over $`\mathbf{z}_{t}`$ by an MLP. The generative part
of the model includes a BLSTM layer with an internal state of dimension
128, which takes the sampled $`\mathbf{z}_{1:T}`$ as input. The output
of this BLSTM layer is finally mapped to the parameters of the
generative model over $`\mathbf{s}_{t}`$ by a single linear layer.

#### IV-C4 SRNN

SRNN is quite different from the two previous models. As shown in
Fig. [3](#S4.F3 "Fig. 3 ‣ IV-A3 Data preprocessing ‣ IV-A Datasets ‣ IV Experiments ‣ Unsupervised Speech Enhancement using Dynamical Variational Autoencoders")(c),
the inference and generative models share a recurrent internal state
vector $`\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{h}}_{t}`$ (module in green) that is
encoding the information from the past observed vectors
$`\mathbf{s}_{1:t-1}`$. This shared module is composed of an MLP with
one layer of dimension $`256`$ followed by a forward LSTM. The dimension
of $`\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{h}}_{t}`$ is $`128`$. For inference,
the concatenation of $`\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{h}}_{t}`$ and
$`\mathbf{s}_{t}`$ is fed into a one-layer MLP of dimension $`256`$
followed by a backward LSTM that provides the vector
$`\overleftarrow{\mathbf{g}}_{t}`$ (of dimension $`128`$). This vector
is then concatenated with the sample of $`\mathbf{z}_{t-1}`$ and fed
into an MLP with two hidden layers of dimension $`64`$ and $`32`$. For
the generative part, we concatenate the shared state
$`\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{h}}_{t}`$ with the sampled latent vector
at the previous or current time frame. An MLP with two hidden layers of
dimension $`64`$ and $`32`$ is used for the generation of
$`\mathbf{z}_{t}`$, and an MLP with one hidden layer of dimension 128 is
used for the generation of $`\mathbf{s}_{t}`$. All MLP hidden layers use
the $`\tanh`$ activation function.

### IV-D DVAEs pre-training

For the pre-training of the three DVAE models on clean speech signals,
we used the Adam optimizer \[[70](#bib.bib70)\] with a learning rate of
$`1e\!-\!3`$ and $`\beta_{1}=0.9`$, $`\beta_{2}=0.99`$. On both
datasets, we trained each model with a batch size of $`128`$ during
$`300`$ epochs and kept the model snapshot with lowest validation loss.
We applied a linear KL annealing for the first $`20`$ epochs to warm-up
the latent space \[[71](#bib.bib71)\].

As an autoregressive model, SRNN deserves a particular treatment during
pre-training. Indeed, in the conventional training of autoregressive
models, the ground-truth past clean speech vectors
$`\mathbf{s}_{1:t-1}`$ are used to generate the current one
$`\mathbf{s}_{t}`$, a strategy sometimes referred to as “teacher
forcing” in the literature \[[72](#bib.bib72)\]. We have seen in
Section [III-B2](#S3.SS2.SSS2 "III-B2 Introducing the past speech vectors ‣ III-B Speech reconstruction ‣ III DVAE for Speech Enhancement ‣ Unsupervised Speech Enhancement using Dynamical Variational Autoencoders")
that it is not possible to do that in the proposed speech enhancement
algorithm, where $`\mathbf{s}_{1:t-1}`$ is replaced by its proxy
$`\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_{1:t-1}`$ (recursively computed from the decoder
output). It is shown in \[[54](#bib.bib54)\] that directly using
$`\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_{1:t-1}`$ in the SRNN model trained with
$`\mathbf{s}_{1:t-1}`$ significantly decreases the quality of the
reconstructed speech spectrogram, due to the mismatch between train and
test conditions. To avoid such a mismatch (here between DVAE training
and speech enhancement conditions), we trained SRNN using
$`\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_{1:t-1}`$ (instead of $`\mathbf{s}_{1:t-1}`$) to
generate $`\mathbf{s}_{t}`$. Such a training is difficult in practice
and to make it efficient, we adopted a “scheduled sampling” approach
\[[73](#bib.bib73)\], i.e., we progressively replace
$`\mathbf{s}_{1:t-1}`$ with $`\tilde{{\mathbf{s}}}_{1:t-1}`$, with a
proportion going from $`0\%`$ to $`100\%`$ along the training
iterations. That is, at the beginning of the training,
$`\mathbf{s}_{t}`$ is generated completely from $`\mathbf{s}_{1:t-1}`$
and $`\mathbf{z}_{t}`$. Then the probability to use
$`\tilde{{\mathbf{s}}}_{1:t-1}`$ increases during the training
procedure. Finally, $`\mathbf{s}_{t}`$ is generated completely from
$`\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_{1:t-1}`$ and $`\mathbf{z}_{t}`$. This takes 80
epochs after the KL annealing step.

optimizer \[[70](#bib.bib70)\] with a learning rate of $`1e\!-\!3`$.

### IV-F Baselines

Regarding baseline supervised methods, we used Open-Unmix (UMX) and
MetricGAN+. UMX is an open-source method based on a BLSTM network. It
was originally proposed for music source separation \[[76](#bib.bib76)\]
and was later adapted for speech enhancement \[[75](#bib.bib75)\].
MetricGAN+ \[[10](#bib.bib10)\] also adopts BLSTMs for mask-based
prediction of the clean speech. In addition, it introduces a metric
network that is trained to approximate the PESQ evaluation score, and
then MetricGAN+ is trained to maximise this proxy of the PESQ score.

Regarding baseline unsupervised methods, we choose VAE-VEM
\[[42](#bib.bib42)\], MetricGAN-U \[[22](#bib.bib22)\] and NyTT
\[[21](#bib.bib21)\]. VAE-VEM, referred to as VAE-FFNN in
 \[[42](#bib.bib42)\], uses the same optimization methodology as our
approach, except there is no temporal model. In practice, we tried with
different model complexities for the VAE, and we report the one
exhibiting the best performance. MetricGAN-U is the unsupervised version
of MetricGAN+. Since the supervision in MetricGAN+ comes from the PESQ
score computation using the paired enhanced and clean speech signal,
MetricGAN-U adopts a non-intrusive speech quality metric instead, namely
the DNSMOS measure \[[23](#bib.bib23)\], to bypass the paired
supervision. Two versions of MetricGAN-U were proposed by the authors:
the “full” one is trained entirely without supervision, while the “half”
version monitors the PESQ measure to perform early-stopping. Since PESQ
is an intrusive measure, this version of MetricGAN-U can be seen as
weakly supervised (supervision is used only for validation and not for
training per se) \[[22](#bib.bib22)\]. NyTT is based on a noisy speech
target training strategy, where the network is trained to remove an
additional noise added to the noisy speech. Since there is no need for
noisy/clean speech pairs, NyTT can also be considered as an unsupervised
speech enhancement method. It should be noted that both MetricGAN-U and
NyTT are trained using noisy speech as input, so the resulting model is
noise-dependent. This contrasts with the proposed method where only
clean speech is used for the DVAE pre-training, resulting in a
noise-agnostic speech enhancement method. Finally, we could not re-train
MetricGAN-U or NyTT. Indeed, since MetricGAN-U uses the DNSMOS service,
each training epoch needs 2 days for evaluation, which is impractical.
Regarding NyTT, we cannot retrain it since there is no public release of
the code.

When comparing with the baseline unsupervised methods, hence only on the
VB-DMD dataset, the proposed method achieves competitive results. Even
if the highest SI-SDR performance is achieved by NyTT ($`17.7`$ dB),
this is possibly due to the use of large amounts of noise, given that
its performance drops significantly ($`12.1`$ dB) when only the noise
from the DMD dataset is used. The proposed RVAE-VEM algorithm reaches
very similar performance ($`17.1`$ dB) without training on any kind of
noise. In terms of PESQ WB, the proposed approaches outperform NyTT
independently of the amount of noise used at training time. Similar
conclusions are drawn when comparing to MetricGAN-U, in which case the
performance difference in terms of SI-SDR is very large ($`+8`$ to
$`+10`$ dB). Remarkably, the proposed method achieves competitive
performance with, and sometimes outperforms, MetricGAN-U (half) in terms
of PESQ WB score, even though MetricGAN-U (half) uses the PESQ WB score
on the validation set as a training stop criterion. In this regard,
MetricGAN-U (half) is expected to exhibit higher PESQ WB values than
MetricGAN-U (full).

When comparing with the supervised methods, we see that MetricGAN+
obtains PESQ scores that are significantly higher (e.g., $`3.59`$ and
$`2.83`$ PESQ NB on VB-DMD and WSJ0-QUT respectively) than those of all
the other methods (RVAE-VEM reaches a maximum of $`3.23`$ and $`2.27`$
on VB-DMD and WSJ0-QUT). This can be explained by the fact that PESQ is
the criterion optimized during the MetricGAN+ model training. In
contrast, when it is evaluated with other metrics, the results obtained
by this method are considerably worse, especially in terms SI-SDR for
which MetricGAN+ performs the worst. We found that this may be due to
the fact that the energy in the speech signal estimated by MetricGAN+ is
mostly concentrated in the low-frequency part of the spectrum, whereas
the mid- and high-frequency parts are poorly recovered. Regarding UMX,
we observe that its performance is systematically under the one of the
proposed method, in terms of SI-SDR or any of the PESQ measures for both
datasets.

To evaluate the generalization capability of the different models, we
report in
Table [III](#S4.T3 "TABLE III ‣ IV-G Speech enhancement results ‣ IV Experiments ‣ Unsupervised Speech Enhancement using Dynamical Variational Autoencoders")
their performance obtained when the training set and test set originate
from different corpora. It is unsurprising that the performance of
supervised methods significantly decreases in this setting. For example,
MetricGAN+ obtains a PESQ WB value of $`3.13`$ on the VB-DMD test
dataset when trained on the VB-DMD train dataset, whereas it drops to
$`2.51`$ when trained on the WSJ0-QUT. In the same vein, UMX goes from
$`14.0`$ dB SI-SDR to $`10.4`$ dB SI-SDR. Similary behavior is found for
the other metrics and datasets. As for the unsupervised noise-dependent
methods, we can also see a significant decrease of performance. For
example, Metric-GAN-U (trained on VB-DMD and provided by the authors)
obtains a negative SI-SDR when tested on WSJ0-QUT. In contrast, the
proposed DVAE-VEM algorithm is much less affected by the dataset
mismatch (for all DKF, RVAE, and SRNN models). Even if, generally
speaking, we observe a mild decrease in performance when the training
and test sets do not match, the proposed method occasionally exhibits
better performance when tested in a different dataset. For example, the
RVAE-VEM algorithm tested on VB-DMD with RVAE trained on WSJ0 obtains
$`17.3`$ dB of SI-SDR, which is even better than when RVAE is trained on
VB ($`17.1`$ dB). This is probably because the WSJ0 dataset is larger
than the VB dataset. RVAE-VEM performs the best when trained on WSJ0 and
tested on VB-DMD, while SRNN-VEM performs the best when trained on VB
and tested on WSJ0-QUT. Overall, we can conclude that the proposed
DVAE-based speech enhancement method exhibits competitive performance
when compared to other supervised and unsupervised methods in the
“corresponding dataset” setting, and superior performance in the
cross-dataset setting.

At the light of the presented results, we extract the following
concluding remarks. When there is no mismatch between the training and
test datasets, the proposed method achieves state-of-the-art performance
in unsupervised speech enhancement, and competitive results when
compared with supervised speech enhancement methods (often outperforming
them depending on the metric). As for cross-dataset experiments, where
the training and test sets are coming from different datasets, we
observe that the performance of the supervised methods is severely
affected by the dataset mismatch, whereas the performance of the
proposed unsupervised method is very robust to it. Overall, the results
obtained in the various settings demonstrate the interest of the
proposed DVAE-VEM methodology for speech enhancement. Audio examples and
code are available at
[https://team.inria.fr/robotlearn/unsupervised-speech-enhancement-using-dynamical-variational-auto-encoders](https://team.inria.fr/robotlearn/unsupervised-speech-enhancement-using-dynamical-variational-auto-encoders).

## V Conclusion

In this paper, we have proposed a general framework for unsupervised
speech enhancement based on DVAEs. In our framework, the DVAEs are used
to model the clean speech signal, while the noise is modeled via NMF.
While DVAEs are pre-trained with a clean speech dataset, the noise
parameters are estimated at test time, together with the clean speech,
from the noisy speech sequence to process. To achieve that, we have
derived a VEM algorithm for the most general formulation of a DVAE
model, which can then be easily adapted to particular instances of
DVAEs. We have illustrated this principle with DKF, RVAE and SRNN, and
this can be extended to other DVAE models, e.g., STORN
\[[51](#bib.bib51)\] or VRNN \[[48](#bib.bib48)\].

We have evaluated the speech enhancement performance obtained with those
three example DVAEs. The proposed approach exhibits superior or
competitive performance compared to supervised and unsupervised
state-of-the-art methods when the training and test datasets are from
the same corpora, and outperforms them on cross-dataset settings, i.e.,
when the training and test datasets are from different corpora. The RVAE
model provided the best performance among the tested DVAEs. SRNN shows a
great potential, provided that it is trained with scheduled sampling in
order to reduce the gap between the training and speech enhancement
conditions. If this gap could be further decreased, we believe that it
could have even better performance than RVAE. This aspect should be
further investigated, possibly including other autoregressive models in
the DVAE family (e.g., VRNN \[[48](#bib.bib48)\]).

So far, the good performance of the proposed iterative DVAE-VEM
algorithm comes at the cost of a high computational time. Indeed,
processing one second of audio with 100 iterations of the algorithm
takes approximately 14, 25 and 21 seconds for DKF, RVAE and SRNN,
respectively, using a single core of an Intel Xeon Gold 6230 at 2.1GHz.
Future work will include developing fast DVAE-based speech enhancement
algorithms, for instance inspiring from \[[35](#bib.bib35)\]. Also, so
far, the inference with DVAEs is non-causal, meaning that past, present,
and future noisy speech observations are required to enhance a given
speech frame. Causal DVAE-based speech enhancement can also be
investigated, but is out of the scope of this paper. Future work also
includes using other powerful encoder-decoder networks, e.g., TCNs
\[[44](#bib.bib44)\] and the Transformer \[[77](#bib.bib77)\], in the
present unsupervised speech enhancement framework. The DVAE models may
be further boosted with more expressive latent variables, e.g.,
introducing hierarchical multi-scale structure and normalizing flows
\[[78](#bib.bib78)\]. We also plan to extend the proposed method to a
multi-modal framework, using the speaker’s lips motion and visual
appearance, in the continuation of VAE-based audio-visual speech
enhancement \[[79](#bib.bib79)\].
````
