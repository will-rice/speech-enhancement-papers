# CONDITIONING AND SAMPLING IN VARIATIONAL DIFFUSION MODELS FOR SPEECH SUPER-RESOLUTION

Chin-Yun Yu\sthanksThe majority of this work was conducted independently
before the author enrolled in Queen Mary University of London   
Sung-Lin Yeh    György Fazekas    Hao Tang

###### Abstract

Recently, diffusion models (DMs) have been increasingly used in audio
processing tasks, including speech super-resolution (SR), which aims to
restore high-frequency content given low-resolution speech utterances.
This is commonly achieved by conditioning the network of noise predictor
with low-resolution audio. In this paper, we propose a novel sampling
algorithm that communicates the information of the low-resolution audio
via the reverse sampling process of DMs. The proposed method can be a
drop-in replacement for the vanilla sampling process and can
significantly improve the performance of the existing works. Moreover,
by coupling the proposed sampling method with an unconditional DM, i.e.,
a DM with no auxiliary inputs to its noise predictor, we can generalize
it to a wide range of SR setups. We also attain state-of-the-art results
on the VCTK Multi-Speaker benchmark with this novel formulation.

###### Index Terms: 

Robust speech super-resolution, bandwidth extension, diffusion
probabilistic models, inverse problem solving, variational lower bound

## 1 Introduction

Speech super-resolution (SR), also known as bandwidth extension, is a
speech generation task that reconstructs high-resolution speech
utterances from low-resolution ones. NU-Wave \[[1](#bib.bib1)\] and
NU-Wave 2 \[[2](#bib.bib2)\] are based on diffusion models
(DMs) \[[3](#bib.bib3), [4](#bib.bib4), [5](#bib.bib5), [6](#bib.bib6),
[7](#bib.bib7), [8](#bib.bib8)\] and are able to reconstruct speech up
to 48 kHz. However, due to the stochastic nature of the diffusion
sampling, and because low-resolution audio is only fed into the noise
predictor network, the low-frequency spectra between the reconstructed
output and the input can deviate \[[2](#bib.bib2)\], where the
difference should ideally be zero. We aim to explore the possibility to
enhance existing diffusion SR models.

DMs are not only impressive generative models, but also powerful
unsupervised problem solvers, by incorporating the condition into the
reverse sampling process with unconditional DMs \[[4](#bib.bib4),
[9](#bib.bib9), [10](#bib.bib10), [11](#bib.bib11), [12](#bib.bib12)\].
This framework has shown impressive results in various inverse problems
for image processing, such as image inpainting \[[9](#bib.bib9),
[10](#bib.bib10), [11](#bib.bib11)\], image
super-resolution \[[9](#bib.bib9), [11](#bib.bib11)\], and medical image
reconstruction \[[12](#bib.bib12)\]. For speech processing, although DMs
have been used as vocoders \[[6](#bib.bib6), [7](#bib.bib7)\], speech
enhancement \[[13](#bib.bib13), [14](#bib.bib14)\], and speech
SR \[[1](#bib.bib1), [2](#bib.bib2)\], there has been no similar attempt
to design a sampling method for recovering audio signals beyond additive
noise \[[13](#bib.bib13), [14](#bib.bib14)\].

In this work, we cast the task of speech SR as an inpainting problem in
the frequency-domain, and propose a diffusion sampling algorithm. The
proposed method can be a drop-in replacement for the reverse sampling
process in other diffusion SR models for quality improvements. Moreover,
by combining the method with a UDM, our approach can generalize to
various SR conditions, such as varying upscaling ratios and types of
downsampling filters. We also observed that training audio DMs on the
variational lower bound is beneficial for our method. Finally, we
outperform the state-of-the-art in terms of _log-spectral-distance_
(LSD) on the 48kHz VCTK Multi-Speaker benchmark, demonstrating
robustness against different downsampling setups.¹¹ 1 The source code
and audio samples are available from
[https://iamycy.github.io/diffwave-sr](https://iamycy.github.io/diffwave-sr).

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Audio resampling

Audio resampling is a function that maps the discrete audio signal
$`{\mathbf{x}}=[x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{Tl}]`$ sampled at frequency $`l`$ to
$`{\mathbf{y}}=[y_{1},y_{2},...,y_{Th}]`$ with a new sampling frequency
$`h`$, where $`T`$ is the length in seconds. It can further be
categorized as downsampling or upsampling depending on whether $`l`$ is
greater or smaller than $`h`$. According to the Nyquist theorem, the
highest frequency a signal can contain is half of its sampling
frequency. Thus, a lowpass filter with a cutoff frequency
$`\min(l,h)/2`$ is applied to avoid aliasing when resampling
$`{\mathbf{x}}`$. Notice that downsampling eliminates all frequency
information between $`l/2`$ to $`h/2`$. Reversing this process is the
essence of SR. To distinguish it from regular upsampling, we will refer
to this operation as upscaling and use upscaling ratio for $`l/h`$ in
later paragraphs.

### 2.2 Variational diffusion models

Variational DMs (VDMs) \[[3](#bib.bib3), [5](#bib.bib5),
[8](#bib.bib8)\] can generate complex samples starting from a simple
normal distribution with a Markov chain structure. In particular, VDM
assumes an observed sample $`{\mathbf{x}}`$ is generated by a chain of
$`T`$ latent variables $`{\mathbf{z}}_{t}`$. Sampling
$`{\mathbf{z}}_{t}`$ from $`{\mathbf{x}}`$ is trivial because its
distribution is parameterized as

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
q({\mathbf{z}}_{t}|{\mathbf{x}})=\mathcal{N}(\alpha_{t}{\mathbf{x}},\sigma_{t}^{2}\mathbf{I}),
``` |  | (1) |

where $`\alpha_{t}\in(0,1)`$, $`\sigma_{t}^{2}=1-\alpha_{t}^{2}`$,
$`1\leq t\leq T`$. $`\alpha_{t}`$ is monotonically decreasing in $`t`$,
making $`{\mathbf{z}}_{t}`$ noisier in $`t`$ and eventually
$`q({\mathbf{z}}_{T}|{\mathbf{x}})\approx\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I})`$.
Sampling $`{\mathbf{x}}`$, however, requires a chain of actions.
Starting from $`{\mathbf{z}}_{T}`$ sampled from
$`p({\mathbf{z}}_{T})=\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I})`$, the *reverse
diffusion* step $`p({\mathbf{z}}_{t-1}|{\mathbf{z}}_{t})`$ is applied
repeatedly until $`{\mathbf{z}}_{1}\approx{\mathbf{x}}`$. We do not know
the distribution of $`p({\mathbf{z}}_{t-1}|{\mathbf{z}}_{t})`$, but we
can approximate it by maximizing the variational lower bound (VLB)

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\begin{split}\log p({\mathbf{x}})\geq VLB({\mathbf{x}})&=\mathbb{E}_{q({\mathbf{z}}_{1}|{\mathbf{x}})}[\log p({\mathbf{x}}|{\mathbf{z}}_{1})]\\
&-D_{KL}(q({\mathbf{z}}_{T}|{\mathbf{x}})||p({\mathbf{z}}_{T}))\\
&-\mathcal{L}_{T}({\mathbf{x}}),\end{split}
``` |  | (2) |

with diffusion loss

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{T}({\mathbf{x}})=\sum_{t=2}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[D_{KL}\left(q({\mathbf{z}}_{t-1}|{\mathbf{z}}_{t},{\mathbf{x}})||p({\mathbf{z}}_{t-1}|{\mathbf{z}}_{t})\right)\right].
``` |  | (3) |

The term $`q({\mathbf{z}}_{t-1}|{\mathbf{z}}_{t},{\mathbf{x}})`$ can be
derived from
Eq. ([1](#S2.E1 "In 2.2 Variational diffusion models ‣ 2 Background ‣ CONDITIONING AND SAMPLING IN VARIATIONAL DIFFUSION MODELS FOR SPEECH SUPER-RESOLUTION"))
using Bayes rule and is given by

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\begin{split}&q({\mathbf{z}}_{t-1}|{\mathbf{z}}_{t},{\mathbf{x}})=\\
\mathcal{N}&\left(\frac{\alpha_{\bar{t}}\sigma_{t-1}^{2}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}{\mathbf{z}}_{t}+\frac{\alpha_{t-1}\sigma_{\bar{t}}^{2}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}{\mathbf{x}},\frac{\sigma_{\bar{t}}^{2}\sigma_{t-1}^{2}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}\mathbf{I}\right),\end{split}
``` |  | (4) |

where $`\alpha_{\bar{t}}=\alpha_{t}/\alpha_{t-1}`$ and
$`\sigma_{\bar{t}}^{2}=\sigma_{t}^{2}-\alpha_{\bar{t}}^{2}\sigma_{t-1}^{2}`$.
To train the model, a common practice is to parameterize
$`p({\mathbf{z}}_{t-1}|{\mathbf{z}}_{t})`$ as
$`q({\mathbf{z}}_{t-1}|{\mathbf{z}}_{t},{\mathbf{x}}=\hat{{\mathbf{x}}}_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{t};t))`$
where
$`\hat{{\mathbf{x}}}_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{t};t)=({\mathbf{z}}_{t}-\sigma_{t}\hat{{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}}_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{t};t))/\alpha_{t}`$
and $`\hat{{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}}_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{t};t)`$ is a
neural network that predicts the added noise from
$`{\mathbf{z}}_{t}`$ \[[5](#bib.bib5), [8](#bib.bib8)\].

For training efficiency, we can use the following equation to replace
Eq. ([3](#S2.E3 "In 2.2 Variational diffusion models ‣ 2 Background ‣ CONDITIONING AND SAMPLING IN VARIATIONAL DIFFUSION MODELS FOR SPEECH SUPER-RESOLUTION")):

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}({\mathbf{x}})=\frac{\delta_{max}-\delta_{min}}{2}\mathbb{E}_{{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}},\upsilon}\left[\|{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}-\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}}_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{\upsilon};\upsilon)\|_{2}^{2}\right],
``` |  | (5) |

where
$`\delta_{max}=\log(\alpha_{1}^{2}/\sigma_{1}^{2}),\delta_{min}=\log(\alpha_{\infty}^{2}/\sigma_{\infty}^{2}),{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I}),\upsilon\sim\mathcal{U}(\delta_{min},\delta_{max})`$,
and
$`{\mathbf{z}}_{\upsilon}=\sqrt{\phi(\upsilon)}{\mathbf{x}}+\sqrt{\phi(-\upsilon)}{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}`$,
$`\phi`$ is the sigmoid function.
Eq. ([5](#S2.E5 "In 2.2 Variational diffusion models ‣ 2 Background ‣ CONDITIONING AND SAMPLING IN VARIATIONAL DIFFUSION MODELS FOR SPEECH SUPER-RESOLUTION"))
is a stochastic estimate of diffusion loss when $`T\rightarrow\infty`$.
The related proof can be found in \[[8](#bib.bib8)\]. Prior works of
audio DMs \[[1](#bib.bib1), [2](#bib.bib2), [6](#bib.bib6),
[7](#bib.bib7), [13](#bib.bib13), [14](#bib.bib14)\] were trained with a
biased diffusion
loss ([3](#S2.E3 "In 2.2 Variational diffusion models ‣ 2 Background ‣ CONDITIONING AND SAMPLING IN VARIATIONAL DIFFUSION MODELS FOR SPEECH SUPER-RESOLUTION"))
and fixed noise schedule endpoints ($`\alpha_{1}`$ and
$`\alpha_{\infty})`$. However, Kingma et al. \[[8](#bib.bib8)\] show
that training on the VLB with a learnable $`\alpha_{t}`$ can improve the
likelihood on unconditional generation.

## 3 Approach

Assuming we have $`\alpha_{t}`$ and
$`\hat{{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}}_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{t};t)`$ that
accurately estimate the high-resolution audio distribution
$`p({\mathbf{x}})`$, we can then leverage it to perform audio
super-resolution by injecting low-resolution audio $`{\mathbf{y}}`$ into
the sampling process if the downsampling scheme is known. In other
words, we can transform the prior distribution $`p({\mathbf{x}})`$ into
a conditional distribution $`p({\mathbf{x}}|{\mathbf{y}})`$ by replacing
$`p({\mathbf{z}}_{t-1}|{\mathbf{z}}_{t})`$ with the *conditional reverse
diffusion* step $`p({\mathbf{z}}_{t-1}|{\mathbf{z}}_{t},{\mathbf{y}})`$.
We introduce $`\hat{{\mathbf{y}}}`$ by upsampling $`{\mathbf{y}}`$ to
make sure all the signals have the same dimension. The process of
downsampling followed by upsampling can be viewed as a low pass filter
$`\mathcal{F}_{h/2}`$ with cut off frequency $`h/2`$ so
$`\hat{{\mathbf{y}}}=\mathcal{F}_{h/2}({\mathbf{x}})`$. Let
$`\mathbf{X}^{\omega},\hat{\mathbf{Y}}^{\omega},\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\omega},\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{t}^{\omega}\in\mathbb{C}`$
be the response of
$`{\mathbf{x}},\hat{{\mathbf{y}}},{\mathbf{z}}_{t},\hat{{\mathbf{x}}}_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{t};t)`$
at frequency $`\omega`$ after a normalized Fourier transform. We can
rewrite
Eq. ([4](#S2.E4 "In 2.2 Variational diffusion models ‣ 2 Background ‣ CONDITIONING AND SAMPLING IN VARIATIONAL DIFFUSION MODELS FOR SPEECH SUPER-RESOLUTION"))
in the frequency-domain as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\begin{split}&q(\mathbf{Z}_{t-1}^{\omega}|\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\omega},\mathbf{X}^{\omega})=\\
\mathcal{CN}&\left(\frac{\alpha_{\bar{t}}\sigma_{t-1}^{2}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\omega}+\frac{\alpha_{t-1}\sigma_{\bar{t}}^{2}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}\mathbf{X}^{\omega},\frac{\sigma_{\bar{t}}^{2}\sigma_{t-1}^{2}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}\right).\end{split}
``` |  | (6) |

$`\hat{\mathbf{Y}}^{\omega}`$ equals $`c_{\omega}\mathbf{X}^{\omega}`$
where $`c_{\omega}`$ is the coefficient of $`\mathcal{F}_{h/2}`$ at
frequency $`\omega`$. If $`\mathcal{F}_{h/2}`$ is ideal, $`c_{\omega}`$
equals one when $`\omega<h/2`$ and zero otherwise. In order to utilize
the available $`\mathbf{X}^{\omega}`$ from
$`\hat{\mathbf{Y}}^{\omega}`$, we propose to parameterize the reverse
step as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\begin{split}&p(\mathbf{Z}_{t-1}^{\omega}|\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\omega},\hat{\mathbf{Y}}^{\omega})=\\
&q(\mathbf{Z}_{t-1}^{\omega}|\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\omega},\mathbf{X}^{\omega}=\hat{\mathbf{Y}}^{\omega}+(1-c_{\omega})\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{t}^{\omega}).\end{split}
``` |  | (7) |

What Eq.
([7](#S3.E7 "In 3 Approach ‣ CONDITIONING AND SAMPLING IN VARIATIONAL DIFFUSION MODELS FOR SPEECH SUPER-RESOLUTION"))
does is it replaces $`\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{t}^{\omega}`$ in the
low-frequency region with ground truth $`\mathbf{X}^{\omega}`$. In this
way, we can sample low frequencies from the ground truth information,
and the deviation of low frequencies in the final output
$`\hat{{\mathbf{x}}}_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{1};1)`$ is then proportional
to the variance $`\sigma_{1}^{2}/\alpha_{1}^{2}`$ according to Eq.
([1](#S2.E1 "In 2.2 Variational diffusion models ‣ 2 Background ‣ CONDITIONING AND SAMPLING IN VARIATIONAL DIFFUSION MODELS FOR SPEECH SUPER-RESOLUTION")).
Although we still use the estimation $`\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{t}^{\omega}`$
to sample high frequencies, since
$`\hat{{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}}_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{t};t)`$ utilize
all the frequency in $`{\mathbf{z}}_{t}`$ for noise prediction, the
low-frequency information of $`{\mathbf{x}}`$ in $`{\mathbf{z}}_{t}`$
can implicitly guide the whole process to gradually *inpaint* the
missing high frequencies that are coherent to the provided low
frequencies. In addition,
$`\hat{{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}}_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{t};t)`$ can come
from any DM which is either unconditional or only conditioned on
$`{\mathbf{y}}`$.

Most of the existing audio DMs operate in the
time-domain \[[1](#bib.bib1), [2](#bib.bib2), [6](#bib.bib6),
[7](#bib.bib7), [13](#bib.bib13)\]. Fortunately, the time-domain version
of
Eq. ([7](#S3.E7 "In 3 Approach ‣ CONDITIONING AND SAMPLING IN VARIATIONAL DIFFUSION MODELS FOR SPEECH SUPER-RESOLUTION")),
$`p({\mathbf{z}}_{t-1}|{\mathbf{z}}_{t},\hat{{\mathbf{y}}})`$, is equal
to
$`q({\mathbf{z}}_{t-1}|{\mathbf{z}}_{t},{\mathbf{x}}=\hat{{\mathbf{y}}}+\hat{{\mathbf{x}}}_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{t};t)-\mathcal{F}_{h/2}(\hat{{\mathbf{x}}}_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{t};t)))`$.
If we use time-domain filters for $`\mathcal{F}_{h/2}`$ then the whole
reverse process can be done in the time-domain. On the one hand, this
process is similar to RePaint \[[10](#bib.bib10)\] but the masking is
performed in the frequency-domain based on Eq.
([7](#S3.E7 "In 3 Approach ‣ CONDITIONING AND SAMPLING IN VARIATIONAL DIFFUSION MODELS FOR SPEECH SUPER-RESOLUTION")).
On the other hand, the use of filters helps to perform diffusion without
projecting $`{\mathbf{z}}_{t}`$ into spectral space in every iteration,
either using transformation matrices calculated from
SVD \[[9](#bib.bib9)\] or Fourier transform \[[12](#bib.bib12)\], thus
it is computationally more efficient. In practice, we set
$`p({\mathbf{x}}|{\mathbf{z}}_{1},{\mathbf{y}})`$ the same as
$`p({\mathbf{x}}|{\mathbf{z}}_{1})`$ and assume
$`p({\mathbf{z}}_{T}|\hat{{\mathbf{y}}})=p({\mathbf{z}}_{T})`$.

To further increase performance, we also incorporate the Manifold
Constrained Gradient (MCG) \[[11](#bib.bib11)\] method into our
algorithm. Specifically, we subtract a gradient term
$`\partial\|\hat{{\mathbf{y}}}-\mathcal{F}_{h/2}(\hat{{\mathbf{x}}}_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{t};t)))\|_{2}^{2}/\partial{\mathbf{z}}_{t}`$
in each reverse step. We control this gradient term with a step size
$`\eta`$. The full inference procedure is summarized in Algorithm
[1](#algorithm1 "In 3 Approach ‣ CONDITIONING AND SAMPLING IN VARIATIONAL DIFFUSION MODELS FOR SPEECH SUPER-RESOLUTION").

Require : $`{\mathbf{y}},T,\eta`$

2: def *$`\mathcal{F}_{h/2}({\mathbf{x}})`$*:

    3: return Upsample(*Downsample(*$`{\mathbf{x}}`$*)*)

4: $`\hat{{\mathbf{y}}}\leftarrow`$ Upsample(*$`{\mathbf{y}}`$*)

5: $`{\mathbf{z}}_{T}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I})`$

6: for *$`t=T,T-1,...,2`$* do

    7:
$`\hat{{\mathbf{x}}}\leftarrow({\mathbf{z}}_{t}-\sigma_{t}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}}_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{t};\delta_{t}))/\alpha_{t}`$

    8:
$`\mathbf{g}\leftarrow\frac{\partial}{\partial{\mathbf{z}}_{t}}\|\hat{{\mathbf{y}}}-\mathcal{F}_{h/2}(\hat{{\mathbf{x}}})\|_{2}^{2}`$
% MCG

    9:
$`\hat{{\mathbf{x}}}\leftarrow\hat{{\mathbf{y}}}+\hat{{\mathbf{x}}}-\mathcal{F}_{h/2}(\hat{{\mathbf{x}}})`$
% Inpainting

    10:
$`\boldsymbol{\mu}\leftarrow\frac{\alpha_{\bar{t}}\sigma_{t-1}^{2}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}{\mathbf{z}}_{t}+\frac{\alpha_{t-1}\sigma_{\bar{t}}^{2}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}\hat{{\mathbf{x}}}`$

    11:
$`\boldsymbol{\mu}\leftarrow\boldsymbol{\mu}-\eta(\mathbf{g}-\mathcal{F}_{h/2}(\mathbf{g}))`$
% MCG update

    12: $`{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I})`$

    13:
$`{\mathbf{z}}_{t-1}\leftarrow\boldsymbol{\mu}+\frac{\sigma_{\bar{t}}^{2}\sigma_{t-1}^{2}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}`$

15: end for

16:
$`\hat{{\mathbf{x}}}\leftarrow({\mathbf{z}}_{1}-\sigma_{1}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}}_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{1};\delta_{max}))/\alpha_{1}`$

## 4 Experiments

### 4.1 Dataset

We run all the experiments on the VCTK-0.92 dataset \[[15](#bib.bib15)\]
which contains 44 hours of speech recordings spoken by 108 English
speakers with different accents. We follow the same configurations
proposed in \[[1](#bib.bib1)\] to split training and test data. We did
the evaluations at the 48 kHz and 16 kHz sampling rate. To generate
low-resolution audio, we consider two configurations of low pass filter
for downsampling: 1) the spectrogram-based method following the settings
proposed in \[[1](#bib.bib1)\] and 2) sinc filter method
from \[[16](#bib.bib16)\]. These filters all have narrow passband and
zero phase response ²² 2 The settings for the sinc filter are 128
zero-crossings and 0.962 roll-off, and we windowed it using a Kaiser
window with $`\beta\approx 14.77`$., serving as good approximations to
the ideal filter.

### 4.2 Baselines

For comparison, we choose cubic spline interpolation,
NU-Wave \[[1](#bib.bib1)\], NU-Wave 2 \[[2](#bib.bib2)\],
WSRGlow \[[17](#bib.bib17)\], and NVSR \[[18](#bib.bib18)\] as our
baselines. We used community-contributed checkpoints³³ 3
[https://github.com/mindslab-ai/nuwave/issues/18](https://github.com/mindslab-ai/nuwave/issues/18)
of NU-Wave from their official repository. We trained NU-Wave 2 using
the official implementation. We reproduced WSRGlow using an open-source
implementation⁴⁴ 4
[https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.4353123](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.4353123)
of WaveGlow \[[19](#bib.bib19)\] and trained it with original
configurations and the spectrogram-based downsampling filter. For NVSR
we use their official checkpoint. All baselines were trained on the same
training set in Section
[4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 Dataset ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ CONDITIONING AND SAMPLING IN VARIATIONAL DIFFUSION MODELS FOR SPEECH SUPER-RESOLUTION").
Table
[1](#S3.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 3 Approach ‣ CONDITIONING AND SAMPLING IN VARIATIONAL DIFFUSION MODELS FOR SPEECH SUPER-RESOLUTION")
gives a comparison of the baselines.

### 4.3 Model and training configurations

For the choice of UDM, we adopt DiffWave \[[7](#bib.bib7)\] and remove
all the condition layers. We set the channel size to 64 for the 48 kHz
model and 128 for the 16 kHz model. Both UDMs were trained on the
negative
VLB ([2](#S2.E2 "In 2.2 Variational diffusion models ‣ 2 Background ‣ CONDITIONING AND SAMPLING IN VARIATIONAL DIFFUSION MODELS FOR SPEECH SUPER-RESOLUTION"))
for 500k steps using Adam \[[20](#bib.bib20)\] with a learning rate of
0.0002. We parameterize $`p({\mathbf{x}}|{\mathbf{z}}_{1})`$ as
$`\mathcal{N}({\mathbf{z}}_{1}/\alpha_{1},\exp(-\delta_{max})\mathbf{I})`$.
We initialized $`\delta_{max}`$ and $`\delta_{min}`$ to 10 and 0,
respectively. The model weights are the exponential moving averages of
parameters with a 0.9999 momentum. We set
$`\hat{{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}}_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{t};t)=\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}}_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{\delta_{t}};\delta_{t})`$
where $`\delta_{t}`$ is the *log-signal-to-noise-ratio* scheduler. We
found that simple linear schedule
$`\delta_{t}=\frac{t-1}{T-1}\delta_{min}+\frac{T-t}{T-1}\delta_{max}`$
can generate much more consistent samples than other schedulers so we
used it in all our experiments. We evaluate three variations of our
algorithm: 1) Algorithm
[1](#algorithm1 "In 3 Approach ‣ CONDITIONING AND SAMPLING IN VARIATIONAL DIFFUSION MODELS FOR SPEECH SUPER-RESOLUTION")
with our trained UDM as noise predicotr (UDM+), 2) Algorithm
[1](#algorithm1 "In 3 Approach ‣ CONDITIONING AND SAMPLING IN VARIATIONAL DIFFUSION MODELS FOR SPEECH SUPER-RESOLUTION")
with the same noise predictor from the NU-Wave baseline (NU-Wave+), and
3) Algorithm
[1](#algorithm1 "In 3 Approach ‣ CONDITIONING AND SAMPLING IN VARIATIONAL DIFFUSION MODELS FOR SPEECH SUPER-RESOLUTION")
with the same noise predictor from the NU-Wave 2 baseline (NU-Wave 2+).
We upsample the input audio to the target resolution using the same sinc
filter from Section
[4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 Dataset ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ CONDITIONING AND SAMPLING IN VARIATIONAL DIFFUSION MODELS FOR SPEECH SUPER-RESOLUTION")
for all the variations.

### 4.4 Evaluation

We choose the commonly used LSD \[[1](#bib.bib1), [18](#bib.bib18),
[17](#bib.bib17)\] for objective evaluation and a perceptual metric
PESQ \[[21](#bib.bib21), [22](#bib.bib22)\] for subjective evaluation.
We evaluate the metrics on each utterance and report the average on the
test set. For all the diffusion based models, we use the scheduler
trained at the same operating frequency for fair comparisons. We tuned
$`\eta`$ for each evaluation setting empirically using a few utterances
from the training set. We report all the scores with $`T=50`$ unless we
mention specifically. We apply downsampling on the model outputs if the
model’s operating rate is higher than that of the experiment. We also
compute the LSD below $`h/2`$ Hz (LSD-LF) in the 48k Hz experiment using
simulated input, which is generated by adding random noise with a
variance of $`\sigma_{1}^{2}/\alpha_{1}^{2}`$ to the test samples.

## 5 Results and analysis

Table
[2](#S5.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 5 Results and analysis ‣ CONDITIONING AND SAMPLING IN VARIATIONAL DIFFUSION MODELS FOR SPEECH SUPER-RESOLUTION")
shows that our method can enhance existing diffusion baselines,
especially for NU-Wave+, which shows a 0.11 to 0.13 reduction in LSD
compared to NU-Wave. UDM+ outperforms the baselines in all the settings
except for ratio of 3 with STFT filter. We think the extra gains come
from the training objective because this is the only differences between
our model and NU-Wave besides the absence of condition layers. For the
16 kHz experiment, Table
[3](#S5.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 5 Results and analysis ‣ CONDITIONING AND SAMPLING IN VARIATIONAL DIFFUSION MODELS FOR SPEECH SUPER-RESOLUTION")
shows UDM+ achieves comparable performance to NVSR in LSD. The inferior
result in PESQ can be improved if we remove MCG. Nevertheless, removing
MCG can results in great deterioration in LSD, which can be seen in
Table
[3](#S5.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 5 Results and analysis ‣ CONDITIONING AND SAMPLING IN VARIATIONAL DIFFUSION MODELS FOR SPEECH SUPER-RESOLUTION")
and Table
[4](#S5.T4 "Table 4 ‣ 5 Results and analysis ‣ CONDITIONING AND SAMPLING IN VARIATIONAL DIFFUSION MODELS FOR SPEECH SUPER-RESOLUTION"),
especially when using fewer diffusion steps.

The robustness to different filters can also be inspected from the LSD
scores in Table
[2](#S5.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 5 Results and analysis ‣ CONDITIONING AND SAMPLING IN VARIATIONAL DIFFUSION MODELS FOR SPEECH SUPER-RESOLUTION")
and Table
[3](#S5.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 5 Results and analysis ‣ CONDITIONING AND SAMPLING IN VARIATIONAL DIFFUSION MODELS FOR SPEECH SUPER-RESOLUTION").
UDM+ and NVSR have the smallest differences across filters among all the
models. Good generalizability to different filters can be achieved
through data augmentation \[[2](#bib.bib2), [22](#bib.bib22)\] but our
method overcomes this by disentangling the filter conditions from
training. NVSR uses reconstructed mel-spectrograms to generate
high-resolution audio, and this low-dimensional representation can blur
out the characteristics of the low pass filter. The same robustness
cannot be achieved if the noise predictor was trained with condition as
auxiliary input, as shown in the results from NU-Wave+/NU-Wave 2+.

Finally, the LSD-LF we obtain for ratio of 2 and 3 are 0.056 and 0.052,
respectively, which are roughly six times smaller than the numbers
reported from NU-Wave 2 \[[2](#bib.bib2)\], demonstrating its ability to
better retain low frequencies. In addition, we found our UDMs performed
poorly on unconditional generation. Because our method depends on the
quality of prior models, there is still room for improvement.

## 6 Conclusion

We presented a conditional sampling algorithm for SR that can be
flexibly coupled with existing audio DMs. Our experiments on the 48 kHz
SR benchmark showed that our conditional sampling with MCG improved the
performance of previous conditional audio DMs and achieve
state-of-the-art when combined with a UDM, which was trained on the
unbiased diffusion loss. We also demonstrated the robustness of our
method against different zero-phase downsampling filters.
````
