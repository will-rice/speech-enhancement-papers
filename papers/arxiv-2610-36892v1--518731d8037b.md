###### Abstract

Speech enhancement (SE) systems are commonly evaluated with objective
metrics, with and without references, and with categorical subjective
listening tests, all measuring speech quality and intelligibility. So
far, it is unclear from such evaluations whether the processed speech is
perceptually different or is preferred by everyday listeners. We
investigate discrimination and preference as complementary dimensions of
SE evaluation through crowdsourced triangle and pairwise preference
tests across four SE systems, with comparisons to clean references.
Results show that both listeners’ ability to distinguish and whether
they exhibit systematic preference vary across systems and substantially
across acoustic conditions. Pairwise evaluations of objective metrics
showed variable correlation with both discriminability and preference,
motivating further studies into these novel aspects in speech
enhancement evaluation.

## 1 Introduction

Distinguishing speech from noise in recordings from authentic
environments is a fundamental problem in spoken language processing.
This challenging inverse problem has long-standing traditions in
statistical signal processing, estimating speech and noise model
parameters to infer clean speech from noisy observations \[1, 2\]. The
paradigm has now transitioned to learning-based studies with multiple
underlying methods, which are generally categorised into discriminative
\[17, 10\] and generative \[14, 16\] approaches. However, the goal
remains the same: infer clean speech from noisy observations.

The ”gold standard” for evaluating perceptual quality of speech
enhancement is human listening tests, with established ITU-T p.800
methods including absolute (ACR), degradation (DCR), and comparative
(CCR) categorical quality assessment \[6, 7, 8\]. ACR and DCR are rated
on a five-point scale. CCR is rated on a seven-point scale. Results are
reported as mean opinion-score (MOS, DMOS, and CMOS, respectively)
\[6\]. ITU-T P. 808 specifies how to crowdsource these experiments
\[8\]. The ITU-T P.835 standard prescribes a method for conducting
subjective evaluation of speech enhancement methods, using ACR; it asks
listeners to rate the enhanced speech, attending to the speech signal,
background, and overall quality, respectively \[7\]. Because subjective
evaluation is expensive, SE benchmarks also rely heavily on objective
measures of signal fidelity, intelligibility, and perceptual quality.
Popular examples include SI-SDR \[12\], STOI \[28\] and ESTOI \[9\],
composite predictors of the P.835 quality dimensions \[3\], and PESQ
\[25\]. These measures are all intrusive, requiring access to a clean
reference signal, which is generally unavailable for real-world
recordings. This has motivated the development of non-intrusive
speech-quality measures \[26, 23, 24, 18, 27, 21\], which estimate
perceptual quality directly from the observed signal. In parallel,
reference-free models such as SQUIM \[11\] estimate established
objective measures, including STOI, PESQ, and SI-SDR, without access to
the corresponding clean speech signal.

SE benchmarks are commonly summarised by corpus-level means or medians
of objective or subjective scores, with differences between systems
interpreted as evidence of improved performance. However, differences on
these scales show separation according to that measure, but do not
necessarily mean listeners can distinguish the related signals.
Furtermore, it is unclear if metric separation or distinguishability
corresponds to a systematic preference from everyday listeners. These
distinction become important as high-performing SE systems achieve
increasingly similar scores on selected benchmarks. The URGENT
challenges \[15\] results have highlighted discrepancies between
objective and subjective SE evaluation \[15\], while recent work has
moved toward comparative evaluation, including pairwise objective
quality assessment \[31, 32\]. ITU-T P.800 and P.808 already describe
pairwise evaluation with CCR, in which listeners jointly indicate the
direction and magnitude of a perceived quality difference on a
seven-point scale \[6, 8\]. However, the magnitude of such differences
may become small for high-performing SE systems, as recent CCR
evaluations also observe \[15\].

This work introduces discriminability and preference as complementary
dimensions to SE evaluation. First, an evaluation framework is adopted
that evaluates these new dimensions through subjective listening tests.
Second, a crowdsourced evaluation of four high-performing contemporary
SE systems is conducted, characterising discrimination and preference
across system-pairs and speech material. Finally, it is investigated
whether difference scores of a range of objective metrics can predict
these new SE dimensions. The web-based listening-test framework and
experimental material are made publicly available to facilitate
reproducible evaluation of future SE systems¹¹ 1 Code published upon
acceptance..

## 2 Methods

This study enforces a paired comparison framework in contrast to most
current SE evaluations. This means two systems are compared using
utterance-wise metric differences as,

```math
\Delta m_{u,A,B}=m(x_{A})-m(x_{B}),
```

where $`m`$ is the score of an objective metric, $`u`$ is the utterance
index, and $`A,B`$ denote the compared SE systems. This approach aligns
with recent efforts \[30\] and allows comparison with paired subjective
evaluation. The magnitude of difference is here described as the
absolute metric difference

```math
D^{(m)}_{u,A,B}=|\Delta m_{u,A,B}|=|m(x_{A})-m(x_{B})|. \tag{1}
```

### 2.1 Discrimination Testing

Detecting whether the same utterance processed by different SE
algorithms is perceptually different is infeasible using the cited ITU-T
standards, since the experimental variable (speech quality) differs
fundamentally. It is therefore proposed to adopt a method from
discrimination testing in sensory evaluation. The ISO 4120 specifies the
procedure for the triangle test of perceptual difference \[5\].
Participants are presented with three stimuli of the same utterance
processed by two different SE systems $`A,B`$. Two stimuli are
identical; one differs (positions are randomised across six possible
combinations, e.g. $`A,A,B`$). The listener should identify the odd one
out, making an arbitrary guess if uncertain.

```math
Y^{D}_{i,u,A,B}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{correct},\\
0,&\text{incorrect},\end{cases}\Rightarrow Y^{D}_{i,u,A,B}\sim\text{Bernoulli}(p^{D}_{u,A,B}),
```

where $`i`$ is the listener index, $`u`$ is the utterance index, with
chance probability $`p_{0}=\frac{1}{3}`$. Repeated judgements follow a
binomial distribution,
$`k_{u,A,B}\sim\text{Binomial}(n_{u,A,B},p_{u,A,B}).`$

Statistical significance is determined according to the standard \[5\],
using an exact one-sided binomial test of the alternative hypothesis,
$`H_{A}:p_{A}>\frac{1}{3}`$. A secondary analysis investigates whether
the paired objective metrics predict the discriminability rate from the
listening test. Objective metrics provide scalar estimates of quality
and intelligibility, whereas the triangle test measures perceptual
discrimination; to test whether pairwise metric separation is associated
with perceptual separation, the Spearman Rank Correlation Coefficient
(SRCC) is calculated from the estimate of discrimination rate:

```math
\hat{p}^{D}_{u,A,B}=\frac{k_{u,A,B}}{n_{u,A,B}}, \tag{2}
```

and the correlation is then $`\rho_{S}(\hat{p}^{D},D^{(m)})`$.
System-pair discrimination is summarised by the unweighted
utterance-level estimates,

```math
\overline{p}^{D}_{A,B}=\frac{1}{U_{A,B}}\sum_{u=1}^{U_{A,B}}\hat{p}^{D}_{u,A,B} \tag{3}
```

with $`95\%`$ confidence interval (CI) obtained by bootstrap resampling
over utterances. The relationship between the objective metric
difference and the estimated discriminability rate is modelled by a
psychometric function adopted from \[33\],

```math
P(Y^{D}=1|D^{(m)})=\frac{1}{3}+\frac{2}{3}\sigma(\alpha_{m}+D^{(m)}\beta_{m}). \tag{4}
```

The asymptote at $`\frac{1}{3}`$ is given by the chance rate of the
triangle test, the function $`\sigma(\cdot)`$ is the logistic function,
the parameters $`\alpha_{m}`$ and $`\beta_{m}`$ are estimated by maximum
likelihood, and represent the intercept and slope of the curve.

### 2.2 Preference Testing

Two stimuli ($`A,B`$) of the same utterance processed by different SE
systems are presented to a listener. The listener is asked ”Listen to
both recordings, then choose which you would prefer for everyday
listening. Select “No preference” when neither is preferred”. This is
the paired comparison paradigm commonly referred to as A/B/N \[20\]. The
inclusion of no preference is in opposition to ISO 5495 \[4\] for A/B,
and its effects are still a debated research question \[19\]. However,
the ”tie” option tends to yield higher precision \[20, 13\]. The ”no
preference” category allows perceptually similar conditions to be
treated as preference-equivalent and thus distinguished from genuine
disagreement. To this end, decisiveness is defined from the response
outcomes $`R\in\{A,B,N\}`$ as
$`Y^{\mathrm{dec}}_{u,A,B}=\mathbb{I}\{R\in\{A,B\}\}`$. Decisiveness is
estimated empirically from the relation

```math
\hat{p}^{\mathrm{dec}}_{u,A,B}=\frac{N_{A}+N_{B}}{N_{A}+N_{B}+N_{N}}, \tag{5}
```

and estimates the probability that listeners express a preference for
either system under evaluation. The SRCC between the discrimination rate
and decisiveness is calculated as
$`\rho_{S}(\hat{p}^{D},\hat{p}^{dec})`$, since it is expected that no
preference is expressed when two signals are indistinguishable. To
evaluate the direction of preference, a signed preference variable is
defined as:

```math
Y^{\mathrm{pref}}_{u,A,B}=\begin{cases}+1,&R=A,\\
0,&R=N,\\
-1,&R=B.\end{cases} \tag{6}
```

The mean preference direction is then
$`S_{A,B}=\mathbb{E}[Y^{\mathrm{pref}}]=P(R=A)-P(R=B)`$, and is
estimated empirically from

```math
\hat{S}_{A,B}=\frac{N_{A}-N_{B}}{N_{A}+N_{B}+N_{N}},\qquad\hat{S}_{A,B}\in[-1;1],
```

where positive and negative scores indicate a preference for system A or
B, respectively. The magnitude of this score represents both the
agreement and decisiveness frequency. Both decisiveness and preference
direction are modelled as functions of $`D^{(m)}`$ and $`\Delta m`$
respectively. Decisiveness is modelled similarly to discrimination, but
without the chance floor, and preference is modelled as a bidirectional
logistic function,

```math
\displaystyle P(Y^{dec}|D^{(m)}) \displaystyle=\sigma(\alpha^{dec}_{m}+\beta^{dec}_{m}D^{(m)}), \tag{7}
```

```math
\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[Y^{\mathrm{pref}}|\Delta{m}\right] \displaystyle=2\sigma(\alpha_{m}^{\mathrm{pref}}+\beta_{m}^{\mathrm{pref}}\Delta{m})-1. \tag{8}
```

## 3 Experimental Setup

The proposed methods are used to evaluate four state-of-the-art SE
systems: the discriminative models MP-SEnet ²² 2
https://github.com/yxlu-0102/MP-SENet.git (MP)\[17\] and MambAttention
³³ 3 https://github.com/NikolaiKyhne/MambAttention.git (MA)\[10\]; the
generative model MeanFlowSE ⁴⁴ 4
https://github.com/liduojia1/MeanFlowSE.git (MF)\[16\]; and the hybrid
StoRM ⁵⁵ 5 https://github.com/sp-uhh/storm.git (Sto)\[14\]. All models
are trained on the VoiceBank+DEMAND dataset \[29\], and inference is
performed using publicly available checkpoints. The trained models
enhance a selection of 45 utterances randomly sampled from the DNS2020
test set \[22\]. Each utterance is truncated to 4 seconds, and the model
outputs are loudness normalised⁶⁶ 6
https://github.com/csteinmetz1/pyloudnorm. For each of the four chosen
SE systems, a wide selection of both intrusive and non-intrusive metrics
is calculated \[25, 9, 12, 3, 18, 24, 27, 21, 11, 26\]. Using an
incomplete pairwise design, all four systems are paired with each other
and the clean references of the DNS dataset, giving 10 total
system-pairs. For each utterance, 3 unique system-pairs are evaluated,
while balancing the occurrence of each system and system-pair across the
experiment, giving 135 utterance/system-pair conditions. Each
participant completes 30 trials in both the discrimination and
preference blocks. Every trial evaluates a single utterance/pair
condition. A schedule is derived for each session such that the same
utterance is only presented once per block and, when feasible, the same
utterance/system-pair is not presented to a participant in both blocks,
to minimise familiarisation effects. The presentation order in the
triangle trials is randomised and balanced to minimise the effects of
systematic guessing patterns. The presentation order of the blocks is
randomised to balance the familiarisation and fatigue effects. 92
subjects without any known hearing impairment volunteered to participate
anonymously. A headphone screening was performed; failing participants
were excluded and are not included in the total number of subjects.

## 4 Results

### 4.1 Triangle Test

Figure 1: Triangle test discrimination rate across system pairs. Each

The triangle test results show that listeners reliably distinguished the
processed samples from one another and from the clean references. The
estimated discrimination rate across all responses was
$`\hat{p}^{D}=\frac{k}{n}=\frac{1775}{2541}\approx 0.7`$ which is
significantly above chance. Fig. 1 shows that discriminability varies
across system pairs, where the mean utterance-level discrimination rate
($`\overline{p}^{D}_{A,B}`$) is also above chance for every system pair,
with the StoRM (Sto) model vs clean references being the most
discriminable, with $`\overline{p}^{D}_{\textrm{Sto,cle}}=0.83`$.
Conversely, the MambAttention vs MeanFlow models have the lowest mean
utterance-level discrimination rate at
$`\overline{p}^{D}_{\textrm{MA,MF}}=0.56`$. However, inter-pair
comparability is limited in this experimental setup because each system
pair is evaluated on different utterances.

This point is further emphasised by evaluating the utterance-level
discrimination rate, which is shown in Figure 2. Considerable variation
is observed across utterances, with mean discrimination across system
pairs ranging from 0.34 to 1.0. This suggests that perceptual
differences between SE systems depend on the properties of the acoustic
evidence, which the triangle test itself is not able to identify.

Although some of the variation is expected from the measurement noise
introduced by the finite number of evaluations per condition, these
results motivate the inclusion of utterance-wise paired evaluation, also
when considering objective metrics, since system-level averages could
hide perceptually important differences for individual speech signals.
The absolute metric differences $`D^{(m)}_{u,A,B}`$ are evaluated for
all selected utterances, and the SRCC is calculated relative to the
empirical discrimination rate of those utterances. The results are shown
in Table 1. The correlation varies considerably across metrics, with the
Torchaudio-Squim predictor of the STOI metric showing the strongest
correlation with discrimination ($`\rho_{S}=0.62`$); other non-intrusive
methods such as Score-Q and DistillMOS also show comparable correlation
(0.59 and 0.51, respectively). Despite its widespread use in
speech-enhancement benchmarking, PESQ showed almost no correlation with
perceptual discriminability ($`\rho_{S}=0.05`$). The chance-constrained
psychometric model (4) is fitted with maximum likelihood, as shown in
Fig. 3 for PESQ and $`\mathrm{SQUIM}_{\mathrm{STOI}}`$. These results
indicate that perceptual difference is an underrepresented factor in SE
evaluation.

### 4.2 Preference and Decisiveness

Figure 4: Decisiveness of the preference test across system pairs. Each
open dot is the empirically estimated decisiveness rate of an
utterance/system-pair condition as in 5. The filled dots represent the
average across utterances. The error bars represent the $`95\%`$ CI by

To analyse the preference experiment, an account is made towards the
inclusion of a No Preference option. Responses were analysed in terms of
decisiveness, $`\hat{p}^{\mathrm{dec}}_{u,A,B}`$, describing the
proportion of trials in which listeners expressed a preference for
either system, and preference direction, $`\hat{S}_{u,A,B}`$, describing
which system was preferred, with no-preference responses as zero-valued
observations. Decisiveness differs from discrimination, because two
processed signals can be perceptually different without listeners having
a preference. Across all trials, listeners expressed a preference for
either system in $`68\%`$ of the trials. Slightly less than the global
discriminability. The decisiveness is evaluated empirically across
system pairs as the utterance-level averages of equation 5. Fig. 4 shows
the results. Decisiveness also varies across system pairs: MF vs clean
reference shows the highest average decisiveness, followed by StoRM vs
clean, with mean utterance-level decisiveness rates of 0.84 and 0.79,
respectively. MeanFlow vs StoRM is the least decisive pair, with a rate
of 0.51. All system pairs show considerable utterance-level variation,
indicating that decisiveness depends on the acoustic evidence. These
results are interpreted similarly to the discrimination results in Fig.

### 4.3 Limitations

The study has shown that discriminability and preference are
utterance-dependent; however, some of the observed variations might also
be attributed to other factors. As mentioned, a limited number of
evaluations per trial can introduce variation due to measurement noise.
Measurement noise could here originate from uncontrollable parameters
inherent in the crowdsourcing approach. Different playback devices,
individual sound pressure, listener attention, miss-hits, background
noise, and language proficiency/background are unmeasured variations in
this study that could introduce uncontrolled variability. Because the
evaluated samples in this experiment come from a different corpus than
the models were trained on, they likely introduced utterance-dependent
variability due to mismatched acoustic conditions. The choice of an
incomplete pairwise experimental design allowed a broader representation
of acoustic conditions, but limits inter-pair comparability, and
utterance-wise averages should be compared descriptively. All reported
metrics measure factors other than perceptual similarity or preference,
and intuitively, two signals can be rated as similar in quality yet
still be easily distinguished.

## 5 Conclusion

Speech enhancement evaluation often reduces system differences to
objective or subjective quality metrics. Our results show that systems
with similar objective scores can remain perceptually distinguishable,
and that discriminability is not necessarily the same as having a
systematic listener preference. Additionally, most objective metrics
showed poor correlation with both discriminability and preference,
Future work should focus on substantiating this study with
laboratory-setting controlled listening experiments, with more repeated
judgements and direct comparison with ITU standards for subjective
listening tests. Probabilistic choice models could be used to account
for listener and stimulus variations and provide a framework for
comparing models and ensuring transitivity.
