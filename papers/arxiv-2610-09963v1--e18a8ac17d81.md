# LIFT-SE: Linguistic Inference Followed by Flow Transformation for Generative Speech Enhancement

Haoyin Yan    Chengwei Liu    Zheng Xue    Xiaotao Liang    Jifa Cai   
Zeyu Zhao    Jingjing Wang

###### Abstract

Generative speech enhancement (SE) is prone to linguistic hallucination
when semantic constraint is unreliable under severe noise and
reverberation. Moreover, approaches that generate discrete codec tokens
are bounded by the quantization error of the codec decoder, regardless
of token-prediction accuracy. We propose LIFT-SE, a two-stage generative
framework that decouples linguistic inference from acoustic synthesis
within QRes-Codec, which exposes a quantized latent and its
residual-completed continuous form. The first stage predicts clean codec
tokens autoregressively, conditioned on frame-aligned features from a
self-supervised front-end distilled toward clean speech. The second
stage applies conditional flow matching to transport Gaussian noise to
the continuous latent conditioned on the predicted tokens, and the
frozen decoder reconstructs the enhanced waveform. Discrete generation
provides naturalness, while continuous refinement restores signal
fidelity. Experiments on the DNS1 and URGENT benchmarks show that
LIFT-SE attains favorable linguistic consistency under reverberant
conditions together with competitive perceptual quality, and systematic
ablations verify the necessity of both stages. Code will be released in
the future.

## 1 Introduction

Speech enhancement (SE) aims to recover clean speech from noisy
mixtures, improving perceptual quality and intelligibility for both
human listeners and downstream applications such as automatic speech
recognition (ASR) \[1\] and speaker verification \[2\].
Deep-learning-based SE methods can be broadly grouped into
discriminative approaches \[3, 4\], which estimate clean speech or its
representations from noisy input, and generative approaches, which
synthesize enhanced speech from learned speech priors. The latter
include diffusion models \[5, 6\], flow matching models \[7, 8\], and
language-model (LM)-based approaches \[9, 10, 11\]. Generative SE can
produce highly natural-sounding speech, but this capability entails a
fundamental risk: perceptual plausibility does not guarantee content
faithfulness, i.e., linguistic hallucination \[12, 13\].

Achieving content faithfulness becomes particularly challenging when
noise or reverberation obscures the acoustic evidence for the spoken
words. Many LM-based SE systems generate discrete speech tokens, such as
codec tokens, conditioned on representations of the degraded signal
\[14, 11\]. When the conditioning is corrupted by severe noise or
reverberation, the autoregressive prior dominates and the model produces
fluent yet linguistically incorrect content \[15\]. Continuous
generative models similarly drift toward plausible but unfaithful speech
in the absence of an explicit semantic anchor \[7, 5\]. Existing work
strengthens semantic guidance through noise-invariant representations:
L3-SE distills noisy-speech representations toward clean acoustic and
semantic teachers and reports strong linguistic consistency under
reverberant conditions \[15\]; PASE and StuPASE instead constrain
generation in the continuous domain with a distilled
self-supervised-learning (SSL) phonological prior \[13, 8\]. However,
the discrete modeling of L3-SE leaves reconstruction quality bounded by
the quantization loss of the codec decoder, whereas the
continuous-representation modeling of PASE and StuPASE falls short of
autoregressive generation in naturalness. SenSE combines discrete token
generation with flow-based waveform synthesis, but its token predictions
and flow targets use different representations, requiring a mapping
between the two stages \[16\]. These observations motivate a framework
that performs discrete linguistic inference and continuous acoustic
refinement over a shared underlying speech representation.

We therefore propose LIFT-SE, a two-stage generative SE framework that
decouples linguistic inference from acoustic synthesis. LIFT-SE is built
on QRes-Codec, which exposes a quantized latent $`z_{q}`$ for discrete
prediction and its residual-completed continuous form
$`z_{q}+z_{\mathrm{res}}`$ for high-fidelity reconstruction. Concretely,
the first stage is a conditional autoregressive (AR) model that predicts
clean discrete codec tokens, conditioned on frame-aligned features from
an SSL front-end and a frozen codec encoder that are injected as
prepended key–value pairs at every decoder layer. The SSL front-end is
adapted with low-rank adaptation (LoRA) and distilled toward the
clean-speech representations of the same frozen backbone, which thus
serves as its own teacher. The second stage is a conditional flow
matching (CFM) refiner \[17\] that transports Gaussian noise to the
continuous latent of QRes-Codec, conditioned on the AR-predicted tokens;
the frozen codec decoder then reconstructs the enhanced waveform.

Our contributions are as follows. First, we present QRes-Codec, in which
discrete prediction and continuous refinement share a single
representation space, eliminating the cross-representation conversion
required when tokens and flow targets come from different
representations. Second, we design a two-stage framework in which
discrete autoregressive generation provides naturalness and an explicit
causal decision over linguistic content, while continuous refinement
recovers the information that quantization discards, lifting restoration
quality beyond the fidelity of the discrete path. Third, on the DNS1 and
URGENT benchmarks, LIFT-SE compares favorably with strong generative
baselines under reverberant conditions while maintaining competitive
perceptual quality. Removing either stage degrades the result,
indicating that the two stages are complementary.

## 2 Proposed method

### 2.1 Overview

Figure 1 presents LIFT-SE, which enhances a noisy waveform $`x`$ in two
stages that share a frozen QRes-Codec. QRes-Codec represents speech at a
frame rate of $`50`$ Hz as a discrete token sequence
$`\mathbf{c}=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{T})`$ and a continuous latent sequence.
Let $`\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times 32}`$ denote the dequantization
table, where $`K=4096`$ is the codebook size. A table lookup maps tokens
to quantized latents, $`z_{q}=\mathbf{Q}[\mathbf{c}]`$, allowing both
stages to operate on representations defined by the same codec.

Stage 1 predicts clean codec tokens autoregressively from the noisy
input and obtains $`z_{q}`$ by table lookup. Stage 2 uses conditional
flow matching to estimate the target continuous latent
$`z=z_{q}+z_{\mathrm{res}}`$, where $`z_{\mathrm{res}}`$ restores
information omitted by quantization. The frozen codec decoder converts
the refined latent $`\hat{z}`$ into the enhanced waveform $`\hat{x}`$.
QRes-Codec and the self-supervised backbone (w2v-BERT-2.0) \[18\] remain
frozen, whereas the LoRA adapters, AR model, and CFM refiner are
trained.

### 2.2 QRes-Codec with semantic distillation

Inspired by the homogeneous Transformer autoencoder design of
MOSS-Audio-Tokenizer \[19\], QRes-Codec (Figure 2) processes $`16`$-kHz
waveforms using non-causal Transformer blocks. The waveform is divided
into $`320`$-sample patches, yielding a frame rate of $`50`$ Hz; both
the encoder and decoder contain $`20`$ Transformer layers with a hidden
dimension of $`768`$. Two projection heads each map the encoder output
to a $`32`$-dimensional latent. The first head produces
$`z_{\mathrm{vq}}`$, which is quantized with finite scalar quantization
(FSQ) \[20\] using levels $`[4,4,4,4,4,4]`$. The resulting single
codebook contains $`4^{6}=4096`$ entries and has a bit rate of $`600`$
bps. The second head produces the residual $`z_{\mathrm{res}}`$. A
single decoder reconstructs speech from $`z_{q}`$ in the discrete path
and from $`z_{q}+z_{\mathrm{res}}`$ in the continuous path.

Figure 2: The frozen QRes-Codec provides a quantized latent $`z_{q}`$
for discrete prediction and a continuous latent
$`z_{q}+z_{\mathrm{res}}`$ for high-fidelity reconstruction. Semantic
distillation is applied to the quantized branch.

QRes-Codec is trained independently and then frozen for speech
enhancement. Its objective combines reconstruction, residual, semantic,
adversarial, and feature-matching terms:

```math
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{codec}}={} \displaystyle 10\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{mel}}^{q}+10\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{mel}}^{q+r}+10\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{res}}+5\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sem}}
```

```math
\displaystyle+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{adv}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fm}}, \tag{1}
```

where $`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{mel}}^{q}`$ and
$`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{mel}}^{q+r}`$ denote multi-resolution log-mel
losses for the discrete and continuous paths, respectively. During
training, the quantized latent is detached in the continuous path, and
$`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{res}}=\lVert z_{\mathrm{res}}\rVert_{2}^{2}`$
encourages the codes to retain the principal information. Multi-period
and multi-resolution discriminators provide
$`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{adv}}`$ and $`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fm}}`$.
Following semantic codec training \[21\], a lightweight semantic module
built on ConvNeXt \[22\] maps $`z_{q}`$ to the layer-17 representation
of a frozen w2v-BERT-2.0 model \[18\]; the mean-squared error defines
$`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sem}}`$. This loss encourages the quantized
representation to retain linguistic information. Gaussian noise with a
standard deviation of $`0.05`$ is added only to
$`z_{q}+z_{\mathrm{res}}`$ during codec training, improving the
robustness of the downstream refiner.

### 2.3 Stage 1: conditional autoregressive token prediction

Conditioning front-end. We extract features from noisy speech $`x`$
using w2v-BERT-2.0, whose pretrained weights are frozen. Each of its
$`25`$ hidden states is normalized by its own precomputed
root-mean-square constant, and the normalized states are combined using
learned softmax weights. The result is concatenated with the
frame-aligned representation from the frozen codec encoder and projected
to a sequence of fusion tokens $`h\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times 1024}`$.
Rank-$`16`$ LoRA adapters are added to the query, key, value, and output
projections of each w2v-BERT-2.0 attention layer.

The LoRA adapters learn noise-invariant conditioning by aligning
noisy-speech features with the clean-speech features of the same frozen
backbone, following the noisy-to-clean representation distillation in
PASE \[13\]. Let $`f_{\mathrm{L}}`$ denote the backbone with LoRA
enabled and $`f_{0}`$ the backbone with LoRA disabled. The latter is
evaluated without gradients and serves as the teacher:

```math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ssl}}=\left\lVert\mathrm{LN}\!\left(f_{\mathrm{L}}(x)\right)-\mathrm{LN}\!\left(f_{0}(x^{\mathrm{clean}})\right)\right\rVert_{2}^{2}. \tag{2}
```

Here, $`\mathrm{LN}(\cdot)`$ denotes layer normalization. Thus, the
teacher exists but requires no additional training.

Autoregressive model. The AR model is a decoder-only Transformer that
follows the Qwen2 architecture \[23\]. It contains $`12`$ layers with a
hidden dimension of $`768`$, $`12`$ attention heads, an intermediate
dimension of $`3072`$, and a vocabulary of $`4096`$ codec tokens. No
end-of-sequence token is needed because the output length equals the
conditioning length. The model factorizes the clean token sequence as

```math
p_{\theta}(\mathbf{c}\mid h)=\prod_{t=1}^{T}p_{\theta}(c_{t}\mid c_{<t},h). \tag{3}
```

Frame-aligned conditioning. At each decoder layer, an independent
projection maps the fusion tokens $`h`$ to conditional key and value
vectors, which are prepended to the keys and values derived from the
AR-token states. After attention, outputs at the fusion-token positions
are discarded, and only the outputs at the AR-token positions are
propagated to the next layer. The fusion tokens and AR tokens share
rotary indices $`0,\ldots,T-1`$; consequently, the two tokens at the
same frame have zero relative rotary distance, independent of $`T`$.
This framewise alignment allows inference to accommodate input audio of
arbitrary duration.

Objective and inference. Stage 1 is trained with teacher forcing under

```math
\mathcal{L}_{1}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ssl}}, \tag{4}
```

where $`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}`$ is the token cross-entropy. At
inference, greedy generation produces $`T`$ tokens and the
dequantization table maps them to $`z_{q}`$.

### 2.4 Stage 2: conditional flow matching refinement

Stage 2 refines $`z_{q}`$ in the continuous codec representation rather
than in a mel-spectrogram space. The $`32`$-dimensional codec latent is
lower-dimensional than typical mel-spectrogram representations, and it
can be decoded directly without a separately trained vocoder. Let
$`s_{1}=z=z_{q}^{\mathrm{clean}}+z_{\mathrm{res}}^{\mathrm{clean}}`$ be
the target continuous latent, where the superscript $`\mathrm{clean}`$
marks latents obtained from the corresponding clean speech, and let
$`s_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)`$ be the noise endpoint. For flow time
$`\tau\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1)`$, we use the optimal-transport path \[17\]

```math
\phi_{\tau}=(1-\tau)s_{0}+\tau s_{1},\qquad u_{\tau}=s_{1}-s_{0}, \tag{5}
```

and train the velocity field with

```math
\mathcal{L}_{2}=\mathbb{E}_{s_{0},s_{1},\tau}\left[\left\lVert v_{\theta}(\phi_{\tau},\tau\mid h,z_{q})-u_{\tau}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right]. \tag{6}
```

Here, $`z_{q}`$ is obtained from Stage 1, and the fusion tokens $`h`$
provide the noisy-speech evidence used by both stages.

The refiner consists of eight bidirectional Transformer blocks with a
hidden dimension of $`512`$, eight attention heads, and an intermediate
dimension of $`2048`$. Its condition is

```math
g=W_{h}h+W_{q}z_{q}. \tag{7}
```

The condition $`g`$ and $`W_{x}\phi_{\tau}`$ are concatenated along the
time axis, and the two segments use the same frame indices for rotary
position encoding. Here, $`W_{h}`$, $`W_{q}`$, and $`W_{x}`$ are
learnable linear projections that map the fusion tokens $`h`$, quantized
latent $`z_{q}`$, and interpolated latent $`\phi_{\tau}`$, respectively,
to the refiner hidden dimension. The velocity prediction head is applied
only to the outputs corresponding to the latent segment. A sinusoidal
embedding of $`\tau`$ modulates every Transformer block through adaptive
layer normalization \[24\]. During training, the entire condition is
dropped with probability $`0.2`$ to enable classifier-free guidance
\[25\].

At inference, Stage 1 first generates $`\hat{\mathbf{c}}`$ greedily, and
the dequantization table yields $`z_{q}=\mathbf{Q}[\hat{\mathbf{c}}]`$.
Starting from $`s^{(0)}=s_{0}`$, Euler integration updates

```math
s^{(i+1)}=s^{(i)}+\Delta\tau_{i}\left[v_{\mathrm{c}}+\gamma\left(v_{\mathrm{c}}-v_{\mathrm{u}}\right)\right], \tag{8}
```

where $`v_{\mathrm{c}}`$ and $`v_{\mathrm{u}}`$ are the velocities
predicted with and without the condition, respectively, $`\gamma`$ is
the guidance strength, and $`\Delta\tau_{i}`$ is the step size of the
$`i`$-th integration step. The final latent $`\hat{z}`$ is passed to the
frozen codec decoder to obtain $`\hat{x}`$.

## 3 Experiments

### 3.1 Datasets

QRes-Codec is trained on VoxBox \[26\], which contains approximately
$`100{,}000`$ hours of speech. For SE training, clean speech is drawn
from Multilingual LibriSpeech \[27\] and Emilia \[28\], about
$`3{,}000`$ hours in total, and the noise collection, about $`330`$
hours in total, comprises the DNS Challenge noise set \[29\], DESED
\[30\], DISCO \[31\], WHAM! \[32\], FSD50K \[33\], ESC-50 \[34\], DEMAND
\[35\], MUSAN \[36\], and TUT Urban Acoustic Scenes 2018 \[37\]. All
training mixtures are generated online at $`16`$ kHz. We apply a
simulated room impulse response (RIR) from SLR28 with probability
$`0.5`$; the corresponding target is generated with the same RIR
truncated $`1`$ ms after its direct-path peak. Noise is added with
probability $`0.8`$ at an SNR sampled uniformly from $`[-5,15]`$ dB,
after which the mixture is normalized to a level sampled from
$`[-30,-15]`$ dB. We add three further distortions to the simulation to
obtain a universal enhancement version, LIFT-SE-U: bandwidth limitation
with probability $`0.2`$, which band-limits the signal to a sampling
rate drawn from $`\{3,4,6,8,11.025,12,16\}`$ kHz; clipping with
probability $`0.2`$, with the lower and upper thresholds set at sample
quantiles drawn from $`[0,0.1]`$ and $`[0.9,1]`$; and packet loss with
probability $`0.15`$, which drops $`20`$ ms packets at a loss rate drawn
from $`[0.05,0.25]`$.

QRes-Codec is evaluated on the full LibriSpeech test-clean split \[38\]
($`2620`$ utterances) as a clean-speech reconstruction task without
simulated degradation. SE is evaluated on the synthetic DNS1 test set
\[29\], which contains a no-reverb and a with-reverb subset of $`150`$
utterances each. For with-reverb, the reference is the anechoic clean
speech, so the evaluation covers dereverberation as well as denoising.
LIFT-SE-U is additionally evaluated on the non-blind test set of the
Interspeech 2025 URGENT challenge \[39\], which contains $`1000`$
multilingual utterances degraded by up to five of seven distortion
types: additive noise, reverberation, clipping, bandwidth limitation,
codec distortion, packet loss, and wind noise. The input sampling rates
range from $`8`$ to $`48`$ kHz and are uniformly resampled to $`16`$ kHz
for processing.

### 3.3 Evaluation metrics

We assess the outputs from four perspectives.

- •
  Perceptual quality: DNSMOS P.835 \[47\] and UTMOS \[48\], which
  predict perceived quality without a reference. We report the overall
  (OVRL) score of DNSMOS; because DNSMOS is sensitive to signal level,
  each enhanced output is scaled to the root-mean-square level of its
  noisy input before scoring.
- •
  Signal fidelity: wideband PESQ \[49\] and STOI \[50\], which compare
  the output with the reference waveform.
- •
  Linguistic consistency: SpeechBERTScore (SBS) \[51\], Levenshtein
  phoneme similarity (LPS) \[12\], and word error rate, where SBS and
  LPS follow the URGENT 2025 challenge implementation \[39\]. For
  QRes-Codec, WER is computed against the ground-truth text with a
  HuBERT-Large model.¹¹ 1
  [https://huggingface.co/facebook/hubert-large-ls960-ft](https://huggingface.co/facebook/hubert-large-ls960-ft)
  DNS1 provides no transcripts, so for SE we report the differential WER
  (dWER) between the Whisper large-v3 \[52\] transcripts of the enhanced
  speech and of the clean reference. For URGENT 2025, we report the WER
  between the transcripts of the output speech and the reference text.
- •
  Speaker preservation: speaker similarity (SIM), the cosine similarity
  between speaker embeddings of the output and the reference, extracted
  by a speaker verification model based on WavLM-Large features
  \[53\].²² 2
  [https://github.com/microsoft/UniSpeech/tree/main/downstreams/speaker_verification](https://github.com/microsoft/UniSpeech/tree/main/downstreams/speaker_verification)

For QRes-Codec, we report PESQ, STOI, UTMOS, SIM, and WER; for SE, we
report DNSMOS, UTMOS, SBS, LPS, SIM, dWER, and WER. WER and dWER are
reported as percentages. Higher is better for all metrics except WER and
dWER.

### 3.4 Implementation details

QRes-Codec contains approximately $`307`$M parameters and is trained
with AdamW using a learning rate of $`1.8\times 10^{-4}`$ for both the
generator and the discriminators and gradient clipping at $`5.0`$; it
remains frozen in all SE experiments. The $`580`$M-parameter
w2v-BERT 2.0 backbone is also frozen, while rank-$`16`$ LoRA adapters
with $`\alpha=32`$ and dropout $`0.05`$ are applied to the query, key,
value, and output projections of every attention layer, adding
approximately $`3.1`$M trainable parameters. The $`140`$M-parameter AR
model is trained for $`80`$k updates using AdamW with a peak learning
rate of $`3\times 10^{-4}`$. The $`49.5`$M-parameter CFM refiner is
trained for $`50`$k updates with the same optimizer settings while
Stage 1 is frozen, with token conditions sampled from teacher-forced
Stage 1 predictions. At inference, Stage 1 decodes greedily, and the
refiner uses $`4`$ Euler steps ($`6`$ for LIFT-SE-U) on a sway-sampled
time grid with coefficient $`-1`$ \[54\] and guidance strength
$`\gamma=0.5`$. LIFT-SE contains approximately $`193`$M trainable
parameters.

### 3.5 QRes-Codec evaluation

Table 1 compares the discrete and continuous reconstruction paths of
QRes-Codec. At $`600`$ bps, the quantized path obtains a UTMOS of
$`4.19`$, but its PESQ, SIM, and WER indicate a substantial loss of fine
acoustic detail, speaker characteristics, and linguistic content. Since
UTMOS is a learned non-intrusive predictor, values above that of the
ground-truth recordings should not be interpreted as higher
reconstruction fidelity \[55\].

The continuous path retains similar predicted naturalness while
improving PESQ, STOI, SIM, and WER to $`3.75`$, $`0.97`$, $`0.92`$, and
$`2.72`$, respectively, indicating that $`z_{\mathrm{res}}`$ contains
acoustic information critical to improving fidelity. The contrast
between the two paths motivates the proposed decomposition: Stage 1
predicts the discrete representation, and Stage 2 recovers the
information absent from that representation.

### 3.6 Speech enhancement results

Table 2 compares the systems on DNS1. On no-reverb, LIFT-SE performs
competitively in predicted naturalness and phonetic preservation. Under
reverberant conditions, LIFT-SE attains the lowest dWER together with
the highest UTMOS and speaker similarity, exhibiting a favorable balance
across naturalness, speaker similarity, and linguistic fidelity. In
contrast, the high DNSMOS and UTMOS of UniSE do not translate into
comparable dWER, underscoring the need to assess generative SE with both
perceptual and content-based metrics. LIFT-SE-U performs comparably to
LIFT-SE on both subsets, indicating that training on a wider range of
distortions does not degrade performance in conventional scenarios.
Spectrograms and transcripts of selected with-reverb utterances are
provided in Appendix A.

all enhancement systems raise DNSMOS and UTMOS well above the noisy
input. UniSE obtains the highest DNSMOS, but its WER is very high,
whereas TF-GridNet and UniPASE keep the WER close to that of the noisy
input. Although LIFT-SE-U sees no codec distortion or wind noise during
training, it still obtains the best UTMOS, WER, and LPS. However, its
SIM is lower than that of TF-GridNet, a purely discriminative model,
possibly because generative modeling tends to restore natural-sounding
speech under more complex distortions at the cost of some speaker
characteristics.

## 4 Conclusion

We presented LIFT-SE, a two-stage generative SE system that separates
discrete linguistic inference from continuous acoustic refinement within
the representation space of QRes-Codec. Evaluations of QRes-Codec and
LIFT-SE consistently show that autoregressive token prediction primarily
establishes speech naturalness, while refinement in the continuous
latent improves signal fidelity, speaker preservation, and linguistic
consistency. On DNS1, LIFT-SE is particularly competitive under
reverberation, and the ablations support the complementary roles of the
two stages. Extending the training simulation to more distortion types
largely maintains this behavior on DNS1 and yields a favorable word
error rate on the multi-distortion URGENT 2025 non-blind test set. Some
limitations remain: sequential autoregressive decoding increases
inference latency, and speaker similarity in complex scenarios still has
room for improvement. Future work will consider improved speaker
preservation and more efficient token generation.
