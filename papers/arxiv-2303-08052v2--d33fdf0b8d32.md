# Localizing Spatial Information in Neural Spatiospectral Filters

Annika Briegleb   Thomas Haubner   Vasileios Belagiannis   Walter
Kellermann This work has been accepted to EUSIPCO 2023. Multimedia
Communications and Signal Processing  
Friedrich-Alexander-Universität Erlangen-Nürnberg, Erlangen, Germany  
{annika.briegleb, thomas.haubner, vasileios.belagiannis,
walter.kellermann}@fau.de

###### Abstract

Beamforming for multichannel speech enhancement relies on the estimation
of spatial characteristics of the acoustic scene. In its simplest form,
the delay-and-sum beamformer (DSB) introduces a time delay to all
channels to align the desired signal components for constructive
superposition. Recent investigations of neural spatiospectral filtering
revealed that these filters can be characterized by a beampattern
similar to one of traditional beamformers, which shows that artificial
neural networks can learn and explicitly represent spatial structure.
Using the [Complex-valued Spatial Autoencoder](#id2.2.id2)
([COSPA](#id2.2.id2)) as an exemplary neural spatiospectral filter for
multichannel speech enhancement, we investigate where and how such
networks represent spatial information. We show via clustering that for
[COSPA](#id2.2.id2) the spatial information is represented by the
features generated by a gated recurrent unit (GRU) layer that has access
to all channels simultaneously and that these features are not source-
but only direction of arrival-dependent.

###### Index Terms:

spatial filtering, multichannel speech enhancement, DNN interpretability

## I Introduction

Spatial filtering for speech and audio signals usually relies on
signal-independent and -dependent beamformers such as the [delay-and-sum
beamformer](#id5.5.id5) ([DSB](#id5.5.id5)) or the [minimum variance
distortionless response](#id10.10.id10) ([MVDR](#id10.10.id10))
beamformer. Currently, (deep) neural networks that can support
\[[1](#bib.bib1), [2](#bib.bib2), [3](#bib.bib3), [4](#bib.bib4),
[5](#bib.bib5)\] or substitute \[[6](#bib.bib6), [7](#bib.bib7),
[8](#bib.bib8), [9](#bib.bib9), [10](#bib.bib10)\] conventional
beamformers are moving into the focus for speech enhancement and target
speech extraction in noisy and reverberant scenarios. We denote the
latter approaches as neural spatiospectral filters.

The simplest method for spatial filtering in speech enhancement, the
[DSB](#id5.5.id5), solely relies on phase alignment of the signals
captured by the different channels to enhance the desired signal. For
this, traditional beamformers require knowledge of the [direction of
arrival](#id4.4.id4) ([DoA](#id4.4.id4)) of the desired source. On the
other hand, for a single desired speaker in the presence of non-speech
or nondirectional background noise, neural spatiospectral filters, often
do not require this information \[[6](#bib.bib6), [7](#bib.bib7)\].
While beampatterns demonstrate that neural spatiospectral filters can
represent spatial information \[[6](#bib.bib6), [11](#bib.bib11)\], it
still needs to be clarified how the spatial information contained in the
multichannel input is processed and represented by these neural
spatiospectral filters. In this contribution, we investigate where and
how spatial information is captured in an exemplary neural
spatiospectral filter. For this, we analyze the [COSPA](#id2.2.id2)
\[[6](#bib.bib6)\], which estimates a complex-valued multichannel mask
for speech enhancement. While each neural spatiospectral filter has its
own architecture design, most filters include temporal processing. We
choose [COSPA](#id2.2.id2) because the joint temporal processing of all
channels is localized in one [gated recurrent unit](#id8.8.id8)
([GRU](#id8.8.id8)) layer in the bottleneck of the network and the
specific architecture of [COSPA](#id2.2.id2)
(cf. Sec. [II](#S2 "II Spatial filtering with COSPA ‣ Localizing Spatial Information in Neural Spatiospectral Filters"))
allows to analyze the spatial processing conveniently. Consequently, we
investigate the spatial information contained in the features of the
network before and after this [GRU](#id8.8.id8) layer as it is the only
network part with memory that can modify all channels individually,
which is required to align the phases of the different channel signals.

In
Sec. [II](#S2 "II Spatial filtering with COSPA ‣ Localizing Spatial Information in Neural Spatiospectral Filters"),
we discuss the signal model, the architecture of [COSPA](#id2.2.id2),
and why its [GRU](#id8.8.id8) layer is of special interest for spatial
filtering. In
Sec. [III](#S3 "III Quantifying spatial information ‣ Localizing Spatial Information in Neural Spatiospectral Filters"),
we explain how we measure the spatial information contained in the
network’s features. We describe our experimental setup in
Sec. [IV-A](#S4.SS1 "IV-A Experimental setup ‣ IV Experimental Validation ‣ Localizing Spatial Information in Neural Spatiospectral Filters")
and present and discuss our results in
Sec. [IV-B](#S4.SS2 "IV-B Results ‣ IV Experimental Validation ‣ Localizing Spatial Information in Neural Spatiospectral Filters").
Sec. [V](#S5 "V Conclusion ‣ Localizing Spatial Information in Neural Spatiospectral Filters")
concludes the paper.

## II Spatial filtering with COSPA

In this contribution, we consider the task of extracting the signal of a
point-like speech source from a multichannel signal recorded by $`M`$
microphones in a reverberant room using a neural spatiospectral filter
that estimates a complex-valued mask for speech enhancement. The filter
can thus modify both the magnitude and the phase of the input signal. In
the [short-time Fourier transform](#id17.17.id17)
([STFT](#id17.17.id17)) domain, we consider the frame-wise
single-channel speech signal estimate $`\hat{S}{(\tau)}`$, the
multichannel signal $`{{{X_{m}{(\tau)}},m} = 1},{\ldots,M}`$, and the
complex-valued multichannel filter mask $`\mathcal{M}_{m}{(\tau)}`$
estimated by the neural filter. Frames are half-overlapping and indexed
by $`\tau`$ in both the time and the [STFT](#id17.17.id17) domain. We
omit the frequency index for brevity. The estimated speech signal is
obtained by

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
{{\hat{S}{(\tau)}} = {\sum\limits_{m = 1}^{M}{{{\mathcal{M}_{m}{(\tau)}} \cdot X_{m}}{(\tau)}}}}.
``` |  | (1) |

As neural spatiospectral filter for our investigations we choose
[COSPA](#id2.2.id2) as introduced in \[[6](#bib.bib6)\], which estimates
the complex-valued mask $`\mathcal{M}_{m}`$ for each channel $`m`$. A
simplified structure of [COSPA](#id2.2.id2) is depicted in
Fig. [1](#S2.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ II Spatial filtering with COSPA ‣ Localizing Spatial Information in Neural Spatiospectral Filters").
[COSPA](#id2.2.id2) consists of an encoder and a decoder, which both
process all signal channels equally, and a compandor, which can process
each channel individually. As spatial information is obtained from the
differences between signals captured by spatially distributed sensors,
and processing it usually includes phase alignment, i.e., delays, the
compandor is of specific interest for investigating the representation
of spatial information inside [COSPA](#id2.2.id2). As shown in
Fig. [1](#S2.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ II Spatial filtering with COSPA ‣ Localizing Spatial Information in Neural Spatiospectral Filters"),
the compandor consists of a complex-valued [GRU](#id8.8.id8) layer
\[[12](#bib.bib12), [13](#bib.bib13)\] in between two complex-valued
fully connected layers. The [GRU](#id8.8.id8) layer consisting of
$`U_{\text{out}}`$ complex-valued [GRUs](#id8.8.id8) (see
\[[6](#bib.bib6)\]) provides memory to the network, which is expected to
facilitate the phase alignment of the channels and is thus in the focus
of our analysis. The relevance of the [GRU](#id8.8.id8) layer is also
supported by \[[14](#bib.bib14)\], where the [GRU](#id8.8.id8) layer is
initialized with features derived from the [DoA](#id4.4.id4) of the
desired speaker to guide the network for target speaker extraction. Note
that, here, for the considered scenario of speech enhancement without
speech interferers [COSPA](#id2.2.id2) does not receive any explicit
information about the [DoA](#id4.4.id4) of the desired source.

As shown in
Fig. [1](#S2.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ II Spatial filtering with COSPA ‣ Localizing Spatial Information in Neural Spatiospectral Filters"),
we denote the features at the input of the [GRU](#id8.8.id8) layer as
$`{{\overset{\sim}{\mathbf{h}}}_{\text{in}}{(\tau)}} = {\lbrack{{\overset{\sim}{h}}_{\text{in}}{(\tau,1)}},\ldots,{{\overset{\sim}{h}}_{\text{in}}{(\tau,U_{\text{in}})}}\rbrack}^{\top}`$,
with $`U_{\text{in}}`$ being the number of input channels of the
[GRU](#id8.8.id8) layer, and
$`{{\overset{\sim}{h}}_{\text{in}}{(\tau,u)}} \in {\mathbb{C}}`$ being a
single feature for unit $`u`$ and time frame $`\tau`$. These features
are obtained from the first fully connected layer in the compandor,
which is the first layer in [COSPA](#id2.2.id2) that can fuse all
channels. Similarly, we define
$`{{\overset{\sim}{\mathbf{h}}}_{\text{out}}{(\tau)}} = {\lbrack{{\overset{\sim}{h}}_{\text{out}}{(\tau,1)}},\ldots,{{\overset{\sim}{h}}_{\text{out}}{(\tau,U_{\text{out}})}}\rbrack}^{\top}`$
as features at the output of the [GRU](#id8.8.id8) layer with
$`U_{\text{out}}`$ being the number of [GRUs](#id8.8.id8) in the
[GRU](#id8.8.id8) layer.

## III Quantifying spatial information

As explained in
Sec. [II](#S2 "II Spatial filtering with COSPA ‣ Localizing Spatial Information in Neural Spatiospectral Filters"),
we expect that the features generated by [COSPA](#id2.2.id2)’s
[GRU](#id8.8.id8) layer contain spatial information about the desired
source. Hence, for a test signal in which the speaker position changes
discretely with time, we expect that output features of the
[GRU](#id8.8.id8) layer corresponding to frames coming from the same
source position are similar to each other and thereby express DoA
information resulting from phase alignment. In contrast, at the input of
the [GRU](#id8.8.id8) layer we do not expect to find such similarities
in the feature vectors. To identify frames characterized by similar
feature vectors and to quantify the amount of spatial information
contained in the features, we use the $`k`$-means clustering algorithm
\[[15](#bib.bib15)\] on the features
$`{\overset{\sim}{\mathbf{h}}}_{\text{in/out}}{(\tau)}`$ at the input
and the output of the [GRU](#id8.8.id8) layer in the compandor. As these
features can have very different value ranges per unit, we normalize the
features of each unit per test sequence to have an amplitude range of
$`- 1`$ to $`1`$ for our clustering analysis. Hence, for clustering,
each frame is characterized by its normalized feature vector
$`\mathbf{h}_{\text{in/out}}{(\tau)}`$.

Due to the expected discriminative nature of the features, we use the
L$`1`$ distance $`\text{d}{( \cdot , \cdot )}`$ for the $`k`$-means
algorithm. Given $`Q`$ sources, which can be different speakers or the
same speaker at different positions, we use $`k = {Q + 1}`$ cluster
centers, to allow one cluster for each of the $`Q`$ sources in a test
sequence, and one cluster for signal pauses as we assume that spatial
information cannot be extracted from silent signal frames. We assign the
pause label to the cluster that has the lowest signal energy in the
corresponding target signal segments. The labels for the $`Q`$ sources
are assigned by a majority vote of the assigned frames.

We quantify the clustering results in terms of the grouping success, the
average distance $`\overline{d}`$ of the features to their assigned
cluster center, and the overall percentage of frames assigned to the
pause cluster. The grouping success measures in percent how many of the
frames belonging to one source are assigned to the same cluster. For
$`Q = 2`$, a grouping success of 50% means that no distinction can be
made between the two sources, while a grouping success of 100% means
that for both sources all frames belonging to the active segments of the
corresponding source are assigned to the same cluster. For the
calculation of the grouping success we only consider frames assigned to
one of the active source clusters and disregard all frames assigned to
the pause cluster. Since pauses can also appear while a speaker is
active, the assignment to the pause cluster is not necessarily wrong but
uninformative. Hence, we disregard these frames.

To account for the pause cluster, we measure the overall percentage of
frames assigned to the pause cluster as we expect the features after the
[GRU](#id8.8.id8) layer to be more expressive with respect to the
corresponding [DoA](#id4.4.id4) and to benefit from the memory of the
[GRU](#id8.8.id8) layer. Hence, we expect that the features
$`\mathbf{h}_{\text{out}}{(\tau)}`$ at the output of the
[GRU](#id8.8.id8) layer can be clustered more consistently than the
features $`\mathbf{h}_{\text{in}}{(\tau)}`$ at the input, and therefore
the pause clusters should exhibit a lower percentage.

Finally, the average distance of the features to the cluster centers
$`\overline{d}`$ is computed as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
{\overline{d} = {\frac{1}{S}{\sum\limits_{s = 1}^{S}\left( {\frac{1}{k}{\sum\limits_{n = 1}^{k}\left( {\frac{1}{T_{n,s}}{\sum\limits_{\tau_{n,s} = 1}^{T_{n}}{\text{d}{(\mathbf{c}_{n,s},{\mathbf{h}{(\tau_{n,s})}})}}}} \right)}} \right)}}},
``` |  | (2) |

where $`\tau_{n,s} = {1,\ldots,T_{n,s}}`$ indexes the frames assigned to
cluster $`n`$ in sequence $`{{s,s} = 1},{\ldots,S}`$, and
$`\text{d}{(\mathbf{c}_{n,s},{\mathbf{h}{(\tau_{n,s})}})}`$ describes
the L$`1`$ distance from feature vector $`\mathbf{h}{(\tau_{n,s})}`$ to
cluster center $`\mathbf{c}_{n,s}`$. $`\overline{d}`$ describes how
dense the clusters are: a smaller value means that the assigned features
are closer to their cluster center and hence more similar to each other.

## IV Experimental Validation

So far, experiments with [COSPA](#id2.2.id2) have only been conducted
for scenarios with one spatially static desired source
\[[6](#bib.bib6)\]. To investigate the exploitation of the spatial
information contained in the multichannel signal, we perform experiments
with changing speaker positions within one signal sequence. The purpose
of our experiments is to verify the hypothesis that a layer with memory
that can access all channels individually in a neural spatial filter,
i.e., the [GRU](#id8.8.id8) layer in [COSPA](#id2.2.id2)’s compandor,
explicitly represents spatial information about the acoustic scene at
its output. We first examine [COSPA](#id2.2.id2)’s masking behavior for
changing speaker positions in a scenario without background noise and
with only one active speaker at any time. We analyze the features before
and after the [GRU](#id8.8.id8) layer and measure their discriminative
power via clustering. Then, we investigate whether the features
distinguish between speakers or between [DoAs](#id4.4.id4), and,
finally, we assess how robust the spatial processing is against
spatially and spectrally white background noise.

### IV-A Experimental setup

For all experiments, signals are generated by simulating a room with
dimensions sampled uniformly from \[4-8, 4-8, 1-4\] m and a
reverberation time between 200 ms and 500 ms. A uniformly spaced linear
microphone array (ULA) with $`M = 3`$ microphones and element spacing of
0.04 m, is randomly placed in the room with uniformly distributed
positions. Defining the array’s endfire directions as 0° and 180°,
speaker positions are chosen between 0° and 180°. The speech data is
taken from the TIMIT database \[[16](#bib.bib16)\] with its default
split between training data and test data, and convolved with the
corresponding room impulse response generated according to
\[[17](#bib.bib17)\] for the respective speaker positions. All training
and test sequences have a length of 7 s at 16 kHz sampling rate.

We create two training datasets of approximately 8 h each for our
experiments. In the first dataset, denoted by DS-clean, each training
sequence contains two speakers which are placed randomly at $`Q = 2`$
different positions in the room and are talking alternatingly. In each
sequence, there are three segments, where Speaker $`1`$ is active in the
first and third segment and Speaker $`2`$ is active in the middle
segment. The time instants of the two speaker changes are chosen
randomly, uniformly distributed between $`1`$ and $`3`$ s, and $`5`$ and
$`6`$ s respectively. The angular distance of the two speakers’
[DoAs](#id4.4.id4) is at least $`20`$° and their distance to the array
and the walls is at least 0.3 m. For creating the target signal, the
multichannel input speech signal image corresponding to each speaker
separately is filtered with a [DSB](#id5.5.id5) steered towards the
position of the respective speaker. The two signals for the respective
speakers are combined to obtain the target signal for the end-to-end
training of [COSPA](#id2.2.id2). Note that this dataset does not contain
any background noise and only serves the purpose of investigating the
phase alignment capabilities of [COSPA](#id2.2.id2).

The second training dataset, denoted by DS-WGN, has the same setup as
DS-clean but the signals consist of the speech signals and additive
spatially and spectrally white Gaussian noise at [signal-to-noise
ratios](#id15.15.id15) between -10 dB and 50 dB in steps of 5 dB. We
train [COSPA](#id2.2.id2) individually on both training datasets and
denote the resulting trained networks as [COSPA](#id2.2.id2)_(clean) and
[COSPA](#id2.2.id2)_(WGN) respectively.

For testing, we define four datasets. DST-clean has the same setup as
the training dataset DS-clean and consists of 50 test sequences used for
the verification of the hypothesis that the GRU layer output
$`{\overset{\sim}{\mathbf{h}}}_{\text{out}}{(\tau)}`$ represents spatial
information about the source. DST-WGN has 50 test sequences per
[SNR](#id15.15.id15) $`\in {\lbrack{- 10},5,0,5,10,20,30,50\rbrack}`$ dB
but otherwise has the same setup as the training dataset DS-WGN. This
test dataset is used to test the robustness against noise of the spatial
representation in the [GRU](#id8.8.id8) layer. To show that the features
$`{\overset{\sim}{\mathbf{h}}}_{\text{out}}{(\tau)}`$ encode spatial and
not speaker information, we use DST-1Pos, which contains 50 sequences
with two alternating speakers placed at the same position in the room,
and DST-1Spk, which contains 50 signals from a single speaker including
one position change. $`Q = 2`$ and $`k = 3`$ for all experiments.

We parameterize [COSPA](#id2.2.id2) as published in \[[6](#bib.bib6)\].
Especially, the fully connected layer before the [GRU](#id8.8.id8) layer
in the compandor and the [GRU](#id8.8.id8) layer itself both have
$`U_{\text{in}} = U_{\text{out}} = 128`$ units generating both the real
and imaginary part of the $`U_{\text{in/out}}`$ complex-valued features
$`{\overset{\sim}{h}}_{\text{in/out}}{(\tau,u)}`$. For computing the
[STFT](#id17.17.id17) features as input to [COSPA](#id2.2.id2), we use
frames of 1024 time-domain signal samples and a frame shift of 512
samples.

Since clustering is initialization-dependent, we report the results for
the clustering as average over five trials, where in each trial the best
out of five $`k`$-means clustering attempts is used to compute the
evaluation metrics.

#### Spatial adjustment

We first investigate whether [COSPA](#id2.2.id2) can react to changing
spatial conditions within an acoustic scene without guiding information
during testing. As a typical example, in
Fig. [2](#S4.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ IV-B Results ‣ IV Experimental Validation ‣ Localizing Spatial Information in Neural Spatiospectral Filters"),
it can be seen that for the test dataset DST-clean the phase mask
$`\mathcal{M}_{2}`$ generated by [COSPA](#id2.2.id2)_(clean) indeed
reflects the [DoA](#id4.4.id4) change following the changing speaker
activity. From this it can be concluded that spatial information of the
received signal is used by [COSPA](#id2.2.id2) to generate the phase
component of the estimated mask $`\mathcal{M}_{m}`$.

Based on this observation and the special role of the [GRU](#id8.8.id8)
layer in [COSPA](#id2.2.id2)’s compandor
(cf. Sec. [II](#S2 "II Spatial filtering with COSPA ‣ Localizing Spatial Information in Neural Spatiospectral Filters")),
we investigate whether the features generated before and after the
[GRU](#id8.8.id8) layer also reflect the positional changes in the
signal.

#### The pause cluster

It can be noted from both
Fig. [3](#S4.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ IV-B Results ‣ IV Experimental Validation ‣ Localizing Spatial Information in Neural Spatiospectral Filters")
and
Table [I](#S4.T1 "TABLE I ‣ Clustering results ‣ IV-B Results ‣ IV Experimental Validation ‣ Localizing Spatial Information in Neural Spatiospectral Filters")
that fewer frames are assigned to the pause cluster at the output of the
[GRU](#id8.8.id8) layer than at the input, and that those frames belong
to longer speech pauses and are not just single frames. This can be
explained by the fact that at the input of the [GRU](#id8.8.id8) layer,
the pause cluster aside from the silent frames also contains several
undecided frames and therefore has relatively many frames assigned to
it. At the output of the [GRU](#id8.8.id8) layer this cluster contains
only frames with actual speech pauses and hence also has a lower number
of frames assigned to it. This also shows the beneficial effect of the
[GRU](#id8.8.id8) layer’s memory, since it creates, based on previous
signal parts, more meaningful features for frames where signal energy is
low
(cf. Figs. [3](#S4.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ IV-B Results ‣ IV Experimental Validation ‣ Localizing Spatial Information in Neural Spatiospectral Filters")[3](#S4.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ IV-B Results ‣ IV Experimental Validation ‣ Localizing Spatial Information in Neural Spatiospectral Filters"),[3](#S4.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ IV-B Results ‣ IV Experimental Validation ‣ Localizing Spatial Information in Neural Spatiospectral Filters")).
Before the [GRU](#id8.8.id8) layer, these frames would be assigned to
the pause cluster or, even worse, to the wrong speaker cluster. Note
that since the grouping success is only computed based on frames sorted
into one of the speaker clusters, erroneously assigning many low-level
speech frames to the pause cluster can lead to relatively high grouping
success scores even at the input of the [GRU](#id8.8.id8) layer.

#### Adaptation to [DoA](#id4.4.id4) changes

We observed in our experiments that the grouping success for the frames
directly following a position change depends on whether the position
change coincides with a speech pause or not. As an example, in
Fig. [3](#S4.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ IV-B Results ‣ IV Experimental Validation ‣ Localizing Spatial Information in Neural Spatiospectral Filters")
the second switch around $`\tau = 5.4`$ s coincides with a switch from a
pause to a speech signal. [COSPA](#id2.2.id2) is able to identify this
change immediately. If the change had happened at $`\tau = 4.9`$s,
[COSPA](#id2.2.id2) would not know that the position of the desired
speaker has changed, because this will only become apparent when the
speaker starts talking. Unsurprisingly and similar to acoustic source
localization, adaptation to a new DoA cannot be expected during pauses.
A similar effect can be observed for a switch between two
[DoAs](#id4.4.id4) during speech activity. In some scenarios the
assignment of the input features after this [DoA](#id4.4.id4) switch
changes almost instantaneously, while for the output features changing
the cluster assignment takes a few frames when speech is present. This
is expected behavior, since the memory provided by the GRU will remember
the spatial setup of the scene and hence will wait for more evidence for
a [DoA](#id4.4.id4) change before reassigning the features. The delay in
changing the features is beneficial for increasing robustness against
directional interferers, e.g., when applying [COSPA](#id2.2.id2) to
target speaker extraction and speech enhancement in the presence of
multiple sources. (Note that this behavior depends also on the
differences in amplitude of the signals and cannot be observed in
Fig. [3](#S4.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ IV-B Results ‣ IV Experimental Validation ‣ Localizing Spatial Information in Neural Spatiospectral Filters")[3](#S4.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ IV-B Results ‣ IV Experimental Validation ‣ Localizing Spatial Information in Neural Spatiospectral Filters")
at $`\tau = 2.9`$ s as the signal amplitudes differ notably.)

#### Source independence of features

To validate that the features generated by the [GRU](#id8.8.id8) layer
indeed reflect different spatial setups and not different speakers as
they are present in DST-clean, we also test [COSPA](#id2.2.id2)_(WGN) on
DST-1Spk and DST-1Pos, which contain signals from a single speaker with
changing position or from two speakers at the same position respectively
(cf. Sec. [IV-A](#S4.SS1 "IV-A Experimental setup ‣ IV Experimental Validation ‣ Localizing Spatial Information in Neural Spatiospectral Filters")).
As can be seen in
Table [I](#S4.T1 "TABLE I ‣ Clustering results ‣ IV-B Results ‣ IV Experimental Validation ‣ Localizing Spatial Information in Neural Spatiospectral Filters"),
for DST-1Spk the clustering scores for $`\mathbf{h}_{\text{out}}`$ are
good even though only one speaker is present. On the other hand, the
grouping success for $`\mathbf{h}_{\text{out}}`$ for DST-1Pos is really
low, which shows that the two speakers at the same position cannot be
distinguished by the features of the [GRU](#id8.8.id8) layer. These
results show that [COSPA](#id2.2.id2) indeed reacts to a change of
spatial setup and not to the characteristics of a different speaker.

Apart from the results shown here, we find that [COSPA](#id2.2.id2) can
also adapt to a different number of position changes during testing than
it was trained on. This underscores the flexibility of such a neural
spatiospectral filter.

#### Robustness against noise

So far, the results have been presented for [COSPA](#id2.2.id2) tested
on signals without any background noise. To demonstrate how well the
spatial information is captured in the [GRU](#id8.8.id8) layer in the
presence of noise and for the task of noise reduction rather than just
phase alignment, we provide the clustering test results for
[COSPA](#id2.2.id2)_(WGN), which was trained for a wide range of
[SNR](#id15.15.id15) levels, for test sequences with various
[SNR](#id15.15.id15) levels in
Table [I](#S4.T1 "TABLE I ‣ Clustering results ‣ IV-B Results ‣ IV Experimental Validation ‣ Localizing Spatial Information in Neural Spatiospectral Filters").
It can be seen that for all [SNRs](#id15.15.id15) both the grouping
success and the cluster density are notably better for the output than
for the input features of the [GRU](#id8.8.id8) layer. As expected, the
grouping success decreases for lower [SNRs](#id15.15.id15). The number
of frames assigned to the pause cluster also decreases from input to
output of the [GRU](#id8.8.id8) layer, which points to more
discriminative features and a good feature memory in the
[GRU](#id8.8.id8) layer.

In summary it can be stated that the spatial information exploited for
spatiospectral filtering with [COSPA](#id2.2.id2) is processed by the
[GRU](#id8.8.id8) layer in the compandor and clearly expressed in the
output features of this [GRU](#id8.8.id8) layer. From the exemplary
character of [COSPA](#id2.2.id2)’s single [GRU](#id8.8.id8) layer in an
otherwise memoryless neural network, we can infer that the
[GRU](#id8.8.id8) layer indeed implements a noise-robust and source
signal-independent phase equalization for coherent multichannel signals.

## V Conclusion

In this paper, we investigated, through the example of
[COSPA](#id2.2.id2), the capabilities of a neural spatiospectral filter
to represent and exploit spatial information. We found that the
complex-valued [GRU](#id8.8.id8) layer in [COSPA](#id2.2.id2)’s
compandor processes the spatial information and that the features
obtained at the output of the [GRU](#id8.8.id8) layer provide
discriminative information about the [DoA](#id4.4.id4) of a signal.
Different from a [DSB](#id5.5.id5) and as long as only one point-like
speech source is active, [COSPA](#id2.2.id2) does not require
information about the [DoA](#id4.4.id4) of the signal to adapt to
positional changes of the source. Furthermore, we showed that clustering
of input and output features is an appropriate tool for assessing the
capabilities of a neural network layer for the task of spatial
filtering.
````
