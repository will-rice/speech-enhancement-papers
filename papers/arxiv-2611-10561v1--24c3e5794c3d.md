# BAmbiDrop: Array-Agnostic Speech Enhancement with Zero-Shot binaural Reproduction using Multi-center Ambisonics Encoding

Michael Tatarjitzky    Vladimir Tourbabin    Boaz Rafaely

###### Abstract

Array-agnostic enhancement networks generalize across microphone
geometries but produce a single channel, while binaural enhancement
methods preserve interaural cues only for one fixed device geometry. We
propose BAmbiDrop, a framework that extends array-agnostic enhancement
to binaural reproduction without retraining. Exploiting the fact that
the Ambisonics origin is an arbitrary point in space, a frozen
single-channel AmbiDrop network estimates a complex mask at multiple
centers across the array; these masks are fused by a complex mean into a
single shared mask, applied to the microphone signals and rendered by
fixed BSM filters. Across 21 free-field and rigid-sphere arrays, the
fused mask improves enhancement and interaural cue reproduction
simultaneously, whereas masking each microphone independently degrades
the latter without a consistent gain in the former.

###### Index Terms: 

Binaural reproduction, speech enhancement, Ambisonics, array-agnostic
modeling, spatial cue preservation

## 1 Introduction

Capturing an acoustic scene with a microphone array and reproducing it
for a listener is a central problem in spatial audio, underlying
immersive telepresence, augmented reality, hearing assistance and
teleconferencing \[23\]. In binaural reproduction, the recording is
rendered to the two ears of a listener, such that the interaural cues
supporting localization and externalization are preserved \[23\]. Real
scenes may contain competing talkers, noise and reverberation, so the
recording must also be enhanced for the target talker to be intelligible
\[21\]. Achieving high performance on both operations may therefore
impose a challenge \[23\].

Systems that address both objectives differ mainly in the domain in
which the noise reduction is applied. One line operates in the
Ambisonics domain, where the enhanced signal remains a sound-field
representation that can still be rendered binaurally, by masking
designed for binaural reproduction \[17\], direction- and
reverberation-preserving noise reduction \[11\], or deep
Ambisonics-to-Ambisonics networks \[10, 15\]. A second line operates at
the ears, where the trade-off between noise reduction and cue
preservation is well documented: beamformers for binaural hearing
devices constrain the interaural cues of the residual signal explicitly
\[6, 20\], and learning-based systems produce a two-channel output under
cue-preservation loss terms \[31\]. A third line enhances the microphone
signals themselves, estimating one output per microphone so that a
renderer may follow \[22, 16\], an architecture recently motivated by
binaural reproduction \[7\]. Although these methods were found useful,
the learning-based ones are typically preferable over classical
approaches due to their improved speech enhancement \[13\]. However,
learning-based neural networks are usually optimized for one microphone
array layout and generalize poorly to others \[28\], and although they
can be retrained per device, this requires substantial data which may
not always be available. This shortcoming has motivated array-agnostic
processing approaches.

Array-agnostic enhancement has been approached by architectures that
remain invariant to the number and the placement of the microphones,
employing transform-average-concatenate layers \[18\] or attention
across channels \[33\], at the cost of large multi-geometry training
sets. Recently, we proposed AmbiDrop \[28, 29\], which instead trains on
ideal Ambisonics with channel-wise dropout and encodes an arbitrary
array by Ambisonics Signal Matching (ASM) \[9\] at inference, so that no
array geometry is observed during training. These methods, however,
produce a single channel, and the spatial attributes of their output are
not preserved. The closest exception maps array signals to a binaural
output through a network trained with a geometry-invariant coherence
feature \[12\], but its renderer is itself learned from data of one
array family, and its invariance is demonstrated on a small set of
compact configurations. In summary, the development and study of methods
for array-agnostic speech enhancement that also deliver binaural audio
remains a gap in current literature.

We propose BAmbiDrop to bridge this gap. The proposed method applies the
mask of a trained AmbiDrop network \[28\] by re-positioning the
Ambisonics coordinate-system origin to align with each of the array
microphones, then applying Binaural Signal Matching (BSM) filters \[19\]
to the enhanced microphone signals to produce binaural signals. The
method exploits the generalization of the spherical harmonics
representation: relocation can be applied to any microphone position
using the same trained mask without loss of performance. We further show
that a common mask shared between microphones provides a more accurate
binaural signal as it reduces inter-channel variability compared to
microphone-dependent masks. The contributions of this work are as
follows: (i) the development of an array-agnostic speech enhancement
framework with an accurate and efficient projection to binaural signals,
(ii) once trained, the network can be applied to any array without
re-training, (iii) the proposed method has been evaluated for both
speech enhancement and binaural reproduction, showing improved
performance compared to baselines.

## 2 Background

This section presents the array signal model, Ambisonics encoding by
ASM, binaural reproduction by BSM, and the AmbiDrop enhancement model.
The spherical coordinate system $`(r,\theta,\phi)`$ denotes radius,
elevation and azimuth, and the wavenumber is $`k=\frac{2\pi}{c}f`$, with
$`c`$ the speed of sound and $`f`$ the frequency.

### 2.1 Array Signal Model

Consider an arbitrary array of $`D`$ omnidirectional microphones
positioned at $`(r_{d},\theta_{d},\phi_{d})`$,
$`\forall\,1\leq d\leq D`$, in a sound field composed of $`Q`$ plane
waves which represent the direct sound from far-field sources together
with their reflections from the enclosure boundaries. The plane-wave
arrival directions are denoted by
$`\mathbf{u}_{q}=(\theta_{q},\phi_{q})`$, $`\forall\,1\leq q\leq Q`$.
The array steering matrix $`\mathbf{V}(k)`$ of dimensions $`D\times Q`$,
with element $`[\mathbf{V}(k)]_{\mathit{d,q}}`$ represents the transfer
function between the $`q`$-th plane wave and the $`d`$-th microphone.
With this model, the microphone signals can be formulated as:

```math
\mathbf{x}(k)=\mathbf{V}(k)\mathbf{s}(k)+\mathbf{n}(k), \tag{1}
```

where $`\mathbf{s}(k)`$ holds the plane-wave amplitudes at the origin
and $`\mathbf{n}(k)`$ is additive sensor noise, assumed spatially white
and uncorrelated with the source signals.

### 2.2 Ambisonics Encoding and ASM

The Ambisonics signals up to order $`N_{\mathit{a}}`$ are obtained from
the plane-wave amplitudes as
$`\mathbf{a}(k)=\mathbf{Y}^{H}_{\mathit{\Omega_{Q}}}\mathbf{s}(k)`$
\[24\], where $`\mathbf{a}(k)`$ stacks the Ambisonics signals
$`a_{\mathit{nm}}(k)`$ and $`\mathbf{Y}_{\mathit{\Omega_{Q}}}`$ is the
SH matrix of dimensions $`Q\times(N_{\mathit{a}}+1)^{2}`$ whose columns
$`\mathbf{y}_{\mathit{nm}}`$ contain the SH basis functions of order
$`0\leq n\leq N_{\mathit{a}}`$ and degree $`-n\leq m\leq n`$ evaluated
at the $`Q`$ plane-wave directions. This representation depends on the
sound field alone and not on the array that observes it.

ASM \[9\] estimates these signals from an arbitrary array by a linear
mapping of the microphone signals,

```math
\hat{a}_{\mathit{nm}}(k)={[\mathbf{c}_{\mathit{nm}}^{ASM}(k)]}^{H}\mathbf{x}(k), \tag{2}
```

with the coefficients $`\mathbf{c}(k)`$ designed to minimize the NMSE
between the estimated and the true Ambisonics signals. Under a diffuse
field with source covariance $`\sigma_{\mathit{s}}^{2}\mathbf{I}`$ and
spatially white noise of power $`\sigma_{\mathit{n}}^{2}`$, the
minimizer is:

```math
\mathbf{c}_{\mathit{nm}}^{ASM}(k)=\left(\mathbf{V}(k)\mathbf{V}^{H}(k)+\frac{\sigma_{\mathit{n}}^{2}}{\sigma_{\mathit{s}}^{2}}\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{V}(k)\mathbf{y}_{\mathit{nm}}, \tag{3}
```

which requires $`(N_{\mathit{a}}+1)^{2}\leq D`$ for all channels to be
encoded \[24\]. The accuracy of individual channels nonetheless depends
on the array geometry, and so some channels may be poorly
estimated \[9\].

### 2.3 Binaural Reproduction With BSM

BSM \[19\] reconstructs the binaural signal at a listener’s ears from
signals recorded by an arbitrary microphone array, sharing the same
linear structure as ASM (Eq. 2). The binaural signals
$`\hat{p}_{\mathit{l,r}}(k)={[\mathbf{c}_{\mathit{l,r}}^{BSM}(k)]}^{H}\mathbf{x}(k)`$,
combined into vector
$`\mathbf{p}(k)=[p_{\mathit{l}}(k),p_{\mathit{r}}(k)]^{T}`$, are
estimated by the filter $`\mathbf{c}_{\mathit{l,r}}^{BSM}(k)`$. This
filter minimizes the NMSE to the true binaural signals, and has a form
similar to Eq. (3). Above a crossover frequency the least-squares fit is
replaced by a magnitude least-squares minimization \[19\], which relaxes
the phase constraint where interaural phase is perceptually less
important.

### 2.4 Speech Enhancement With AmbiDrop

AmbiDrop \[29\] performs the task of speech enhancement in the
Ambisonics domain, using an FT-JNF network \[30\] trained exclusively on
ideal Ambisonics signals of order $`N_{\mathit{a}}=2`$, with
channel-wise dropout applied during training. Since these signals depend
on the sound field alone, as noted in Sec. 2.2, the network training is
independent of any microphone array, while the dropout improves
robustness to the channel-dependent encoding errors that ASM introduces
at inference.

At inference, the microphone signals of an arbitrary array are encoded
by ASM, transformed to the STFT domain, and processed by the network,
which estimates a complex mask $`M(t,f)`$ at each time-frequency bin
$`(t,f)`$. The mask is applied to the omnidirectional channel,

```math
\tilde{a}_{00}(t,f)=M(t,f)\,\hat{a}_{00}(t,f), \tag{4}
```

yielding a single-channel enhanced signal. The model used here differs
from the published one in the training reference alone, which is the
reverberant target speaker rather than its direct-path component:
reverberation carries the externalization, perceived distance and
envelopment cues a binaural renderer must reproduce \[5, 4, 3\], a
well-documented consideration in spatial audio reproduction.

## 3 Proposed Method

We propose BAmbiDrop, a binaural extension of the single-channel-output
AmbiDrop framework, in which speech enhancement is applied separately to
each microphone by repositioning the center of the Ambisonics coordinate
system for each microphone across the array, and fusing the
microphone-dependent masks into a single shared mask prior to binaural
rendering. The proposed method is outlined in Fig. 1, and detailed
below.

Figure 1: The BAmbiDrop pipeline (Sec. 3). The microphone array feeds
$`D`$ microphone-aligned ASM encoders, one per microphone position
$`\mathbf{r}_{\mathit{d}}`$ (Sec. 3.1); the same frozen AmbiDrop network
(Sec. 3.1) is evaluated once per microphone, giving one mask
$`M_{\mathit{d}}(t,f)`$ each, and Fusion (Sec. 3.1) averages them into a
single shared mask $`\bar{M}(t,f)`$. Masking (Sec. 3.1) applies that
mask to the unprocessed microphone signals, and BSM (Sec. 3.1) renders
the result to the binaural signal. Dashed outlines mark repeated
instances of the same block, one per microphone.

### 3.1 BAmbiDrop

Microphone-Aligned ASM: The Ambisonics channel for which AmbiDrop
applies masking as in Eq. (4) is $`a_{00}`$ which represent the sound
pressure at the origin of the Ambisonics coordinate system \[24\].
Because the approach of the proposed method is based on applying the
mask computed for channel $`a_{00}`$ to an array microphone, it is
hypothesized that this approach will work better when the microphone is
aligned with the origin of the Ambisonics coordinate system. For this
reason, the Microphone-Aligned ASM block aligns the origin of the
coordinate system with one array microphone before applying ASM and the
AmbiDrop mask to that microphone. In the following, this alignment
process is presented in detail.

The main effect of this origin alignment is on the steering matrix
$`\mathbf{V}(k)`$. Because it represents the transfer function from a
plane-wave of an amplitude defined at the origin to the output of the
array microphones, aligning the origin of the coordinate system simply
leads to a shift in the plane-wave amplitude, as also exploited in \[1\]
for HRTF alignment. The elements of the aligned steering matrix can
therefore be formulated as:

```math
[\mathbf{V}^{\boldsymbol{\delta}}(k)]_{\mathit{d,q}}=[\mathbf{V}(k)]_{\mathit{d,q}}\,\exp\!\big(jk\,\mathbf{u}_{\mathit{q}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\delta}\big). \tag{5}
```

where $`\mathbf{u}_{\mathit{q}}`$ denotes the direction of the $`q`$-th
plane-wave and $`\boldsymbol{\delta}`$ denotes the displacement of the
origin relative to the origin defined for $`\mathbf{V}(k)`$.
Substituting Eq. (5) in Eq. (3) leads to

```math
\mathbf{c}_{\mathit{nm}}^{\boldsymbol{\delta}}(k)=\left(\mathbf{V}(k)\mathbf{V}^{H}(k)+\frac{\sigma_{\mathit{n}}^{2}}{\sigma_{\mathit{s}}^{2}}\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{V}^{\boldsymbol{\delta}}(k)\,\mathbf{y}_{\mathit{nm}}, \tag{6}
```

where it is noted that
$`\mathbf{V}^{\boldsymbol{\delta}}(k)[\mathbf{V}^{\boldsymbol{\delta}}(k)]^{H}=\mathbf{V}(k)[\mathbf{V}(k)]^{H}`$
due to the cancellation of the exponential function.

BAmbiDrop sets $`\boldsymbol{\delta}=\mathbf{r}_{\mathit{d}}`$, the
position of the $`d`$-th microphone of Sec. 2.1, for every
$`1\leq d\leq D`$: the microphone signals are re-encoded using ASM $`D`$
times, once per microphone position, giving $`D`$ sets of
microphone-aligned Ambisonics channels, transformed to the STFT domain
for further processing.

AmbiDrop: The network of Sec. 2.4, trained once on ideal Ambisonics,
independent of any array, is frozen (no further training or adaptation),
and evaluated $`D`$ times - once on the microphone-aligned channels of
each microphone $`d`$. Each evaluation returns one complex mask,
$`M_{\mathit{d}}(t,f)`$, the instance of Eq. (4) $`M(t,f)`$ at that
microphone’s origin.

Fusion: The AmbiDrop stage as above estimated a total of $`D`$ masks,
one for each microphone. In this fusion stage, these masks are averaged
to form a single mask:

```math
\bar{M}(t,f)=\frac{1}{D}\sum_{d=1}^{D}M_{\mathit{d}}(t,f). \tag{7}
```

It is expected that such a mask common to all microphones will reduce
inter-channel distortion and improve the binaural reproduction quality
that follows. Nevertheless, this may come at the expense of speech
enhancement performance. The variations described in Sec. 3.2 are
designed to study this tradeoff.

Masking: In this stage, the common mask is applied directly to each of
the unprocessed microphone signals,

```math
\tilde{X}_{\mathit{d}}(t,f)=\bar{M}(t,f)\,X_{\mathit{d}}(t,f),\quad 1\leq d\leq D, \tag{8}
```

leading to speech-enhanced microphone signals ready for binaural
reproduction.

BSM: The masked signals $`\tilde{X}_{\mathit{d}}(t,f)`$ are decoded to
binaural signals by the fixed BSM filters, detailed in Sec. 2.3. Note
that this BSM filter is common to each variation detailed next in
Sec. 3.2.

### 3.2 Variations of the Proposed Method

In this paper the proposed method is studied alongside several
variations as detailed next.  
Fusion: This is the main method, as detailed in Sec. 3.1.  
Center: sets $`\boldsymbol{\delta}=\mathbf{0}`$ in Eq. (5), as used
originally in \[28\] and detailed in Sec. 2.2, and applies this single
mask to each microphone via Eq. (8).  
Per-Mic: this variation skips the Fusion stage, such that the $`D`$
masks $`M_{\mathit{d}}(t,f)`$ are applied individually to each
microphone,
$`\tilde{X}_{\mathit{d}}(t,f)=M_{\mathit{d}}(t,f)X_{\mathit{d}}(t,f)`$,
therefore not sharing a common mask. This configuration may not preserve
the inter-channel relations, but may improve speech enhancement due to
the microphone-specific masks.

### 3.3 Baselines

Two methods are used as baselines for comparing to the proposed method
and its variants. Both use the same FT-JNF architecture as the proposed
method, but trained directly on the $`D`$ microphone signals, extended
to a $`D`$-mask MIMO output, one mask per microphone, in two different
variations, as detailed below. Both baselines are trained on a training
set of various array geometries.  
MIMO FT-JNF applies a separate mask to each channel, the baseline
analogue of _Per-Mic_.  
MIMO FT-JNF (Fusion) averages the $`D`$ masks by a complex mean before
applying them, which is the baseline analogue of _Fusion_.

### 4.1 Setup

Simulated acoustics scenes are generated with a target speaker and five
interfering speakers recorded by an array in a reverberant room,
simulated using the image method, with varying acoustic conditions as
detailed in \[29\]. Clean speech signals are drawn from the WSJ0
corpus \[8\]. Twenty simulated arrays of \[29\], together with a
simulated model of the Aria glasses array, include linear, planar and
volumetric arrays of various configurations, confined to a sphere of
radius 0.1m, each with $`D=7`$ microphones. These are simulated using an
Ambisonics representation of the sound field of order $`N=20`$ and
include both free-field and rigid-sphere steering formulations. The
inference dataset comprises 100 examples per array, and is disjoint from
the training and validation sets.

### 4.2 Methodology and Metrics

In the realization of the proposed method and its variants, the network
of Sec. 2.4 is used unmodified, composed of a two-layer 64-unit BLSTM
with a complex-mask output head, 142,594 parameters and channel dropout
of up to three channels at probability $`0.4`$, with all parameters
identical to \[29\]. For these methods, microphone signals are
preprocessed with an STFT using a $`32\,`$ms Hamming window and 50%
overlap, and encoded by microphone-aligned ASM (Sec. 3.1) as a
Tikhonov-regularized least-squares solution (Eq. 3), with a
regularization per frequency as in \[19\]. _Fusion_ is evaluated over
the $`D`$ microphone positions, Eq. (7). The BSM filters (Sec. 3.1)
follow the same regularized solution (Eq. 3), with the magnitude
least-squares crossover frequency set as in \[19\]. These filters are
designed once per array and reused unchanged for each of the methods.

The two baselines of Sec. 3.3 share the network hyperparameters above
and the same negative SI-SDR objective as in \[29\], with one scaling
shared across all $`D`$ microphones. They are trained with the 10
training arrays of \[29\] and evaluated on those as well as on the 10
test arrays together with the simulated Aria array (Sec. 4.3).

The reference binaural signals are rendered from the full $`N=20`$ order
Ambisonics representation of the scene using the Neumann KU 100
HRTF \[2\]. Two such references are used: the _reference binaural
signal_, rendered from the full reverberant scene, and the _direct
binaural signal_, rendered from the direct-path component alone.
Performance is reported by the following metrics. SI-SDR is measured by
the SI-SDR \[26\] of the binaural signals, with respect to the reference
binaural signal, averaged over both ears, as are PESQ \[25\] and
STOI \[27\]. SI-SDR-Dir measures the distortion caused by the mask to
the direct sound from the sources, and is the SI-SDR of the masked
direct-path signal, with respect to the direct binaural signal, averaged
over both ears. $`\Delta`$C50 is measured by the absolute error of the
clarity index $`C_{50}`$ \[14\] of the masked reverberant target signal
with respect to that of the reference binaural signal. Interaural level
difference (ILD) \[32\] and interaural coherence (IC) \[3\] are measured
from the binaural covariances of the enhanced binaural signal, and
evaluated against the same measures computed from the direct binaural
signal and the reference binaural signal, respectively, leading to
$`\Delta`$ILD and $`\Delta`$IC. They are computed as the RMSE over
frequency after time alignment.

### 4.3 Results

Table 1 reports the mean over the 100 examples of each array. _Fusion_
attains the highest SI-SDR, PESQ, STOI and SI-SDR-Dir, and the lowest
$`\Delta`$IC. This shows that _Fusion_, by averaging the _Per-Mic_ mask
estimates and using a shared mask, reduces binaural errors, as it leaves
the inter-channel relations that BSM exploits in its design intact, so
both enhancement and interaural cues improve. _Center_ with its single
mask falls only shortly behind, showing that averaging the _Per-Mic_
masks is slightly better than using one central mask. An additional
study not presented in the table investigated the addition of three more
masks to the averaging of _Fusion_, which are centered at the two ears
and the center of the head, but this improved the SI-SDR by at most
$`0.1\,`$dB when averaged over all arrays, and so these positions were
eventually excluded from _Fusion_.

_Per-Mic_ behaves as anticipated in Sec. 3.2, with its $`\Delta`$ILD,
SI-SDR-Dir, and $`\Delta`$IC degrade compared to both _Fusion_ and
_Center_. In particular, SI-SDR-Dir degrades to $`-3.23\,`$dB, probably
because the independent per-microphone masks modify the channels by
different complex gains and break the relations BSM assumed in its
design. It nonetheless attains the lowest $`\Delta C_{50}`$, although
the differences to the other methods are not large (about $`0.5\,`$dB).

Table 1 also includes the two baselines of Sec. 3.3, trained on only 10
of the 21 arrays (Sec. 4.2) but evaluated here, like the proposed
method’s variants, on the full 21-array set. Both collapse well below
_Fusion_ on every column, several going negative on SI-SDR and
SI-SDR-Dir: fitting a mask directly to the microphone domain does not
generalize the way the array-agnostic Ambisonics-domain design does, as
was already shown in \[28\].

Note that although the values of SI-SDR are not very high for all
methods, they are close to that of BSM Clean, which incorporates both
residual noise and distortion of the binaural signal, and indicate a
high standing reference point. In conclusion, the proposed _Fusion_
showed the feasibility of extending the previously proposed AmbiDrop
method for array-agnostic speech enhancement to binaural reproduction
without any additional training - simply by applying the trained network
to ASM computed around each microphone position.

## 5 Conclusions

BAmbiDrop extends array-agnostic single-channel speech enhancement to
binaural reproduction. Relocating the Ambisonics origin allows a frozen
AmbiDrop network, trained only on ideal Ambisonics, to provide a mask at
any point in space; fusing the masks estimated across the array into one
shared complex mask and rendering the masked microphone signals with
fixed BSM filters then yields a binaural output with no component
retrained. This configuration improved both enhancement and interaural
cue reproduction, whereas masking each microphone independently degraded
the binaural signals. Future work is proposed to address an off-axis and
dynamic target speaker, improving the practical aspects of the method.
