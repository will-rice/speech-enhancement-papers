###### Abstract

Signal-dependent beamformers are advantageous over signal-independent
beamformers when the acoustic scenario - be it real-world or simulated -
is straightforward in terms of the number of sound sources, the ambient
sound field and their dynamics. However, in the context of augmented
reality audio using head-worn microphone arrays, the acoustic scenarios
encountered are often far from straightforward. The design of robust,
high-performance, adaptive beamformers for such scenarios is an on-going
challenge. This is due to the violation of the typically required
assumptions on the noise field caused by, for example, rapid variations
resulting from complex acoustic environments, and/or rotations of the
listener’s head. This work proposes a multi-channel speech enhancement
algorithm which utilises the adaptability of signal-dependent
beamformers while still benefiting from the computational efficiency and
robust performance of signal-independent super-directive beamformers.
The algorithm has two stages. (i) The first stage is a hybrid beamformer
based on a dictionary of weights corresponding to a set of noise field
models. (ii) The second stage is a wide-band subspace post-filter to
remove any artifacts resulting from (i). The algorithm is evaluated
using both real-world recordings and simulations of a cocktail-party
scenario. Noise suppression, intelligibility and speech quality results
show a significant performance improvement by the proposed algorithm
compared to the baseline super-directive beamformer. A data-driven
implementation of the noise field dictionary is shown to provide more
noise suppression, and similar speech intelligibility and quality,
compared to a parametric dictionary.

## I Introduction

Colin Cherry was the first to identify the ‘cocktail party problem’ as
the task of enhancing one target signal, or voice, in the presence of
multiple interfering signals and noise \[[1](#bib.bib1),
[2](#bib.bib2)\]. Despite advances in speech enhancement and microphone
array processing, the cocktail party problem still remains a challenge
in the audio signal processing community after decades of research
\[[2](#bib.bib2), [3](#bib.bib3), [4](#bib.bib4)\]. Relevant
applications include teleconferencing, speech recognition in
human-machine interactions, hearing aids, and augmented reality audio.

It is widely known that significant enhancement can be achieved using
the acoustic signals captured with a microphone array device
\[[5](#bib.bib5), [6](#bib.bib6), [7](#bib.bib7), [8](#bib.bib8)\]. The
use of wearable microphone arrays is a beguiling approach towards
addressing the cocktail party problem. Wearable arrays, such as AR (AR)
glasses \[[3](#bib.bib3)\], can additionally provide head/array position
and orientation information, as well as source DOA and/or other
environmental information obtained using a camera or other sensors.
However, the compact size and real-world implementation of such systems
restricts the processing to be computationally inexpensive, causal,
real-time and robust to unforeseen scenarios. This paper addresses
scenarios with one or more target talkers in the presence of localized
interferers and ambient noise, using a head-worn microphone array
mounted in AR glasses. It is assumed in the following that the array’s
ATF and the target DOA are known with realistic accuracy.

Multichannel speech enhancement systems \[[9](#bib.bib9)\] typically
employ MISO (MISO) algorithms exploiting spatial, temporal and spectral
information to improve the SNR at the output. The potential for
additional improvement using SISO (SISO) post-processing, such as the
Wiener filter \[[10](#bib.bib10)\], spectral subtraction
\[[11](#bib.bib11)\] or DL (DL)-based methods \[[12](#bib.bib12),
[13](#bib.bib13)\] is recognized but not studied here; the focus in this
work is the MISO block in such systems. Without loss of generality, a
multi-target scenario is considered as multiple single-target scenarios
in which the same MISO method can be applied. Such decomposition of the
scenario is known to improve the enhancement performance due to the use
of fewer distortionless target constraints and correspondingly increase
the potential for noise suppression. In addition, it enables control of
the mixture of enhanced sources in the output depending on the
application.

In this work, a novel analytical beamforming algorithm is proposed which
is causal and able to adapt to complex and non-isotropic noise fields
while being computationally efficient due to the use of pre-calculated
weights. The dictionary of precalculated beamformer weights is shown to
have the flexibility of being analytical (parametric) or data-driven.
Early work on this topic was described in \[[14](#bib.bib14)\]. Here,
the work has been extended to include a new formulation, the data-driven
dictionary, more extensive evaluations and mathematical analysis of the
method.

## II Background Review

Beamforming algorithms spatially filter a sound field with the aim of
preserving signals from the target direction(s) while minimizing the
power of all other signals in the output.

### II-A Classical Beamforming Algorithms

The implementation of classical beamformers is characterized by a
filter-and-sum structure, either in the time domain or STFT (STFT)
domain, and is of relatively low computational complexity
\[[15](#bib.bib15)\]. Signal-independent beamformers, such as
delay-and-sum \[[16](#bib.bib16)\] and fixed versions of MVDR (MVDR)
\[[17](#bib.bib17)\] use static, possibly switched, precalculated filter
coefficients. On the other hand, adaptive beamformers such as MVDR or
LCMV (LCMV) \[[18](#bib.bib18)\] with dynamic covariance input can
potentially improve performance by adapting their spatial filtering
characteristics to a particular, and possibly time-varying, acoustic
scenario. Such signal-dependent beamformers rely on the use of a SCM
(SCM) or NCM (NCM), resulting in the MPDR (MPDR)¹¹ 1 We note that
beamformer nomenclature varies and that the term MVDR is used by some
authors for either or both of the MPDR and MVDR beamformers.
\[[19](#bib.bib19), [17](#bib.bib17)\] or MVDR beamformers,
respectively. The MVDR approach is often preferred due to real-world
inaccuracies in the steering vector causing potential performance
degradation in MPDR because of target signal cancellation
\[[20](#bib.bib20), [21](#bib.bib21)\].

A common and robust choice for the NCM in MVDR implementations is a
signal-independent model. Examples include the matched-filter beamformer
\[[22](#bib.bib22), [5](#bib.bib5)\] in which the NCM is a multiple of
the identity matrix, and the superdirective beamformer
\[[23](#bib.bib23)\] in which the NCM is the covariance matrix
corresponding to a spherically or cylindrically isotropic diffuse noise
field. The use of a signal-dependent NCM potentially improves spatial
filtering performance if it is estimated accurately. Some such adaptive
beamformers aim to estimate NCM either directly during periods when the
target signal is inactive, or indirectly by subtracting the target
contribution from the SCM. Both these approaches require identification
of TF (TF) bins containing target signal activity using, for example,
SAD (SAD) or SPP (SPP) methods \[[15](#bib.bib15)\].

In \[[24](#bib.bib24)\], the SCM is decomposed into isotropic, identity
and PW (PW) components followed by removal of any (near-) target PW from
the modelled SCM to avoid signal cancellation. Although some
signal-dependent beamformers are shown \[[24](#bib.bib24)\] to perform
better than signal-independent beamformers, such evaluations are
typically limited to simulations or simple-scene real-recordings. These
typically avoid the real-world complexities of the cocktail party
problem with wearable arrays such as rapid dynamics of the noise field
due to head rotation or the presence of non-isotropic noise fields.

### II-B Deep Learning Approaches

Typical DL-based MISO speech enhancement methods use neural networks to
estimate either the output (end-to-end methods) \[[25](#bib.bib25)\], or
other information such as the NCM or beamformer weights (‘neural
beamforming’) \[[26](#bib.bib26)\]. In \[[27](#bib.bib27),
[28](#bib.bib28), [29](#bib.bib29), [30](#bib.bib30)\], real- or
complex-valued TF ratio masks are estimated while in \[[31](#bib.bib31),
[32](#bib.bib32)\] the TF bins dominated by noise are identified to
estimate the NCM for use in MVDR beamforming. The work in
\[[33](#bib.bib33)\] employs a U-Net architecture to directly estimate
complex-valued beamforming weights. An extension of this work using
DCCRN \[[12](#bib.bib12)\] is also proposed in \[[34](#bib.bib34)\].
Similarly to other neural beamformers, FaSNet \[[35](#bib.bib35)\] aims
to estimate the beamformer filters but in the time-domain using a
temporal convolutional network. Although the evaluation results from
some of these methods are exceptionally good, these systems are limited
to offline and/or high-latency applications due to non-causality and
relatively heavy computational load. Methods such as \[[33](#bib.bib33),
[35](#bib.bib35)\] are causal and designed for low-latency purposes.
However, so far, they have not been evaluated in real-world, unseen
cocktail party scenarios. In addition, DL-based methods are typically
trained for only a particular architecture, input format, sample rate,
and array geometry. In order to handle extra inputs, such as head
tracking information or changes in the above configurations, the models
usually require re-training and, in some cases, re-designing.

## III Formulation and Baseline Beamformer

For notational simplicity, the formulation is provided for a
single-channel output. However, the same procedure can be repeated
assuming different reference channels for multi-channel output.

### III-A Signals in the STFT domain

Let $`Y_{q}(\nu,\ell)\in\mathbb{C}`$ denote the signal at microphone
index $`q`$, frequency index $`\nu`$ and time-frame index $`\ell`$.
Using a PW representation, this can be expressed as

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
Y_{q}(\nu,\ell)=\sum_{j=1}^{J}X_{q,j}(\nu,\ell)+V_{q}(\nu,\ell),
``` |  | (1) |

where $`X_{q,j}\in\mathbb{C}`$ and $`V_{q}\in\mathbb{C}`$ are,
respectively, the $`j`$-th plane-waves with unique DOA, and the sensor
noise at microphone $`q`$. For brevity, the $`(\nu,\ell)`$ dependency
will be omitted in the following when unambiguous.

Defining $`\mathbf{y}=[Y_{1}\ldots Y_{Q}]^{T}\in\mathbb{C}^{Q\times 1}`$
as the vector of noisy microphone signals, the beamformer output is

|     |                             |     |     |
|-----|-----------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       Z=\mathbf{w}^{H}\mathbf{y},
       ```                          |     | (2) |

where $`\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{C}^{Q\times 1}`$ is the vector of
beamformer weights and $`(.)^{H}`$ indicates Hermitian transpose.

### III-B MVDR

In the MVDR beamformer design, the weights are given by
\[[17](#bib.bib17)\]

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathbf{w}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbf{R}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\mathbf{d}}{\mathbf{d}^{H}\mathbf{R}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\mathbf{d}}`$ |  | (3) |
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathrm{with}\quad\mathbf{R}_{\varepsilon}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\hat{\mathbf{R}}_{\mathcal{N}}+\varepsilon\mathbf{I}`$ |  | (4) |
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathrm{and}\quad\varepsilon`$ | $`\displaystyle=\max\left(\frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}-\kappa_{0}\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}}{\kappa_{0}-1},\,0\right),`$ |  | (5) |

where
$`\mathbf{d}=\tilde{\mathbf{h}}(\Omega_{\text{s}})\in\mathbb{C}^{Q\times 1}`$
is the steering vector for the target DOA $`\Omega_{\text{s}}`$, and
$`\tilde{\mathbf{h}}(\cdot)`$ is the array’s RTF (RTF) that is
determined as the array’s ATF $`\mathbf{h}(\cdot)`$ normalized by the
reference output channel. The
$`\hat{\mathbf{R}}_{\mathcal{N}}\in\mathbb{C}^{Q\times Q}`$ is an
estimate of the NCM, $`\mathbf{I}\in\mathbb{C}^{Q\times Q}`$ is the
identity matrix, $`\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}`$ and
$`\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}`$ are the largest and smallest eigenvalues of
$`\hat{\mathbf{R}}_{\mathcal{N}}`$, and $`\kappa_{0}`$ is the maximum
permitted condition number of $`\mathbf{R}_{\varepsilon}`$ and,
indirectly, constrains the norm of $`\mathbf{w}`$ and the sensitivity of
the beamformer to errors in $`\mathbf{d}`$ \[[36](#bib.bib36),
[37](#bib.bib37)\].

### III-C Isotropic-MVDR (Superdirective) Beamformer

The baseline beamformer in this study is chosen to be the Isotropic-MVDR
referred to as ‘Iso’ (also known as Maximum Directivity), in which a
static, spherically diffuse sound field is assumed for the
$`\hat{\mathbf{R}}_{\mathcal{N}}`$ in
([4](#S3.E4 "In III-B MVDR ‣ III Formulation and Baseline Beamformer ‣ Subspace Hybrid MVDR Beamforming for Augmented Hearing")).
This is equivalent to assuming the presence of uncorrelated interference
with equal power from all directions. The spherically isotropic diffuse
covariance matrix can be obtained as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{\gamma}}=\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{h}(\Omega){\mathbf{h}^{H}}(\Omega)d\Omega,
``` |  | (6) |

where
$`\int_{\Omega}d\Omega=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\sin(\theta)d\theta d\varphi`$
denotes integration along azimuth $`\varphi\in[0,2\pi)`$ and inclination
$`\theta\in[0,\pi]`$.

In the case of using a discrete set of $`\Omega\in\mathcal{I}`$ from a
uniform grid of directions,
([6](#S3.E6 "In III-C Isotropic- (Superdirective) Beamformer ‣ III Formulation and Baseline Beamformer ‣ Subspace Hybrid MVDR Beamforming for Augmented Hearing"))
can be approximated by quadrature weighting giving

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbf{R}_{\text{Iso}}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}q_{i}\mathbf{h}(\Omega_{i}){\mathbf{h}^{H}}(\Omega_{i}),
``` |  | (7) |

where $`q_{i}\in\mathbb{R}`$ is the quadrature weight for each sample
direction of $`\mathbf{h}`$ given by \[[38](#bib.bib38)\] as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
q_{i}=\frac{2\sin\theta_{i}}{N_{\varphi}N_{\theta}}\sum_{p=0}^{\lceil 0.5N_{\theta}-1\rceil}\frac{\sin\left(\left(2p+1\right)\theta_{i}\right)}{2p+1},
``` |  | (8) |

in which $`\theta_{i}`$ is the inclination of sample point $`i`$ and the
number of sample points in azimuth and inclination are $`N_{\varphi}`$
and $`N_{\theta}`$ respectively.

Note that the quadrature weighting is done to preserve the uniform power
isotropy by compensating for the higher density of points closer to the
poles in a uniform grid spatial sampling scheme. The use of other
spatial sampling schemes may require no or different weighting. Using
$`\hat{\mathbf{R}}_{\mathcal{N}}=\mathbf{R}_{\text{Iso}}`$ in
([4](#S3.E4 "In III-B MVDR ‣ III Formulation and Baseline Beamformer ‣ Subspace Hybrid MVDR Beamforming for Augmented Hearing"))
and substituting into
([2](#S3.E2 "In III-A Signals in the domain ‣ III Formulation and Baseline Beamformer ‣ Subspace Hybrid MVDR Beamforming for Augmented Hearing")),
the output of this beamformer is denoted as $`Z_{\text{Iso}}`$.

## IV Proposed Method

![Refer to caption](2311.18689v1/graphics/block6.png)

Fig. 1: The proposed system block diagram.

As illustrated in Fig.
[1](#S4.F1 "Fig. 1 ‣ IV Proposed Method ‣ Subspace Hybrid MVDR Beamforming for Augmented Hearing"),
the proposed method consists of two stages. In the first stage
(Hybrid-MVDR), multiple MVDR beamformers are implemented, each with a
different precalculated $`\hat{\mathbf{R}}_{\mathcal{N}}`$, including
isotropic and non-isotropic noise field models. The first stage outputs
two signals: $`Z_{\text{Iso}}`$ being the result of Iso and
$`Z_{\text{Hyb}}`$ being the output from the Hybrid-MVDR beamforming.
The second stage applies wideband inter-method PCA (PCA). This processes
the two full-band spectral outputs from the first stage in order to
remove the ‘musical noise’ generated in the output of the Hybrid-MVDR.

### IV-A Hybrid-MVDR

Multiple MVDR beamformers are implemented with the same steering vector
but various NCM taken from a dictionary of predefined and precalculated
noise field models. The dictionary is denoted

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{D}=\{\mathbf{R}_{m,\nu}\rightarrow\mathbf{w}_{m,\nu,\Omega}\}\quad\text{for}\quad\forall\nu\quad\text{and}\quad\Omega\in\mathcal{I}_{s}
``` |  | (9) |

and contains the pre-calculated beamformer weights
$`\mathbf{w}_{m,\nu,\Omega}\in\mathbb{C}^{Q\times 1}`$. The weights are
derived using
([3](#S3.E3 "In III-B MVDR ‣ III Formulation and Baseline Beamformer ‣ Subspace Hybrid MVDR Beamforming for Augmented Hearing"))
for $`m=1,\dots,M`$ NCM models, $`\mathbf{R}_{\varepsilon}`$, and a
discrete set $`\mathcal{I}_{s}`$ of possible steering directions
$`\Omega`$. Note that the dictionary
$`\mathcal{D}\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times F\times Q\times\Psi}`$ is a
$`4`$-dimensional table where $`M`$, $`F`$, $`Q`$ and $`\Psi`$ are
respectively the total number of NCM models, frequency bands,
microphones and steering directions.

The detailed choices of the NCM models in the dictionary $`\mathcal{D}`$
will be discussed in Section
[V](#S5 "V Dictionary ‣ Subspace Hybrid MVDR Beamforming for Augmented Hearing").
For simplicity and as a necessity for the description of this section,
it should be noted that the dictionary $`\mathcal{D}`$ includes the
isotropic model, $`\mathbf{R}_{\text{Iso}}`$ in
([7](#S3.E7 "In III-C Isotropic- (Superdirective) Beamformer ‣ III Formulation and Baseline Beamformer ‣ Subspace Hybrid MVDR Beamforming for Augmented Hearing")),
as well as other models to be specified later.

At each TF bin $`(\nu,\ell)`$, and for a known steering direction
$`\Omega_{\text{s}}(\ell)`$, the Hybrid-MVDR in Fig.
[1](#S4.F1 "Fig. 1 ‣ IV Proposed Method ‣ Subspace Hybrid MVDR Beamforming for Augmented Hearing")
implements multiple MVDR beamformers using precalculated weights
associated with the closest steering direction
$`\Omega_{\text{s}}(\ell)`$ stored in $`\mathcal{D}`$. The output with
the minimum power among the models is selected and denoted as

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  |  | $`\displaystyle Z_{\text{Hyb}}=(\mathbf{w}_{j})^{H}\mathbf{y},`$ |  | (10) |
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathrm{with\quad}`$ | $`\displaystyle j=\argmin_{m}{\{\lVert(\mathbf{w}_{m})^{H}\mathbf{y}\rVert^{2}\}}\quad\forall\mathbf{w}_{m}\in\mathcal{D}(\Omega_{\text{s}}).`$ |  | (11) |

The isotropic model is included in $`\mathcal{D}`$ and the corresponding
beamformer $`Z_{\text{Iso}}`$ is output to the next stage as shown in
Fig. [1](#S4.F1 "Fig. 1 ‣ IV Proposed Method ‣ Subspace Hybrid MVDR Beamforming for Augmented Hearing").

As will be shown in
Section [VI](#S6 "VI Evaluations ‣ Subspace Hybrid MVDR Beamforming for Augmented Hearing"),
although the Hybrid beamformer typically results in stronger acoustic
noise reduction compared to Iso, the output may contain ‘musical noise’
due to rapid switching of beam pattern between neighbouring time-frames
and frequencies. To suppress this musical noise, which is assumed to be
sufficiently uncorrelated with the acoustic noise, the second stage
extracts the component of the Hybrid-MVDR output which is correlated
with the Iso-MVDR output.

### IV-B Wideband inter-method PCA

In this stage, a solution is proposed for the removal of the musical
noise in the $`Z_{\text{Hyb}}`$ while preserving its stronger noise
suppression compared to $`Z_{\text{Iso}}`$. This section formulates the
proposed solution whereas the Appendix provides a corresponding
theoretical analysis.

Defining $`\check{(.)}`$ as the vector of full-band spectrum, let
$`\check{\mathbf{z}}_{\text{Iso}}(\ell)=[Z_{\text{Iso}}(1,\ell),\dots,Z_{\text{Iso}}(F,\ell)]\in\mathbb{C}^{1\times F}`$
and
$`\check{\mathbf{z}}_{\text{Hyb}}(\ell)=[Z_{\text{Hyb}}(1,\ell),\dots,Z_{\text{Hyb}}(F,\ell)]\in\mathbb{C}^{1\times F}`$
denote the full-band spectral outputs of Iso and Hybrid-MVDR beamformers
at time-frame $`\ell`$, which are then concatenated to form a
two-channel wideband data matrix $`\mathbf{Z}\in\mathbb{C}^{2\times F}`$
defined as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbf{Z}(\ell)=\begin{bmatrix}\check{\mathbf{z}}_{\text{Hyb}}(\ell)\\
\check{\mathbf{z}}_{\text{Iso}}(\ell)\end{bmatrix}.
``` |  | (12) |

The inter-method wideband covariance matrix
$`\mathbf{R}_{\text{z}}(\ell)\in\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2}`$ is then defined
as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbf{R}_{\text{z}}(\ell)=\mathbb{E}\{\mathbf{Z}(\ell)\mathbf{Z}(\ell)^{H}\},
``` |  | (13) |

where $`\mathbb{E}\{.\}`$ is the expectation operator. An estimate of
$`\mathbf{R}_{\text{z}}`$ in
([13](#S4.E13 "In IV-B Wideband inter-method PCA ‣ IV Proposed Method ‣ Subspace Hybrid MVDR Beamforming for Augmented Hearing"))
can be obtained by applying an EMA (EMA) process to the instantaneous
inter-method covariance matrix as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\hat{\mathbf{R}}_{\text{z}}(\ell)=\alpha\hat{\mathbf{R}}_{\text{z}}(\ell-1)+(1-\alpha)\mathbf{Z}(\ell)\mathbf{Z}(\ell)^{H},
``` |  | (14) |

where $`0<\alpha=e^{-\Delta t/T}<1`$ is the smoothing factor,
$`\Delta t`$ is the STFT time-frame step, and $`T`$ is a forgetting time
constant.

Using EVD (EVD),

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\hat{\mathbf{R}}_{\text{z}}(\ell)=\mathbf{U}(\ell)\mathbf{\Sigma}(\ell)\mathbf{U}^{-1}(\ell),
``` |  | (15) |

where $`\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2}`$ and
$`\mathbf{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}`$ are, respectively, the
eigenvectors and diagonal matrix of eigenvalues. Assuming the columns of
$`\mathbf{\Sigma}`$ are sorted in descending order of eigenvalues, the
first column of
$`\mathbf{U}(\ell)=[\mathbf{U}_{S}(\ell),\mathbf{U}_{N}(\ell)]`$ is
considered to be the signal eigenvector denoted as
$`\mathbf{U}_{S}(\ell)\in\mathbb{C}^{2\times 1}`$.

The SS (SS) of the $`\mathbf{Z}(\ell)`$ is reconstructed as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbf{Z}_{\text{SS}}(\ell)=\mathbf{U}_{S}(\ell)\mathbf{U}_{S}^{H}(\ell)\mathbf{Z}(\ell),
``` |  | (16) |

where the first row of
$`\mathbf{Z}_{\text{SS}}(\ell)=[\check{\mathbf{z}}_{\text{SS-Hyb}}(\ell)^{T},\check{\mathbf{z}}_{\text{SS-Iso}}(\ell)^{T}]^{T}\in\mathbb{C}^{2\times F}`$
is the subspace-Hybrid (SS-Hybrid) output. In a compact form, the final
output spectrum can be written in terms of weighted mixing of
$`\check{\mathbf{z}}_{\text{Iso}}`$ and
$`\check{\mathbf{z}}_{\text{Hyb}}`$ spectra as

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\check{\mathbf{z}}_{\text{SS-Hyb}}(\ell)=\beta_{\text{Hyb}}(\ell)\check{\mathbf{z}}_{\text{Hyb}}(\ell)+\beta_{\text{Iso}}(\ell)\check{\mathbf{z}}_{\text{Iso}}(\ell),`$ |  | (17) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\beta_{\text{Hyb}}(\ell)=U_{1}U^{*}_{1},`$ |  | (18) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\beta_{\text{Iso}}(\ell)=U_{1}U^{*}_{2},`$ |  | (19) |

where the $`U_{1}`$ and $`U_{2}`$ are respectively the first and the
second element in the subspace eigenvector
$`\mathbf{U}_{S}(\ell)=[U_{1},U_{2}]^{T}`$ and $`(\cdot)^{*}`$ denotes
the complex conjugate. A mathematical analysis of $`\mathbf{Z}`$ and
$`\hat{\mathbf{R}}_{\text{z}}`$ in terms of the target and residual
noise from each beamformer is given in the Appendix.

## V Dictionary

This section proposes two alternative dictionary designs $`\mathcal{D}`$
for the Hybrid-MVDR: (A) Parametric or (B) Data-driven. The parametric
dictionary consists of NCM models $`\mathbf{R}`$ that are derived using
array ATF and a set of pre-defined noise field power distributions. The
data-driven dictionary includes NCM models derived from a set of array
recordings assuming the ground truth noise recordings are available.
Details of these two categories are discussed next.

![Refer to caption](2311.18689v1/graphics/aniso.png)

Fig. 2: (Left) Isotropic and horizontally unimodal Anisotropic models

### V-A Parametric Dictionary

A parametric dictionary can be formed by considering both spatially
correlated and uncorrelated NCM models. An example of a spatially
uncorrelated noise model is sensor noise that has no inter-channel
correlation, whereas an example of a spatially correlated noise model is
environmental noise with arbitrary isotropy.

It can be noted that, for spatially uncorrelated models, the NCM,
$`\mathbf{R}`$, is directly formulated whereas for the spatially
correlated models, the formulation is done for the noise field isotropy
$`\mathcal{P}(\Omega)`$, which then can be used along with the available
ATF to obtain the NCM model $`\mathbf{R}`$ as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbf{R}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\mathcal{P}(\Omega_{i})q_{i}\mathbf{h}(\Omega_{i}){\mathbf{h}^{H}}(\Omega_{i}).
``` |  | (20) |

#### V-A1 Identity (Spatially uncorrelated model)

This model results in a diagonal NCM where the diagonal values represent
the relative noise power at each sensor. Assuming a spatially and
spectrally uniform (white) sensor noise power, this model is given as

|     |                                          |     |      |
|-----|------------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       \mathbf{R}_{\text{Identity}}=\mathbf{I},
       ```                                       |     | (21) |

where $`\mathbf{I}\in\mathbb{C}^{Q\times Q}`$ is the identity matrix.
Note that the identity matrix can be replaced with a diagonal matrix
with non-uniform values if the array sensors’ noise powers are
available, non-uniform and spectrally non-white.

Fig. 3: Rows: Identity, Iso, unimodal Aniso and PW models with
$`\varphi_{\text{p}}=90^{\circ}`$. Columns: NCM 2D isotropy (horizontal

inclination), narrowband and wideband (a-weighted) beam patterns
(horizontal plane) with steering direction
$`\Omega_{\text{s}}=(\varphi_{\text{s}},\theta_{\text{s}})=(0,90)^{\circ}`$.

#### V-A2 Isotropic model

The spherically isotropic model has uniform isotropy across all
directions and is given as

|     |                                     |     |      |
|-----|-------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       \mathcal{P}_{\text{Iso}}(\Omega)=1.
       ```                                  |     | (22) |

Substituting
([22](#S5.E22 "In V-A2 Isotropic model ‣ V-A Parametric Dictionary ‣ V Dictionary ‣ Subspace Hybrid MVDR Beamforming for Augmented Hearing"))
into
([20](#S5.E20 "In V-A Parametric Dictionary ‣ V Dictionary ‣ Subspace Hybrid MVDR Beamforming for Augmented Hearing"))
results in $`\mathbf{R}_{\text{Iso}}`$, which is equivalent to the
$`\mathbf{R}`$ used in
([7](#S3.E7 "In III-C Isotropic- (Superdirective) Beamformer ‣ III Formulation and Baseline Beamformer ‣ Subspace Hybrid MVDR Beamforming for Augmented Hearing"))
for the superdirective beamformer in
Section [III-C](#S3.SS3 "III-C Isotropic- (Superdirective) Beamformer ‣ III Formulation and Baseline Beamformer ‣ Subspace Hybrid MVDR Beamforming for Augmented Hearing").

#### V-A3 Anisotropic models

Anisotropic models can be defined in infinitely many ways. However, in
this work, only horizontally unimodal (single-peak) isotropy is
considered. Assuming a linear function for horizontal unimodality, the
anisotropic model with a mode azimuth $`\varphi_{\text{p}}`$ and power
dynamic range $`\mathcal{A}`$ (in decibels) is given as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{P}_{\text{Aniso}}(\Omega)=10^{-\mathcal{A}|\angle(\varphi,\varphi_{\text{p}})|/(10\pi)},
``` |  | (23) |

where $`\angle(\cdot\,,\cdot)`$ is the angular difference in radians
between two directions wrapped to $`[-\pi,\pi]`$. Note that the
linearity of isotropy is on the logarithmic scale for the power,
independent of the inclination $`\theta`$ and symmetric around the mode
azimuth $`\varphi_{\text{p}}`$. The $`\mathbf{R}_{\text{Aniso}}`$ can be
obtained by substituting
([23](#S5.E23 "In V-A3 Anisotropic models ‣ V-A Parametric Dictionary ‣ V Dictionary ‣ Subspace Hybrid MVDR Beamforming for Augmented Hearing"))
into
([20](#S5.E20 "In V-A Parametric Dictionary ‣ V Dictionary ‣ Subspace Hybrid MVDR Beamforming for Augmented Hearing")).
Figure [2](#S5.F2 "Fig. 2 ‣ V Dictionary ‣ Subspace Hybrid MVDR Beamforming for Augmented Hearing")
illustrates the horizontally unimodal anisotropic noise field models for
a few examples with fixed $`\varphi_{\text{p}}`$ and varying
$`\mathcal{A}`$ (left), as well as varying $`\varphi_{\text{p}}`$ and
fixed $`\mathcal{A}`$ (right).

#### V-A4 Plane-wave models

The PW model represents the effect of a single PW with DOA
$`\Omega_{\text{p}}`$ in matrix $`\mathbf{R}`$. This is formulated as

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\text{PW}}(\Omega)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1&\mathrm{for}\quad\Omega=\Omega_{\text{p}}\\
0&\mathrm{otherwise}\end{cases}.`$ |  | (24) |

Empirical analysis suggested the removal of PW models from our
implementations and evaluations. They were shown to be the most
frequently chosen model in the Hybrid-MVDR. This commonly resulted in a
failure to suppress the reverberation during target activity, due to the
corresponding beam-pattern caused by the extreme directionality of PW
model, as also shown in
Fig. [3](#S5.F3 "Fig. 3 ‣ V-A1 Identity (Spatially uncorrelated model) ‣ V-A Parametric Dictionary ‣ V Dictionary ‣ Subspace Hybrid MVDR Beamforming for Augmented Hearing").
An evaluation using PW models in the dictionary is presented in
\[[14](#bib.bib14)\].

Figure [3](#S5.F3 "Fig. 3 ‣ V-A1 Identity (Spatially uncorrelated model) ‣ V-A Parametric Dictionary ‣ V Dictionary ‣ Subspace Hybrid MVDR Beamforming for Augmented Hearing")
shows examples for the 1) Identity, 2) Iso, 3) unimodal Aniso and 4) PW
models (with $`\varphi_{\text{p}}=90^{\circ}`$ for Aniso and PW) with
their assumed 2D and 3D power distributions of NCM. Additionally shown

direction
$`\Omega_{\text{s}}=(\varphi_{\text{s}},\theta_{\text{s}})=(0,90)^{\circ}`$.
These results were obtained for a 32-element rigid SMA (SMA) with a

sampling of $`3^{\circ}`$ resolution across both azimuth and inclination
for the ATF.

### V-B Data-driven Dictionary

The data-driven dictionary contains NCM models estimated from noise-only
array signals. The noise-only signals can be obtained either from signal
segments during which the target is inactive, and/or exploiting ground
truth noise signals if/when available. The term ‘ground truth noise
signals’ refers to the array signal excluding the anechoic (direct-path)
target. Hence, it includes the sensor noise, ambient and interferer(s)
noise, as well as the target reverberation, since an ideal beamformer is
expected to suppress the target reverberation as well as other sources
of noise. Some ground truth noise signals are assumed to be available
and can be obtained by simulating noisy scenarios using array’s ATF.

Using EMA on the instantaneous ground truth covariance matrix, an
estimate of the ground truth NCM can be derived as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbf{R}_{\text{gt}}(\nu,\ell)=\alpha\mathbf{R}_{\text{gt}}(\nu,\ell-1)+\\
(1-\alpha)(\mathbf{y}_{\text{gt}}(\nu,\ell)\mathbf{y}_{\text{gt}}^{{}^{H}}(\nu,\ell)),
``` |  | (25) |

where $`\mathbf{y}_{\text{gt}}=\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{target}}`$
and $`\mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{target}}\in\mathbb{C}^{Q\times 1}`$ is the
vector of anechoic target signals at the array sensors.

The number of the models $`M`$ has no upper bound, unlike the number of
channels $`Q`$, frequency bands $`F`$ and the steering directions
$`\Psi`$ that are either defined in a wrapped space or imposed by the
choice of the array and the STFT settings. In order to restrict the
number of models $`M`$ without imposing any restriction on the number of
ground truth NCM observations, the set of measured
$`\mathbf{R}_{\text{gt}}`$ are compressed into a fixed number of bases
$`\bar{\mathbf{R}}`$ via k-means clustering, as next described.

#### V-B1 Vectorization of NCM

Since k-means clustering operates on real-valued data, the
complex-valued NCM matrix must be vectorized into real-valued vectors.
The NCM matrix $`\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{C}^{Q\times Q}`$ has symmetric
real values and conjugate complex values around its diagonal. In
addition, the diagonal values are always real. This structure is
utilized to form an efficient mapping between complex-valued matrix form
denoted as $`\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{C}^{Q\times Q}`$ and real-valued
vector form denoted as $`\mathbf{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{Q^{2}\times 1}`$ for
NCM.

Figure [4](#S5.F4 "Fig. 4 ‣ V-B1 Vectorization of ‣ V-B Data-driven Dictionary ‣ V Dictionary ‣ Subspace Hybrid MVDR Beamforming for Augmented Hearing")
shows the process of conversion between the complex-valued matrix and
real-valued vector forms. The complex-valued matrix $`\mathbf{R}`$ is
split into real and imaginary parts followed by the concatenation of
$`Q^{2}`$ unique real values from both real and imaginary matrices into
one real-valued vector $`\mathbf{r}`$. This process is perfectly
reversible.

![Refer to caption](2311.18689v1/graphics/vectorization.png)

Fig. 4: The process of conversion between complex-valued matrix form
$`\mathbf{R}`$ and real-valued vector form $`\mathbf{r}`$ of NCM.

#### V-B2 Clustering

Having converted the arbitrary-size ($`N`$) set of estimated ground
truth NCM
$`\mathfrak{R}_{\nu}=\{\mathbf{R}_{\text{gt}}(\nu,\ell)\}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times Q\times Q}`$
per frequency band $`\nu`$ into its vectorized form
$`\mathfrak{r}_{\nu}=\{\mathbf{r}_{\text{gt}}(\nu,\ell)\}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times Q^{2}}`$,
k-means is then used to cluster
$`\mathfrak{r}_{\nu}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times Q^{2}}`$ into
$`\bar{\mathfrak{r}}_{\nu}=\{\bar{\mathbf{r}}_{\text{gt}}(\nu,m)\}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times Q^{2}}`$
centroids, where the number of clusters $`K=M`$ is set to the total
number of models $`M`$ in the dictionary. The real-valued vectorized
basis NCM models are then converted into complex-valued matrix basis NCM
$`\bar{\mathfrak{R}}_{\nu}=\{\bar{\mathbf{R}}_{\text{gt}}(\nu,m)\}\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times Q\times Q}`$,
which can be used in
([3](#S3.E3 "In III-B MVDR ‣ III Formulation and Baseline Beamformer ‣ Subspace Hybrid MVDR Beamforming for Augmented Hearing"))
to form the data-driven dictionary of beamformer weights.

Note that the use of k-means clustering results in the number of models
being independent of the training set size $`N`$. This enables the use
of a compact dictionary with relatively small $`M`$ for computational
efficiency, while benefiting from training on a large set of signals
with relatively large $`N`$.

## VI Evaluations

The algorithm was evaluated in the context of augmented hearing and
augmented reality audio. Two implementations of the proposed method with
parametric and data-driven dictionaries were compared with the Iso-MVDR
baseline beamformer, as well as the ‘passthrough’ signal at the
reference microphone. Data was obtained using a head-worn microphone
array in real-recordings, and from simulated ‘cocktail party’ scenarios.

\[[3](#bib.bib3)\]. Microphones 5 and 6 are in-ear microphones.

### VI-A Head-worn microphone array

The head-worn ‘glasses’ microphone array from EasyCom dataset
\[[3](#bib.bib3)\] was used. As shown in
Fig. [5](#S6.F5 "Fig. 5 ‣ VI Evaluations ‣ Subspace Hybrid MVDR Beamforming for Augmented Hearing"),
it is a $`6`$-channel array with four microphones on the glasses and two
in-ear microphones. The ATF were measured for a quasi-uniform grid of
directions with coverage range of $`[-174^{\circ},180^{\circ}]`$ and
$`[-66^{\circ},84^{\circ}]`$ and resolution of $`6^{\circ}`$ and
$`9^{\circ}`$ across azimuth and elevation, respectively. The
measurement was done using a manikin head wearing the array in an
anechoic chamber of Meta Reality Labs.

### VI-B Dataset

![Refer to caption](2311.18689v1/graphics/EasyCom_Scenes.png)

Fig. 6: Schematic of the scene used in EasyCom \[[3](#bib.bib3)\]. The
‘waiter’, ID=1, is not wearing a close-talking microphone and their
position is not tracked.

The evaluation was performed using part of the SPEAR (SPEAR) dataset
\[[4](#bib.bib4)\], which is an extension of the EasyCom dataset
\[[3](#bib.bib3)\]. The EasyCom dataset contains several realizations of
a scenario illustrated in
Fig. [6](#S6.F6 "Fig. 6 ‣ VI-B Dataset ‣ VI Evaluations ‣ Subspace Hybrid MVDR Beamforming for Augmented Hearing")
in which a small group of people, sitting around a table in a room, have
a natural conversation surrounded by ten loudspeakers playing
restaurant-like ambient noise. The SPEAR dataset is split into
sub-datasets where the first, D1, is the entire real-recording EasyCom
dataset, and the second, D2, contains the simulated version of EasyCom
(D1).

In EasyCom (D1), one participant (ID=2) was wearing the array while
others were only wearing a close-talking headset microphone used as an
approximate reference signal. Although a variety of metadata is provided
in EasyCom, the tested beamformers only used the array’s ATF, the target
DOA relative to the wearer (ID=2) and head-tracking data for each talker
obtained from an optical motion capture system. The talkers’
close-talking headset microphone was only used as the reference signal
during calculation of intrusive metrics in D1.

The D1 dataset embodies real-world realism and complexity. However, in
such recordings, it is impossible to define the ground truth signals
(direct-path target) at the array, that are normally required as
reference signals for the calculation of instrumental (objective)
intrusive metrics. Although close-talking headset microphone recordings
for each talker are provided in EasyCom, their use in calculation of
intrusive evaluation metrics would introduce reference signal errors.
Such errors may be caused by, for example, sensor noise, cross-talk
leakage from other talkers, the frequency response of the close-talking
microphone and its position relative to the mouth, as well as the
potential inaccuracy in time and level alignments. Hence, our evaluation
also uses the D2 simulated dataset where the true reference signals
(noise-free direct-path target at the array) are available, at the cost
of degraded realism compared to D1. The access to the ground truth noise
signals for the data-driven dictionary is also important and only
available in D2.

In terms of the scenarios, both D1 and D2 share the same room dimensions
of approximately $`7\times 6\times$3\text{\,}\mathrm{m}$`$,
reverberation time RT$`{}_{60}=$645\text{\,}\mathrm{ms}$`$ and ten fixed
loudspeakers distributed across the room playing uncorrelated,
restaurant-like ambient noise. The D2 dataset was simulated using the
TASCAR software platform \[[40](#bib.bib40)\] with the presence of a
table and the walls of approximate dimensions. The talkers’ source
signals in D2 were the D1 talkers’ close-talking headset microphone
signals, denoised using the CEDAR DNS Two plugin. Unlike D1 where the
ATF can slightly vary due to different persons wearing the array, the
simulations in D2 were based on the same measured ATF provided in
\[[3](#bib.bib3)\]. The D2 dataset also used the same head-tracking data
and voice activity labels as provided in EasyCom.

In order to evaluate the effect of noise suppression on the localized
interferer(s) such as the interfering talkers, the scenarios in EasyCom
are divided into $`6\text{\,}\mathrm{s}`$ segments such that the
following conditions are met:

1.  1.
    Only one of the talkers is assumed as the target.
2.  2.
    The target onset occurs after $`2\text{\,}\mathrm{s}`$ based on the
    voice activity metadata.
3.  3.
    The wearer participant (ID=2) is not active.
4.  4.
    The ‘waiter’ participant (ID=1) whose DOA and reference signal are
    unavailable is not the target.

The segments that do not meet these constraints are discarded. Note that
while the selected segments share these constraints, they can still
differ in talker, utterance, number of active sources and DOA. For the
visualization of these results, the selected segments were categorized
according to the number of active sources per segment, denoted as
$`N_{s}`$ = $`[1,2,3]`$. The same segments from both D1 and D2 were used
in the evaluation.

![Refer to caption](2311.18689v1/graphics/results_D1.png)

Fig. 7: The absolute (top row) and relative (bottom row) STOI, PESQ,
fwSegSNR, SDR, SIR and SAR scores of methods for $`N_{s}=[1,2,3]`$ in D1
dataset.

### VI-C Methods

The evaluation compares the proposed SS-Hybrid method, including
parametric and data-driven dictionaries, denoted as ‘SSH’ and ‘SSH-K’,
respectively, with the baseline superdirective beamformer denoted as
‘Iso’, and the passthrough signal at the reference channel denoted as
‘Pass’.

To avoid spatial aliasing due to the array geometry, data was lowpass
filtered and down-sampled to $`10\text{\,}\mathrm{kHz}`$ sample rate.
The STFT used a $`16\text{\,}\mathrm{ms}`$ time-window and
$`8\text{\,}\mathrm{ms}`$ step. Microphone 2 (mid-front in
Fig. [5](#S6.F5 "Fig. 5 ‣ VI Evaluations ‣ Subspace Hybrid MVDR Beamforming for Augmented Hearing"))
was selected as the reference channel. Empirical analysis showed that no
condition number limiting on $`\mathbf{R}`$ in
([3](#S3.E3 "In III-B MVDR ‣ III Formulation and Baseline Beamformer ‣ Subspace Hybrid MVDR Beamforming for Augmented Hearing"))
was needed for any of the beamformers and therefore $`\varepsilon=0`$ in
([4](#S3.E4 "In III-B MVDR ‣ III Formulation and Baseline Beamformer ‣ Subspace Hybrid MVDR Beamforming for Augmented Hearing")).
The steering vector at each time-frame was updated with the RTF
associated with the closest direction to the target DOA from the set of
ATF measurement points described in
Section [VI-A](#S6.SS1 "VI-A Head-worn microphone array ‣ VI Evaluations ‣ Subspace Hybrid MVDR Beamforming for Augmented Hearing").
The settings associated with each method were as follows.

#### VI-C1 Iso (baseline superdirective)

The directions set $`\mathcal{I}`$ in
([7](#S3.E7 "In III-C Isotropic- (Superdirective) Beamformer ‣ III Formulation and Baseline Beamformer ‣ Subspace Hybrid MVDR Beamforming for Augmented Hearing"))
used all the available ATF measurement points described in
Section [VI-A](#S6.SS1 "VI-A Head-worn microphone array ‣ VI Evaluations ‣ Subspace Hybrid MVDR Beamforming for Augmented Hearing")
with quadrature weighting as in
([8](#S3.E8 "In III-C Isotropic- (Superdirective) Beamformer ‣ III Formulation and Baseline Beamformer ‣ Subspace Hybrid MVDR Beamforming for Augmented Hearing")).

#### VI-C2 SSH (SS-Hybrid with parametric dictionary)

The parametric dictionary contained an isotropic model
$`\mathbf{R}_{\text{Iso}}`$ as in Iso, an identity model
$`\mathbf{R}_{\text{Identity}}`$, and $`300`$ unimodal anisotropic
models $`\mathbf{R}_{\text{Aniso}}`$ as in
([23](#S5.E23 "In V-A3 Anisotropic models ‣ V-A Parametric Dictionary ‣ V Dictionary ‣ Subspace Hybrid MVDR Beamforming for Augmented Hearing"))
with all possible pairs between sixty different
$`\varphi_{\text{p}}=\{0,6,\dots,354\}^{\circ}`$ and five different
$`\mathcal{A}=\{8,16,24,32,40\}`$ dB. This results in total of $`M=302`$
models. The set of possible steering directions was limited to
$`\pm 90^{\circ}`$ azimuth and $`\pm 30^{\circ}`$ elevation resulting in
total of $`\Psi=217`$ possible steering directions. Considering $`F=81`$
frequency bands based on the sample rate and the STFT settings as well
as $`Q=6`$ channels based on the array configuration, the dictionary
$`\mathcal{D}`$ in
([9](#S4.E9 "In IV-A Hybrid-MVDR ‣ IV Proposed Method ‣ Subspace Hybrid MVDR Beamforming for Augmented Hearing"))
becomes a $`302\times 81\times 6\times 217`$ table containing
approximately $`32`$ million complex numbers. The time constant for the
smoothness factor, $`\alpha`$, in
([14](#S4.E14 "In IV-B Wideband inter-method PCA ‣ IV Proposed Method ‣ Subspace Hybrid MVDR Beamforming for Augmented Hearing"))
was empirically set to $`T=$80\text{\,}\mathrm{ms}$`$.

#### VI-C3 SSH-K (SS-Hybrid with data-driven dictionary)

Apart from the content of the dictionary, the settings for SSH-K were
the same as for SSH unless stated otherwise. The set of ground truth NCM
estimates $`\mathfrak{R}_{\nu}=\{\mathbf{R}_{\text{gt}}(\nu,\ell)\}`$ in
Section [V-B2](#S5.SS2.SSS2 "V-B2 Clustering ‣ V-B Data-driven Dictionary ‣ V Dictionary ‣ Subspace Hybrid MVDR Beamforming for Augmented Hearing")
were obtained using
([25](#S5.E25 "In V-B Data-driven Dictionary ‣ V Dictionary ‣ Subspace Hybrid MVDR Beamforming for Augmented Hearing"))
on half of the segments with $`N_{s}=1`$ in D2, which resulted in
approximately $`N=7500`$ time-frames. The segments included in the
training set were excluded from the evaluation results. The k-means
clustering had random centroid initialization and used $`K=M=302`$ in
order to have the same number of models in both data-driven and
parametric dictionaries. The Iso model was additionally added to the
data-driven dictionary as a requirement in Hybrid-MVDR.

The single-channel output of each method was compared with the reference

## VII Conclusions

A method of beamforming is proposed for multi-channel speech enhancement
that is adaptive and signal-dependent while being comparable in terms of
computational cost to static and signal-independent beamformers. The
proposed beamformer uses a dictionary of pre-calculated weights. The
method operates in two stages where, in the first stage, multiple MVDR
beamformers are implemented using a weight dictionary with entries
corresponding to different noise covariance models. In the second stage,
a wideband inter-method PCA is applied to the outputs of both static
isotropic and adaptive Hybrid-model beamformers per time-frame to remove
any ‘musical noise’ caused by the Hybrid MVDR beamforming. Two
alternative dictionary designs were investigated: a parametric
dictionary using isotropic and horizontally unimodal anisotropoic noise
field isotropy assumptions, and a data-driven dictionary using k-means
clustering and ground truth-aware estimates of the noise covariance
matrices from a simulated dataset. In the context of augmented hearing
and augmented reality audio, evaluations were performed using real
recordings of ‘cocktail party’ scenarios with approximate reference
signals, and simulations with accurate reference signals for a
$`6`$-channel head-worn microphone array. The results for both real
recordings and simulations showed significant performance benefits of
the proposed method compared to the baseline superdirective beamformer.
In terms of noise suppression, average improvements of
$`6\text{\,}\mathrm{dB}`$ fwSegSNR and $`3\text{\,}\mathrm{dB}`$ SDR,
SIR and SAR were found, while improvements in speech intelligibility
(STOI) and quality (PESQ) were at least as good as the baseline
superdirective beamformer. The use of a data-driven dictionary design,
compared to the parametric dictionary, in the proposed method provided
$`1`$ to $`2\text{\,}\mathrm{dB}`$ more noise suppression with a
corresponding improvement in the speech intelligibility and quality. The
plane-wave representation of the array signal in
([1](#S3.E1 "In III-A Signals in the domain ‣ III Formulation and Baseline Beamformer ‣ Subspace Hybrid MVDR Beamforming for Augmented Hearing"))
with $`J`$ PW can be re-written by separating the target PW and the
remaining $`J-1`$ PW as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
Y_{q}(\nu,\ell)=S_{q}(\nu,\ell)+\sum_{j=1}^{J-1}X_{q,j}(\nu,\ell)+V_{q}(\nu,\ell),
``` |  | (26) |

where $`S_{q}=X_{q,j}\in\mathbb{C}`$ is the $`j`$-th PW associated with
the target direction $`\Omega_{\text{s}}`$. Since formulations for the
remaining of this section will be all for a reference microphone, the
microphone index $`q`$ will be omitted for notational simplicity.
Equation
([26](#A0.E26 "In VII Conclusions ‣ Subspace Hybrid MVDR Beamforming for Augmented Hearing"))
can be written for the full-band spectrum at a reference microphone as

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\check{\mathbf{y}}(\ell)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\check{\mathbf{s}}(\ell)+\check{\mathbf{x}}(\ell)+\check{\mathbf{v}}(\ell)`$ |  | (27) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\check{\mathbf{s}}(\ell)+\check{\mathbf{n}}(\ell)`$ |  | (28) |

where $`\check{\mathbf{y}}\in\mathbb{C}^{1\times F}`$,
$`\check{\mathbf{s}}`$,
$`\check{\mathbf{x}}=\sum_{j=1}^{J-1}\check{\mathbf{x}}_{j}`$ and
$`\check{\mathbf{v}}`$ are the full-band spectrum of the array, target
PW and the remaining PW while
$`\check{\mathbf{n}}=\check{\mathbf{x}}+\check{\mathbf{v}}`$ denotes the
residual noise spectrum including the mixture of non-Target PW and the
sensor noise.

The MVDR spectral output of Iso and Hybrid with steering direction
$`\Omega_{\text{s}}`$ can be written as

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\check{\mathbf{z}}_{\text{Iso}}(\ell)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\check{\mathbf{s}}(\ell)+\check{\mathbf{n}}_{\text{Iso}}(\ell),`$ |  | (29) |
|  | $`\displaystyle\check{\mathbf{z}}_{\text{Hyb}}(\ell)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\check{\mathbf{s}}(\ell)+\check{\mathbf{n}}_{\text{Hyb}}(\ell),`$ |  | (30) |

where $`\check{\mathbf{n}}_{\text{Iso}}`$ and
$`\check{\mathbf{n}}_{\text{Hyb}}`$ are the residual noise in the output
of Iso and Hybrid-MVDR beamformers assuming the target PW
$`\check{\mathbf{s}}`$ is preserved due to the distortionless response
constraint in the MVDR formulation. Note that
$`\check{\mathbf{n}}_{\text{Iso}}`$ and
$`\check{\mathbf{n}}_{\text{Hyb}}`$ are the frequency-dependent scaled
versions of $`\check{\mathbf{n}}`$ in
([28](#A0.E28 "In VII Conclusions ‣ Subspace Hybrid MVDR Beamforming for Augmented Hearing"))
where the scaling coefficients are determined by the narrowband
complex-valued beam patterns. The $`\check{\mathbf{s}}`$,
$`\check{\mathbf{n}}_{\text{Iso}}`$ and
$`\check{\mathbf{n}}_{\text{Hyb}}`$ are expected and assumed to be
uncorrelated.

Substituting
([29](#A0.E29 "In VII Conclusions ‣ Subspace Hybrid MVDR Beamforming for Augmented Hearing"))
and
([30](#A0.E30 "In VII Conclusions ‣ Subspace Hybrid MVDR Beamforming for Augmented Hearing"))
in
([12](#S4.E12 "In IV-B Wideband inter-method PCA ‣ IV Proposed Method ‣ Subspace Hybrid MVDR Beamforming for Augmented Hearing"))
gives

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbf{Z}=\begin{bmatrix}\check{\mathbf{s}}\\
\check{\mathbf{s}}\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}\check{\mathbf{n}}_{\text{Hyb}}\\
\check{\mathbf{n}}_{\text{Iso}}\end{bmatrix},
``` |  | (31) |

where time-frame dependency $`(\ell)`$ is omitted for notational
simplicity. The inter-method wideband covariance matrix
$`\mathbf{R}_{\text{z}}\in\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2}`$ in
([13](#S4.E13 "In IV-B Wideband inter-method PCA ‣ IV Proposed Method ‣ Subspace Hybrid MVDR Beamforming for Augmented Hearing"))
is then re-written as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\begin{split}\mathbf{R}_{\text{z}}&=\mathbb{E}\{\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{Z}^{H}\}\\
&=\mathbb{E}\{\begin{bmatrix}\check{\mathbf{s}}\check{\mathbf{s}}^{H}&\check{\mathbf{s}}\check{\mathbf{s}}^{H}\\
\check{\mathbf{s}}\check{\mathbf{s}}^{H}&\check{\mathbf{s}}\check{\mathbf{s}}^{H}\end{bmatrix}\}+\mathbb{E}\{\begin{bmatrix}\check{\mathbf{n}}_{\text{Hyb}}\check{\mathbf{n}}_{\text{Hyb}}^{H}&\check{\mathbf{n}}_{\text{Hyb}}\check{\mathbf{n}}_{\text{Iso}}^{H}\\
\check{\mathbf{n}}_{\text{Iso}}\check{\mathbf{n}}_{\text{Hyb}}^{H}&\check{\mathbf{n}}_{\text{Iso}}\check{\mathbf{n}}_{\text{Iso}}^{H}\end{bmatrix}\}\\
&+\mathbb{E}\{\begin{bmatrix}\check{\mathbf{s}}\check{\mathbf{n}}_{\text{Hyb}}^{H}&\check{\mathbf{s}}\check{\mathbf{n}}_{\text{Iso}}^{H}\\
\check{\mathbf{s}}\check{\mathbf{n}}_{\text{Hyb}}^{H}&\check{\mathbf{s}}\check{\mathbf{n}}_{\text{Iso}}^{H}\end{bmatrix}\}+\mathbb{E}\{\begin{bmatrix}\check{\mathbf{n}}_{\text{Hyb}}\check{\mathbf{s}}^{H}&\check{\mathbf{n}}_{\text{Hyb}}\check{\mathbf{s}}^{H}\\
\check{\mathbf{n}}_{\text{Iso}}\check{\mathbf{s}}^{H}&\check{\mathbf{n}}_{\text{Iso}}\check{\mathbf{s}}^{H}\end{bmatrix}\}.\end{split}
``` |  | (32) |

Since $`\check{\mathbf{s}}`$, $`\check{\mathbf{n}}_{\text{Iso}}`$ and
$`\check{\mathbf{n}}_{\text{Hyb}}`$ are uncorrelated,
([32](#A0.E32 "In VII Conclusions ‣ Subspace Hybrid MVDR Beamforming for Augmented Hearing"))
can be simplified to

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbf{R}_{\text{z}}&=\lVert\check{\mathbf{s}}\rVert^{2}\mathbf{J}+\begin{bmatrix}\lVert\check{\mathbf{n}}_{\text{Hyb}}\rVert^{2}&0\\
0&\lVert\check{\mathbf{n}}_{\text{Iso}}\rVert^{2}\end{bmatrix}\\
&=\lVert\check{\mathbf{s}}\rVert^{2}\mathbf{j}\mathbf{j}^{H}+\mathbf{\sigma}_{1}\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{\sigma}_{2}\mathbf{K},\end{split}`$ |  |  | (33) |
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathrm{where}\quad\mathbf{\sigma}_{1}`$ | $`\displaystyle=(\frac{\lVert\check{\mathbf{n}}_{\text{Hyb}}\rVert^{2}+\lVert\check{\mathbf{n}}_{\text{Iso}}\rVert^{2}}{2}),`$ |  | (34) |
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathrm{and}\quad\mathbf{\sigma}_{2}`$ | $`\displaystyle=(\frac{\lVert\check{\mathbf{n}}_{\text{Hyb}}\rVert^{2}-\lVert\check{\mathbf{n}}_{\text{Iso}}\rVert^{2}}{2}),`$ |  | (35) |

where $`\mathbf{J}=\mathbf{j}\mathbf{j}^{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}`$
is the all-one matrix with $`\mathbf{j}=[1,1]^{T}`$ being all-one
column-vector, $`\mathbf{I}=\diag([1,1])\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}`$ is
the identity matrix and
$`\mathbf{K}=\diag([1,-1])\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}`$ is the signature
matrix. Hence, the signal subspace in the inter-method PCA is expected
to dominantly include $`\check{\mathbf{s}}`$ that is the most correlated
component between the $`\check{\mathbf{z}}_{\text{Hyb}}`$ and
$`\check{\mathbf{z}}_{\text{Iso}}`$ and avoids the least correlated
component between the two that is the musical noise only present in
$`\check{\mathbf{n}}_{\text{Hyb}}`$.

| ![\[Uncaptioned image\]](2311.18689v1/graphics/author_thomas.png) |  Thomas Lunner’s career has focused on man-machine issues related to the correlation between hearing and cognition. He was the first scientist to convincingly show the importance of cognitive ability in relation to being able to recognize speech in adverse listening conditions. He also demonstrated how working memory was a significant factor for the selection of optimum signal processing in hearing aids to be fitted to a particular individual. These studies led to increasing cooperation with other research groups globally, and the establishment of cognitive hearing science as a research field of its own. Dr. Lunner’s Ph.D. research in the mid-1990s resulted in patented signal processing algorithms which led to the development of the first digital hearing aid manufactured by Oticon. The core of this project was a digital filter bank which provided the necessary tuning flexibility with an equally important low power consumption. The filter bank was used in several successive hearing aid models in the years that followed, fitted to millions of hearing aid users worldwide. Two of the models were awarded the European Union’s prestigious technology prize, the IST Grand Prize in 1996 and in 2003. Currently, he leads research in Superhuman Hearing at Meta Reality Labs Research. |
````
