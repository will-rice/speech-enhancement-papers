###### Abstract

Generative speech enhancement has recently shown promising advancements
in improving speech quality in noisy environments. Multiple
diffusion-based frameworks exist, each employing distinct training
objectives and learning techniques. This paper aims to explain the
differences between these frameworks by focusing our investigation on
score-based generative models and the Schrödinger bridge. We conduct a
series of comprehensive experiments to compare their performance and
highlight differing training behaviors. Furthermore, we propose a novel
perceptual loss function tailored for the Schrödinger bridge framework,
demonstrating enhanced performance and improved perceptual quality of
the enhanced speech signals. All experimental code and pre-trained
models are publicly available to facilitate further research and
development in this domain¹¹ 1
[https://github.com/sp-uhh/sgmse](https://github.com/sp-uhh/sgmse).

## I Introduction

Diffusion-based generative models have been successfully employed in
various audio restoration tasks, most notably in speech
enhancement \[[1](#bib.bib1)\]. Generative methods in this task aim at
estimating and sampling from the clean speech distribution conditioned
on noisy speech. Unlike predictive models, generative models enable the
generation of multiple valid estimates for a given input and can be
utilized for generalized (or universal) speech enhancement, effectively
addressing various corruption types \[[2](#bib.bib2)\].

Numerous diffusion-based generative approaches exist, all centered
around the idea of defining a transformation between the data
distribution and a tractable prior distribution (e.g., Gaussian).
Popular frameworks include continuous-time diffusion
models \[[3](#bib.bib3)\], EDM \[[4](#bib.bib4)\], flow
matching \[[5](#bib.bib5)\], and the [SB](#id35)
([SB](#id35)) \[[6](#bib.bib6)\]. Each of these approaches has been
applied to speech enhancement.

[SGMSE](#id36) ([SGMSE](#id36)) \[[7](#bib.bib7), [8](#bib.bib8)\]
employs continuous-time diffusion models based on [SDE](#id8). Follow-up
work utilizes the EDM framework; it has been proposed to use a change of
variable to consider the [SDE](#id8) satisfied by the environmental
noise \[[9](#bib.bib9)\], which results in the required linear affine
drift term. Flow matching has been used in
SpeechFlow \[[10](#bib.bib10)\], where the authors apply masked audio
prediction as a self-supervised pretraining technique.

More recently, the [SB](#id35) has been proposed for speech
enhancement \[[11](#bib.bib11)\]. The [SB](#id35) is a generative model
that seeks an optimal way to transport one probability distribution to
another distribution \[[6](#bib.bib6)\]. This approach enables starting
the generative process directly from the noisy input and allows for
using a data prediction loss \[[12](#bib.bib12)\].

This paper builds upon the above-mentioned advances and explores
multiple training objectives and learning techniques for generative
speech enhancement. We begin by exploring score-based generative models
and connecting various loss functions used to learn the score function.
For further details, we refer to \[[13](#bib.bib13)\], showing how
various diffusion-based generative model objectives can be understood as
special cases of a weighted loss. Second, we examine the [SB](#id35)
approach for speech enhancement \[[11](#bib.bib11)\] and establish a
connection to [SGMSE](#id36) \[[8](#bib.bib8)\]. Moreover, we propose a
novel perceptual loss function for the [SB](#id35) framework and perform
ablation studies to evaluate its effect. Perceptually motivated loss
functions have been extensively integrated into speech enhancement
frameworks \[[14](#bib.bib14), [15](#bib.bib15)\]. However, their use in
diffusion-based generative models, particularly within the Schrödinger
bridge framework, is a novel approach. We specifically choose the PESQ
metric because it is a widely used instrumental measure in speech
enhancement for assessing speech quality and, unlike
POLQA \[[16](#bib.bib16)\], a differentiable version is
available \[[17](#bib.bib17)\].

Our experiments demonstrate that score-based generative models trained
with different objective functions exhibit varying training behaviors,
although they theoretically model the same underlying concepts. We
hypothesize this is due to the neural network’s different training
tasks. Additionally, we show that our novel perceptual loss for the
[SB](#id35) achieves state-of-the-art performance in PESQ on the
[VB-DMD](#id39) ([VB-DMD](#id39)) benchmark \[[18](#bib.bib18)\].

Contemporaneously to our work, Wang et al. \[[19](#bib.bib19)\] explore
the [SB](#id35) and set a symmetric noise scheduling, where the
diffusion shrinks at both boundaries. Furthermore, they combine the SB
concept with a two-stage approach inspired by StoRM \[[20](#bib.bib20)\]
by aiding the generative model with a magnitude ratio mask.

[II](#S2 "II Methods ‣ Investigating Training Objectives forGenerative Speech Enhancement")

## II Methods

This section discusses two existing generative approaches for speech
enhancement and explores their connection. First, we introduce
[SGMSE](#id36) \[[8](#bib.bib8)\]. Second, we examine the [SB](#id35)
for speech enhancement \[[11](#bib.bib11)\]. Both approaches are
diffusion-based stochastic processes aiming to model and manipulate
probability distributions. Score-based models emphasize learning score
functions, whereas the SB approach can be considered as an optimal
transport problem.

### II-A Score-based generative models for speech enhancement

Following \[[8](#bib.bib8)\], the diffusion forward process is described
by the solution to the _forward [SDE](#id8)_

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{t}=\gamma(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}_{t})\mathrm{d}t+g(t)\mathrm{d}\mathbf{w},
``` |  | (1) |

where $`\mathbf{x}_{t}\!\in\!\mathbb{C}^{d}`$ is the process state at
time $`t\!\in\!\left[0,1\right]`$ and
$`\mathbf{y}\!\in\!\mathbb{C}^{d}`$ is the noisy speech. The diffusion
coefficient $`g(t)=\sqrt{c}k^{t}`$ controls the Gaussian noise
introduced by the Wiener process $`\mathbf{w}\!\in\!\mathbb{C}^{d}`$.
Moreover, $`\gamma`$, $`c`$, and $`k`$ are positive scalar constants
that are set as hyperparameters. The forward process is also called
[OUVE](#id12) ([OUVE](#id12)) [SDE](#id8)\[[21](#bib.bib21)\].

The marginals of the time-reversed forward process can be represented as
marginals of another stochastic process (see Theorem A.1 in
\[[22](#bib.bib22)\]). This resulting process is described by the
solution to the so-called *reverse [SDE](#id8)*

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{t}=[-\gamma(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}_{t})+g(t)^{2}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\log p_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{y})]\mathrm{d}t+g(t)\mathrm{d}\bar{\mathbf{w}}\,,
``` |  | (2) |

where $`\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\log p_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{y})`$
is the conditional score function, and $`\bar{\mathbf{w}}`$ is the
Wiener process backward in time.

It can be shown that the [OUVE](#id12) [SDE](#id8) results in an
interpolation between clean speech $`\mathbf{x}_{0}`$ and noisy speech
$`\mathbf{y}`$ with exponentially increasing
variance \[[8](#bib.bib8)\]. The evolution of the marginals is described
by the time-dependent mean

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\bm{\mu}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{y})=e^{-\gamma t}\mathbf{x}_{0}+(1-e^{-\gamma t})\mathbf{y}
``` |  | (3) |

and the time-dependent variance

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\sigma_{t}^{2}=\frac{c(k^{2t}-e^{-2\gamma t})}{2(\gamma+\log k)}\,,
``` |  | (4) |

that allow for direct sampling of the process state $`\mathbf{x}_{t}`$
at time $`t`$ using the perturbation kernel

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
p_{0t}(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{y})=\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbf{x}_{t};\bm{\mu}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{y}),\sigma_{t}^{2}\mathbf{I})\,.
``` |  | (5) |

The score function is typically intractable and approximated by a score
model $`\mathbf{s}_{\theta}`$ with parameters $`\theta`$. To train the
score model, we use the denoising score-matching
objective \[[23](#bib.bib23)\]

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DSM}}=\lambda(t)\lVert\mathbf{s}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{y},t)-\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\log p_{0t}(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{y})\rVert_{2}^{2}
``` |  | (6) |

where $`\lambda(t)`$ is a weighting function, and the other variables
are sampled according to $`t\sim\mathcal{U}[0,1]`$,
$`(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{y})\sim p_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{y})`$
from the dataset, and
$`\mathbf{x}_{t}\sim p_{0t}(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{y})`$.
The score matching loss is essentially equivalent to a noise prediction
loss

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\text{score}}=\lVert\mathbf{s}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{y},t)\,\sigma_{t}+\mathbf{z}\rVert_{2}^{2}~\text{,}
``` |  | (7) |

when $`\lambda(t)=\sigma_{t}^{2}`$ in
Eq. ([6](#S2.E6 "Equation 6 ‣ II-A Score-based generative models for speech enhancement ‣ II Methods ‣ Investigating Training Objectives forGenerative Speech Enhancement")),
and $`\mathbf{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})`$. To improve
numerical stability, the output of an employed neural network
$`F_{\theta}`$ is often scaled by a factor of $`1/\sigma_{t}`$ such that
$`\mathbf{s}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{y},t)=F_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{y},t)/\sigma_{t}`$.

Following the derivations in \[[4](#bib.bib4)\], it can also be shown
that denoising score matching for [SGMSE](#id36) is equivalent to
training a denoiser model $`D_{\theta}`$ with the denoising loss

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\text{denoise}}=\lambda(t)\lVert D_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{y},t)-\bm{\mu}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{y})\rVert^{2}_{2}\,.
``` |  | (8) |

Furthermore, it was argued that it is beneficial to precondition a
neural network $`F_{\theta}`$ to obtain the denoiser

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
D_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{y},t)=c_{\text{skip}}(t)\mathbf{x}_{t}+c_{\text{out}}(t)F_{\theta}(c_{\text{in}}(t)\mathbf{x}_{t},\,c_{\text{in}}(t)\mathbf{y},\,t)
``` |  | (9) |

where $`c_{\text{skip}}(t)`$, $`c_{\text{out}}(t)`$,
$`c_{\text{in}}(t)`$, and $`c_{\text{in}}(t)`$ are time-dependent
functions that can be derived from first principles (see Appendix B.6 in
\[[4](#bib.bib4)\]). Then, the score is then given by

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbf{s}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{y},t)=\frac{D_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{y},t)-\mathbf{x}_{t}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}\,.
``` |  | (10) |

### II-B Schrödinger bridge for speech enhancement

The [SB](#id35) \[[24](#bib.bib24)\] is defined as the minimization of
the Kullback-Leibler divergence $`D_{\text{KL}}`$ between a path measure
$`p`$ and a reference path measure $`p_{\text{ref}}`$, subject to
boundary conditions

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\min_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{\left[0,1\right]}}D_{\text{KL}}\left(p,p_{\mathrm{ref}}\right)\quad\text{s. t.}\quad p_{0}=p_{x},\thickspace p_{1}=p_{y},
``` |  | (11) |

where $`\mathcal{P}_{\left[0,1\right]}`$ is the space of path measures
on $`\left[0,1\right]`$ \[[6](#bib.bib6)\]. An optimal transport
solution is given by a pair of symmetric forward and reverse SDEs, with
the forward [SDE](#id8) being

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{t}=\left[\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_{t})+g(t)^{2}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\log\Psi_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\right]\mathrm{d}t+g(t)\mathrm{d}\mathbf{w}_{t},\,\mathbf{x}_{0}\sim p_{x},
``` |  | (12) |

and the reverse SDE being

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{t}=\left[\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_{t})-g(t)^{2}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\log\bar{\Psi}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\right]\mathrm{d}t+g(t)\mathrm{d}\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{t},\,\mathbf{x}_{1}\sim p_{y},
``` |  | (13) |

where the functions $`\Psi_{t},\bar{\Psi}_{t}`$ are described by coupled
partial differential equations (see Theorem 1 in \[[6](#bib.bib6)\])

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\begin{cases}\frac{\partial\Psi_{t}}{\partial t}=-\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\Psi_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})^{\text{T}}\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_{t})-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Tr}(g(t)^{2}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}^{2}\Psi_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t}))\\
\frac{\partial\bar{\Psi}_{t}}{\partial t}=-\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\cdot(\bar{\Psi}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_{t}))+\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Tr}(g(t)^{2}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}^{2}\bar{\Psi}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t}))\end{cases}`$ |  |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle\textit{s.t.}\,\,\Psi_{0}\bar{\Psi}_{0}=p_{x},\Psi_{1}\bar{\Psi}_{1}=p_{y}.`$ |  | (14) |

However, for a system of symmetric forward and reverse SDEs in
Eqs. ([12](#S2.E12 "Equation 12 ‣ II-B Schrödinger bridge for speech enhancement ‣ II Methods ‣ Investigating Training Objectives forGenerative Speech Enhancement"),
[13](#S2.E13 "Equation 13 ‣ II-B Schrödinger bridge for speech enhancement ‣ II Methods ‣ Investigating Training Objectives forGenerative Speech Enhancement")),
and arbitrary $`\Psi_{t}`$ and $`\bar{\Psi}_{t}`$, there are infinitely
many solutions bridging the prior to the target \[[25](#bib.bib25)\].
According to Nelson’s identity \[[26](#bib.bib26)\]

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\log\Psi_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})+\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\log\bar{\Psi}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})=\nabla_{\mathbf{x_{t}}}\log p_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t}),
``` |  | (15) |

which is a necessary condition for time-reversal \[[25](#bib.bib25)\],
we note that in score-based generative models, this corresponds to
setting $`\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\log\Psi_{t}`$ to zero. This implies
that in score-based generative models, the drift of the forward process,
$`\mathbf{f}_{\text{SGMSE}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})=\gamma(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}_{t})`$
in
Eq. ([1](#S2.E1 "Equation 1 ‣ II-A Score-based generative models for speech enhancement ‣ II Methods ‣ Investigating Training Objectives forGenerative Speech Enhancement")),
has to be chosen such that the perturbation kernel is known
analytically. However, this is not required for the general [SB](#id35)
formulation.

Although solving the general [SB](#id35) is typically intractable,
closed-form solutions are available for specific cases, such as those
involving Gaussian boundary conditions \[[27](#bib.bib27)\]. Assume a
drift $`\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\!=\!f(t)\,\mathbf{x}_{t}`$ and
conditional Gaussian boundary conditions
$`p_{0}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{x}_{0})\!=\!\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}\left(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{x}_{0},\epsilon_{0}^{2}\mathbf{I}\right)`$
and
$`p_{1}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})\!=\!\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}\left(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{y},\epsilon_{1}^{2}\mathbf{I}\right)`$
where
$`\epsilon_{1}=\mathrm{e}^{\int_{0}^{1}f(\tau)\mathrm{d}{\tau}}\epsilon_{0}`$.
For $`\epsilon_{0}\!\to\!0`$, the SB solution between clean speech
$`\mathbf{x}_{0}`$ and noisy speech $`\mathbf{y}`$ can be expressed as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\bar{\Psi}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{x}_{0})=\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}(\alpha_{t}\mathbf{x}_{0},\alpha_{t}^{2}\sigma_{t}^{2}\mathbf{I}),\;\Psi_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{y})=\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}(\bar{\alpha}_{t}\mathbf{y},\alpha_{t}^{2}\bar{\sigma}_{t}^{2}\mathbf{I})
``` |  | (16) |

with parameters
$`\alpha_{t}\!=\!\mathrm{e}^{\int_{0}^{t}f(\tau)\mathrm{d}{\tau}}`$,
$`\sigma_{t}^{2}\!=\!\int_{0}^{t}\frac{g^{2}(\tau)}{\alpha_{\tau}^{2}}\mathrm{d}{\tau}`$,
$`\bar{\alpha}_{t}\!=\!\alpha_{t}\alpha_{1}^{-1}`$ and
$`\bar{\sigma}_{t}^{2}=\sigma_{1}^{2}-\sigma_{t}^{2}`$
\[[12](#bib.bib12)\]. Therefore, the marginal distribution is the
Gaussian distribution

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
p_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{y})=\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}\left(\mathbf{x}_{t};\bm{\mu}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{y}),\sigma_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}^{2}\mathbf{I}\right)
``` |  | (17) |

whose mean and variance are defined as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\bm{\mu}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{y})=w_{x}(t)\mathbf{x}_{0}+w_{y}(t)\mathbf{y},\quad\sigma_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}^{2}=\frac{\alpha_{t}^{2}\bar{\sigma}_{t}^{2}\sigma_{t}^{2}}{\sigma_{1}^{2}},
``` |  | (18) |

with $`w_{x}(t)=\alpha_{t}\bar{\sigma}_{t}^{2}/\sigma_{1}^{2}`$, and
$`w_{y}(t)=\bar{\alpha}_{t}\sigma_{t}^{2}/\sigma_{1}^{2}`$ \[[12](#bib.bib12)\].

In this paper, we adopt the same [VE](#id11) ([VE](#id11)) diffusion
coefficient $`g(t)=\sqrt{c}k^{t}`$ as used in Eq.
([1](#S2.E1 "Equation 1 ‣ II-A Score-based generative models for speech enhancement ‣ II Methods ‣ Investigating Training Objectives forGenerative Speech Enhancement")),
and set $`f(t)=0`$. This [SB](#id35) configuration has shown strong
robustness for both denoising and dereverberation \[[11](#bib.bib11)\].
Consequently, we get $`\alpha_{t}=1`$ and
$`\sigma_{t}^{2}=c(k^{2t}-1)/2\log k`$. Due to the optimal transport
characteristics of the [SB](#id35), the mean exactly interpolates
between the clean speech $`\mathbf{x}_{0}`$ at $`t=0`$ and the noisy
speech $`y`$ at $`t=1`$.

A key advantage of the [SB](#id35) compared to [SGMSE](#id36) is that
the neural network $`F_{\theta}`$ can be trained to directly predict the
data $`\mathbf{x}_{0}`$ \[[12](#bib.bib12)\]. This is in contrast to
[SGMSE](#id36), where the Gaussian noise $`\mathbf{z}`$ is predicted as
shown in
Eq. ([7](#S2.E7 "Equation 7 ‣ II-A Score-based generative models for speech enhancement ‣ II Methods ‣ Investigating Training Objectives forGenerative Speech Enhancement")).
Using the data prediction loss, it has been proposed to include a
time-domain auxiliary loss term based on the
$`\ell_{1}`$-norm \[[11](#bib.bib11)\]. Additionally, we propose
incorporating a perceptual loss term such that

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\text{SB}}=\lVert F_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{y},t)-\mathbf{x}_{0}\rVert_{2}^{2}+\alpha\lVert\underline{\hat{\mathbf{x}}}_{\theta}-\underline{\mathbf{x}}_{0}\rVert_{1}-\alpha_{\text{P}}\operatorname{PESQ}(\underline{\hat{\mathbf{x}}}_{\theta},\underline{\mathbf{x}}_{0})\,,
``` |  | (19) |

where
$`\underline{\hat{\mathbf{x}}}_{\theta}=\operatorname{iSTFT}(F_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{y},t))`$
and $`\underline{\mathbf{x}}_{0}=\operatorname{iSTFT}(\mathbf{x}_{0})`$
represent the corresponding time-domain signals using the [iSTFT](#id7)
([iSTFT](#id7)). Moreover, $`\operatorname{PESQ}(\cdot,\cdot)`$ denotes
a differentiable version of the PESQ metric²² 2
[https://github.com/audiolabs/torch-pesq](https://github.com/audiolabs/torch-pesq),
and $`\alpha`$ and $`\alpha_{\text{P}}`$ are hyperparameters to weight
the different loss terms.

At inference, the reverse SDE in
Eq. ([13](#S2.E13 "Equation 13 ‣ II-B Schrödinger bridge for speech enhancement ‣ II Methods ‣ Investigating Training Objectives forGenerative Speech Enhancement"))
can be solved with an [ODE](#id9) ([ODE](#id9)) sampler or an
[SDE](#id8) sampler \[[12](#bib.bib12)\]. Here, we make use of the
[ODE](#id9) sampler because it has shown better performance for the
speech enhancement task \[[11](#bib.bib11)\]. For a given discretization
schedule $`(t_{N}=1,t_{N-1},\dots,t_{0}=0)`$ with $`N`$ steps, the ODE
sampler is recursively defined as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbf{x}_{t_{n-1}}=a_{n}\mathbf{x}_{t_{n}}+b_{n}F_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t_{n}},\mathbf{y},t_{n})+c_{n}\mathbf{y},\quad\mathbf{x}_{t_{N}}=\mathbf{y}\,,
``` |  | (20) |

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  |  | $`\displaystyle a_{n}=\frac{\alpha_{t_{n-1}}\sigma_{t_{n-1}}\bar{\sigma}_{t_{n-1}}}{\alpha_{t_{n}}\sigma_{t_{n}}\bar{\sigma}_{t_{n}}},`$ |  | (21) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle b_{n}=\frac{\alpha_{t_{n-1}}}{\sigma_{1}^{2}}\left(\bar{\sigma}_{t_{n-1}}^{2}-\frac{\bar{\sigma}_{t_{n}}\sigma_{t_{n-1}}\bar{\sigma}_{t_{n-1}}}{\sigma_{t_{n}}}\right),`$ |  | (22) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle c_{n}=\frac{\alpha_{t_{n-1}}}{\alpha_{1}\sigma_{1}^{2}}\left(\sigma_{t_{n-1}}^{2}-\frac{\sigma_{t_{n}}\sigma_{t_{n-1}}\bar{\sigma}_{t_{n-1}}}{\bar{\sigma}_{t_{n}}}\right).`$ |  | (23) |

that the expected magnitudes of activations, weights, and updates
maintain unit variance. Additionally, all additive biases are removed,
and an extra channel of constant one is concatenated to the network’s
input instead. We use the same number of layers and channels as for the
NCSN++. Furthermore, the authors propose to use a power function
[EMA](#id34) ([EMA](#id34)) that automatically scales according to
training time and has zero contribution at the initial training step.

### III-C Metrics

As intrusive speech enhancement metrics, we include
POLQA \[[16](#bib.bib16)\] and PESQ \[[33](#bib.bib33)\] for predicting
speech quality. Moreover, we employ ESTOI \[[34](#bib.bib34)\] as an
instrumental measure of speech intelligibility and calculate the
[SI-SDR](#id26) ([SI-SDR](#id26)) \[[35](#bib.bib35)\] measured in dB.
As a non-intrusive metric, we use DNSMOS \[[36](#bib.bib36)\], which
employs a neural network trained on human ratings. For all metrics it
holds, the higher, the better.

[VB-DMD](#id39) dataset. We begin by comparing different training

standard [SGMSE](#id36) training objective, replicating the results
reported in the original paper \[[8](#bib.bib8)\]. Minor discrepancies
in these outcomes may be attributed to a different batch size. Model M2
utilizes the denoising loss in
Eq. ([8](#S2.E8 "Equation 8 ‣ II-A Score-based generative models for speech enhancement ‣ II Methods ‣ Investigating Training Objectives forGenerative Speech Enhancement"))
and achieves slightly higher scores than M1. However, its training
process is more unstable, as depicted by the orange lines in Fig.
[1](#S4.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ IV Results ‣ Investigating Training Objectives forGenerative Speech Enhancement").
In contrast, the preconditioned model M3 displays faster training times
but produces results comparable to those of M1. Model M4 explores the
EDM2 network, exhibiting performance that is competitive with M1. Yet,
similar to M2, it experiences instability during training, indicated by
the red lines in Fig.
[1](#S4.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ IV Results ‣ Investigating Training Objectives forGenerative Speech Enhancement").
It is worth noting that the results for M4 are preliminary, as we have
not experimented with various post-hoc [EMA](#id34) configurations and
fine-tuned learning rate schedulers. Among models M1-M4, differences in
ESTOI and DNSMOS are negligible.

Turning the attention to the [SB](#id35) approach, model M5 shows strong
performance in [SI-SDR](#id26); an expected result due to the
$`\ell_{1}`$-loss applied in the time domain. Moreover, M5 demonstrates
relatively stable training behavior, as illustrated by the purple lines
in
Fig. [1](#S4.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ IV Results ‣ Investigating Training Objectives forGenerative Speech Enhancement"),
and achieves the best score for DNSMOS among all methods. Model M6
introduces the PESQ loss and achieves state-of-the-art results in PESQ
with a score of 3.70. However, similar to the extreme behavior of the
PESQetarian \[[32](#bib.bib32)\], this improvement comes at the expense
of SI-SDR. Models M7 and M8 investigate different weightings of the PESQ
term, achieving a better balance between SI-SDR and PESQ, while also
obtaining higher ESTOI scores compared to M5 and M6. Specifically, M7
performs best in POLQA among all models based on the [SB](#id35).

## V Conclusion

This paper explored the distinctions among various diffusion-based
frameworks for generative speech enhancement, explicitly focusing on
score-based generative models and the Schrödinger bridge (SB). Through
comprehensive experimental analysis, we highlighted the variations in
training behaviors and performance across these frameworks. We proposed
adding ad-hoc cost functions to the [SB](#id35) framework, significantly
enhancing the performance and perceptual quality of the processed speech
signals. To support ongoing research and innovation in this field, we
made all experimental code and pre-trained models publicly accessible.
````
