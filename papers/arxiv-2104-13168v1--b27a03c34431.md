###### Abstract

This paper presents dEchorate: a new database of measured multichannel
Room Impulse Responses (RIRs) including annotations of early echo
timings and 3D positions of microphones, real sources and image sources
under different wall configurations in a cuboid room. These data provide
a tool for benchmarking recent methods in echo-aware speech enhancement,
room geometry estimation, RIR estimation, acoustic echo retrieval,
microphone calibration, echo labeling and reflectors estimation. The
database is accompanied with software utilities to easily access,
manipulate and visualize the data as well as baseline methods for
echo-related tasks.

## 1 Introduction

When sound travels from a source to a microphone in a indoor space, it
interacts with the environment by being delayed and attenuated due to
the distance; and reflected, absorbed and diffracted due to the
surfaces. The [RIR](#Sx4.15) ([RIR](#Sx4.15)) represents this phenomenon
as a linear and causal time-domain filter. As depicted
in [Figure 1](#S1.F1 "In 1 Introduction ‣ dEchorate: a Calibrated Room Impulse Response Database for Echo-aware Signal Processing"),
[RIR](#Sx4.15) are commonly subdivided into 3 parts: the direct-path,
corresponding to the line-of-sight propagation; the early echoes,
stemming from few disjoint reflections on the closest reflectors; and
the late reverberation comprising the dense accumulation of later
reflections and scattering effects.

The late reverberation is indicative of the environment size and
reverberation time, producing the so-called listener envelopment, i.e.,
the degree of immersion in the sound field \[[1](#bib.bib1)\]. In
contrast, the direct path and the early echoes carry precise information
on the scene’s geometry, such as the position of the source and room
surfaces relative to the receiver position \[[2](#bib.bib2)\], and on
the surfaces’ reflectivity. Such relation is well explained by the
[ISM](#Sx4.19) ([ISM](#Sx4.19)) \[[3](#bib.bib3)\], in which the echoes
are associated with the contribution of virtual sound sources lying
outside the real room. Therefore, one may consider early echoes as
“spatialized” copies of the source signal, whose [TOA](#Sx4.17) are
related to the source and reflector positions.

Figure 1: Depiction of a measured room impulse response from the
database.

Based on this idea, so-called echo-aware methods have been introduced a
few decades ago, where matched filters (or rake receivers) are used to
constructively sum the sound reflections \[[4](#bib.bib4),
[5](#bib.bib5), [6](#bib.bib6)\] and build beamformers achieving much
better sound qualities \[[7](#bib.bib7)\]. These methods have recently
regained interested as manifested by the European project
SCENIC \[[8](#bib.bib8)\] and the UK research project S³A¹¹ 1
[http://www.s3a-spatialaudio.org/](http://www.s3a-spatialaudio.org/).
Later, a few studies showed that knowing the properties of a few early
echoes could boost the performance of typical indoor audio inverse
problems such as [SE](#Sx4.20) ([SE](#Sx4.20)) \[[9](#bib.bib9),
[10](#bib.bib10)\], sound source localization \[[11](#bib.bib11),
[12](#bib.bib12), [13](#bib.bib13), [14](#bib.bib14)\] and separation
\[[15](#bib.bib15), [16](#bib.bib16), [17](#bib.bib17),
[18](#bib.bib18)\], and speaker verification \[[19](#bib.bib19)\].

Another fervent area of research spanning transversely the audio signal
processing field is estimating the room geometry blindly from acoustic
signals \[[20](#bib.bib20), [21](#bib.bib21), [22](#bib.bib22),
[23](#bib.bib23)\]. As recently reviewed by Crocco et al. in
\[[22](#bib.bib22)\], end-to-end [RooGE](#Sx4.25) ([RooGE](#Sx4.25))
involves a number of subtasks: [RIR](#Sx4.15) estimation, peak picking,
microphones calibration, echo labeling and reflectors’ position
estimation. As interesting applications, these methods have been
recently used in active setting (i.e., knowing the transmitted signals)
on unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs, a.k.a. drones) \[[24](#bib.bib24),
[25](#bib.bib25)\] and on mobile-phones \[[26](#bib.bib26)\]. The lowest
common denominator of all these tasks is [AER](#Sx4.2) ([AER](#Sx4.2)),
that is, estimating the properties of early echoes, such as their
[TOA](#Sx4.17) and energies. The former problem is typically referred to
as [TOA](#Sx4.17) estimation, or [TDOA](#Sx4.18) ([TDOA](#Sx4.18))
estimation when the direct-path is taken as reference.

As listed in \[[27](#bib.bib27)\] and in \[[28](#bib.bib28)\], a number
of recorded [RIR](#Sx4.15) corpora are freely available online, each of
them meeting the demands of certain applications.
[Table 1](#S1.T1 "In 1 Introduction ‣ dEchorate: a Calibrated Room Impulse Response Database for Echo-aware Signal Processing")
summarizes the main characteristics of some of them. One can broadly
identify two main classes of echo-aware [RIR](#Sx4.15) datasets in the
literature: [SE](#Sx4.20)/ [ASR](#Sx4.3) ([ASR](#Sx4.3))-oriented
datasets, e.g. \[[27](#bib.bib27), [30](#bib.bib30), [31](#bib.bib31)\],
and [RooGE](#Sx4.25)-oriented datasets, e.g. \[[21](#bib.bib21),
[22](#bib.bib22), [23](#bib.bib23)\]. The former regards acoustic echoes
as highly correlated interfering sources coming from close reflectors,
such as a table in a meeting room or a near wall. This typically
presents a challenge in estimating the correct source’s [DOA](#Sx4.7)
([DOA](#Sx4.7)) with further consequences in [DOA](#Sx4.7)-based
enhancement algorithm, e.g., beamformers. Although this factor is taken
into account, such datasets lack proper annotation of these echoes in
the [RIR](#Sx4.15) or the absolute position of objects inside the room.
The latter group typically features design choices, such as microphones
scattered across the room, which are not suitable for [SE](#Sx4.20)
applications. Indeed, these typically involve compact or ad hoc arrays.
The main common drawback of these datasets in that they cannot be easily
used for other tasks than the ones which they are designed for.

To bypass the complexity of recording and annotating real [RIR](#Sx4.15)
datasets, acoustic simulators based on the [ISM](#Sx4.19) are
extensively used instead \[[32](#bib.bib32), [33](#bib.bib33),
[34](#bib.bib34), [33](#bib.bib33), [35](#bib.bib35)\]. While such data
are more versatile, simpler and quicker to obtain, they fail to fully
capture the complexity and richness of real acoustic environments. Due
to this, methods trained, calibrated, or validated on them may fail to
generalize to real conditions, as will be shown in this paper.
Interestingly, in the context of learning-based blind room volume
estimation, the authors of \[[28](#bib.bib28)\] combined multiple real
and synthetic [RIR](#Sx4.15) datasets in order to find a balance between
number of training data and realism.

A good echo-oriented [RIR](#Sx4.15) dataset should include a variety of
environments (room geometries and surface materials), of microphone
placings (close to or away from reflectors, scattered or forming ad-hoc
arrays) and, most importantly, precise annotations of the scene’s
geometry and echo timings in the [RIR](#Sx4.15). Moreover, in order to
be versatile and used in both [SE](#Sx4.20) and [RooGE](#Sx4.25)
applications, geometry and timing annotations should be fully
consistent. Such data are difficult to collect since it involves precise
measurements of the positions and orientations of all the acoustic
emitters, receivers and reflective surfaces inside the environment with
dedicated planimetric equipment.

To fill this gap, we present the dEchorate dataset: a fully calibrated
multichannel [RIR](#Sx4.15) database with accurate annotation of the
geometry and echo timings in different configurations of a cuboid room
with varying wall acoustic profiles. The database currently features
1800 annotated [RIR](#Sx4.15) obtained from 6 arrays of 5 microphones
each, 6 sound sources and 11 different acoustic conditions. All the
measurements were carried out at the acoustic lab at Bar-Ilan University
following a consolidated protocol previously established for the
realization of two other multichannel [RIR](#Sx4.15) databases: the
BIU’s Impulse Response Database \[[29](#bib.bib29)\] gathering
[RIR](#Sx4.15) of different reverberation levels sensed by uniform
linear arrays (ULAs); and MIRaGE \[[31](#bib.bib31)\] providing a set of
measurements for a source placed on a dense position grid. The dEchorate
dataset is designed for AER with linear arrays, and is more generally
aimed at analyzing and benchmarking [RooGE](#Sx4.25) and echo-aware
signal processing methods on real data. In particular, it can be used to
assess robustness against the number of reflectors, the reverberation
time, additive spatially-diffuse noise and non-ideal frequency and
directive characteristics of microphone-source pairs and surfaces in a
controlled way. Due to the amount of data and recording conditions, it
could also be used to train machine learning models or as a reference to
improve [RIR](#Sx4.15) simulators. The database is accompanied with a
Python toolbox that can be used to process and visualize the data,
perform analysis or annotate new datasets.

### 2.1 Recording setup

The recording setup is placed in a cuboid room with dimension 6 m
$`\times`$ 6 m $`\times`$ 2.4 m. The 6 facets of the room (walls,
ceiling, floor) are covered by acoustic panels allowing controllable
reverberation time ($`\mathtt{RT}_{60}`$). We placed 4 directional
loudspeakers (direct sources) facing the center of the room and 30
microphones mounted on 6 static linear arrays parallel to the ground. An
additional channel is used for the loop-back signal, which serves to
compute the time of emission and detect errors. Each loudspeaker and
each array is positioned close to one of the walls in such a way that
the source of the strongest echo can be easily identified. Moreover,
their positioning was chosen to cover a wide distribution of
source-to-receiver distances, hence, a wide range of [DRR](#Sx4.6).
Further, 2 more loudspeakers were positioned pointing towards the walls
(indirect sources). This was done to study the case of early reflections
being stronger than the direct-path.

Each linear array consists of 5 microphones with non-uniform
inter-microphone spacings of $`[4,5,7.5,10]`$ cm²² 2 that is,
$`[-12.25,-8.25,-3.25,3.25,13.25]`$ cm w.r.t. the barycenter.
Hereinafter we will refer to these elements as [nULA](#Sx4.13).

### 2.2 Measurements

The main feature of this room is the possibility to change the acoustic
profile of each of its facets by flipping double-sided panels with one
reflective (made of Formica Laminate sheets) and one absorbing face made
of perforated panels filled with rock-wool). A complete list of the
materials of the room is available in [Room
materials](#Sx1.SSx1 "Room materials ‣ Appendix ‣ dEchorate: a Calibrated Room Impulse Response Database for Echo-aware Signal Processing").
This allows to achieve diverse values of $`\mathtt{RT}_{60}`$ that range
from 0.1 to almost 1 second. In this dataset, the panels of the floor
were always kept absorbent.

Two types of measurement sessions were considered, namely, one-hot and
incremental. For the first type, a single facet was placed in reflective
mode while all the others were kept absorbent. For the second type,
starting from fully-absorbent mode, facets were progressively switched
to reflective one after the other until all but the floor are
reflective, as shown
in [Table 3](#S2.T3 "In 2.2 Measurements ‣ 2 Database Description ‣ dEchorate: a Calibrated Room Impulse Response Database for Echo-aware Signal Processing").
The dataset features an extra recording session. For this session,
office furnitures (chairs, coat-hanger and a table) were positioned in
the room to simulate a typical meeting room with chairs and tables
(see [Figure 2](#S1.F2 "In 1 Introduction ‣ dEchorate: a Calibrated Room Impulse Response Database for Echo-aware Signal Processing")).
Theses recordings may be used to assert the robustness of echo-aware
methods in a more realistic scenario

For each room configuration and loudspeaker, three different excitation
signals were played and recorded in sequence: chirps, white noise and
speech utterances. The former consists in a repetition of 3
[ESS](#Sx4.8) ([ESS](#Sx4.8)) signals of duration 10 seconds and
frequency range from 100 Hz to 14 kHz interspersed with 2 seconds of
silence. Such frequency range was chosen to match the characteristics of
the loudspeakers. To prevent rapid phase changes and ‘‘popping’’
effects, the signals were linearly faded in and out over 0.2 seconds
with a Tuckey taper window.³³ 3 The code to generate the reference
signals and to process them is available together with the data. The
code is based on the [pyrirtools](https://github.com/maj4e/pyrirtool)
Python library Second, 10 seconds bursts of white noise and 3 anechoic
speech utterances from the [WSJ](#Sx4.26) ([WSJ](#Sx4.26))
dataset \[[36](#bib.bib36)\] were played in the room. Through all
recordings, at least 40 dB of sound dynamic range compared to the room
silence was asserted, and a room temperature of
$`24^{\circ}\pm 0.5^{\circ}`$C and $`80\%`$ relative humidity were
registered. In these conditions the speed of sounds is
$`c_{\text{air}}=346.98`$ m/s. In addition, 1 minute of room tone (i.e.,
silence) and 4 minutes of diffuse babble noise were recorded for each
session. The latter was simulated by transmitting different chunks of
the same single-channel babble noise recording from additional
loudspeakers facing the four corners of the room.

All microphone signals were synchronously acquired and digitally
converted to 48 kHz with 32 bit/sample using the equipment listed
in [Table 2](#S2.T2 "In 2 Database Description ‣ dEchorate: a Calibrated Room Impulse Response Database for Echo-aware Signal Processing").
The polarity of each microphone was recorded by clapping a book in the
middle of the room and their gain is corrected using the room tone.

Finally, [RIR](#Sx4.15) are estimated with the [ESS](#Sx4.8)
technique \[[37](#bib.bib37)\] where an exponential time-growing
frequency sweep is using as probe signal. Then, the [RIR](#Sx4.15) is
estimated by devolving the microphone signal, implemented as division in
the frequency domain (The authors used the same code mentioned in
[Footnote 3](#footnote3 "In 2.2 Measurements ‣ 2 Database Description ‣ dEchorate: a Calibrated Room Impulse Response Database for Echo-aware Signal Processing")).

### 2.3 Dataset annotation

#### 2.3.1 RIRs annotation

The objective of this database is to feature annotations in the
“geometrical space”, namely the microphone, facet and source positions,
that are fully consistent with annotations in the “signal space”, namely
the echo timings within the [RIR](#Sx4.15). This is achieved as follows:

1.  (i)
    First, the ground-truth positions of the array and source centres
    are acquired via a Beacon indoor positioning system
    ($`\mathtt{bIPS}`$). This system consists in 4 stationary bases
    positioned at the corners of the ceiling and a movable probe used
    for measurements which can be located within errors of $`\pm 2`$ cm.
2.  (ii)
    The estimated [RIR](#Sx4.15) are superimposed on synthetic
    [RIR](#Sx4.15) computed with the [ISM](#Sx4.19) ([ISM](#Sx4.19))
    from the geometry obtained in the previous step. A Python GUI⁴⁴ 4
    This GUI is available in the dataset package. (showed
    in [Figure 5](#S2.F5 "In The “skyline” visualization ‣ 2.3.2 Other tools for RIRs annotation ‣ 2.3 Dataset annotation ‣ 2 Database Description ‣ dEchorate: a Calibrated Room Impulse Response Database for Echo-aware Signal Processing")),
    is used to manually tune a peak finder and label the echoes
    corresponding to found peaks, that is, annotate their timings and
    their corresponding image source position and room facet label.
3.  (iii)
    By solving a simple [MDS](#Sx4.11) ([MDS](#Sx4.11)) problem
    \[[38](#bib.bib38), [39](#bib.bib39), [40](#bib.bib40)\], refined
    microphone and source positions are computed from echo timings. The
    non-convexity of the problem is alleviated by using a good
    initialization (obtained at the previous step), by the high SNR of
    the measurements and, later, by including additional image sources
    in the formulation. The prior information about the arrays’
    structures reduced the number of variables of the problem, leaving
    the 3D positions of the sources and of the arrays’ barycenters in
    addition to the arrays’ tilt on the azimuthal plane.
4.  (iv)
    By employing a multilateration algorithm \[[41](#bib.bib41)\], where
    the positions of one microphone per array serve as anchors and the
    [TOA](#Sx4.17) are converted into distances, it is possible to
    localize image sources alongside the real sources. This step will be
    further discussed
    in [Section 4](#S4 "4 Using the Data ‣ dEchorate: a Calibrated Room Impulse Response Database for Echo-aware Signal Processing").

Knowing the geometry of the room, in step
[(i)](#S2.I1.i1 "Item (i) ‣ 2.3.1 RIRs annotation ‣ 2.3 Dataset annotation ‣ 2 Database Description ‣ dEchorate: a Calibrated Room Impulse Response Database for Echo-aware Signal Processing")
we were able to initially guess the position of the echoes in the
[RIR](#Sx4.15). Then, by iterating through steps
[(ii)](#S2.I1.i2 "Item (ii) ‣ 2.3.1 RIRs annotation ‣ 2.3 Dataset annotation ‣ 2 Database Description ‣ dEchorate: a Calibrated Room Impulse Response Database for Echo-aware Signal Processing"),
[(iii)](#S2.I1.i3 "Item (iii) ‣ 2.3.1 RIRs annotation ‣ 2.3 Dataset annotation ‣ 2 Database Description ‣ dEchorate: a Calibrated Room Impulse Response Database for Echo-aware Signal Processing")
and
[(iv)](#S2.I1.i4 "Item (iv) ‣ 2.3.1 RIRs annotation ‣ 2.3 Dataset annotation ‣ 2 Database Description ‣ dEchorate: a Calibrated Room Impulse Response Database for Echo-aware Signal Processing"),
the position of the echoes are refined to be consistent under the
[ISM](#Sx4.19).

The final geometrical and signal annotation was chosen as a compromise
between the $`\mathtt{bIPS}`$ measurements and the $`\mathtt{MDS}`$
output. While the former ones are noisy but consistent with the scene’s
geometry, the latter ones match the [TOA](#Sx4.17) but not necessarily
the physical world. In particular, geometrical ambiguities such as
global rotation, translation and up-down flips were observed. Instead of
manually correcting this error, we modified the original problem from
using only the direct path distances ($`\mathtt{dMDS}`$) to considering
the image sources’ [TOA](#Sx4.17) of the ceiling as well in the cost
function ($`\mathtt{dcMDS}`$).
[Table 4](#S2.T4 "In 2.3.1 RIRs annotation ‣ 2.3 Dataset annotation ‣ 2 Database Description ‣ dEchorate: a Calibrated Room Impulse Response Database for Echo-aware Signal Processing")
shows numerically the mismatch (in cm) between the geometric space
(defined by the $`\mathtt{bIPS}`$ measurements) and the signal space
(the one defined by the echo timings, converted to cm based on the speed
of sound). To better quantify it, we introduce here a [GoM](#Sx4.10)
([GoM](#Sx4.10)) metric: it measures the fraction of (first-order) echo
timings annotated in the [RIR](#Sx4.15) matching the annotation produced
by the geometry within a threshold. Including the ceiling information,
$`\mathtt{dcMDS}`$ produces a geometrical configuration which has a
small mismatch (0.4 cm on average, 1.86 cm max) in both the signal and
geometric spaces with a $`98.1\%`$ matching all the first order echoes
within a 0.5 ms threshold (i.e., the position of all the image sources
within about 17 cm error). It is worth noting that the $`\mathtt{bIPS}`$
measurements produce a significantly less consistent annotation with
respect to the signal space.

#### 2.3.2 Other tools for RIRs annotation

Finally, we would like to add that the following tools and techniques
were found useful in annotating the echoes.

##### The “skyline” visualization

consists in presenting the intensity of multiple [RIR](#Sx4.15) as an
image, such that the wavefronts corresponding to echoes can be
highlighted \[[42](#bib.bib42)\]. Let $`h_{n}(l)`$ be an [RIR](#Sx4.15)
from the database, where $`l=0,\ldots,L-1`$ denotes sample index and
$`n=0,\ldots,N-1`$ is an arbitrary indexing of all the microphones for a
fixed room configuration. Then, the skyline is the visualization of the
$`L\times N`$ matrix $`\mathbf{H}`$ created by stacking column-wise

|     |     |     |     |

|     |

       ``` math
       \mathbf{H}_{l,n}=\mid h_{n}(l)\mid/\max{\mid{h_{n}(l)}\mid},
       ```                                                           |     | (1) |

where $`\mid{\cdot}\mid`$ denotes the absolute value.

### 2.4 Limitations of current annotation

As stated in \[[44](#bib.bib44)\], we want to emphasize that annotating
the correct [TOA](#Sx4.17) of echoes and even the direct path in “clean”
real [RIR](#Sx4.15) is far from straightforward. The peaks can be
blurred out by the loudspeaker characteristics or the concurrency of
multiple reflections. Nevertheless, as showed
in [Table 4](#S2.T4 "In 2.3.1 RIRs annotation ‣ 2.3 Dataset annotation ‣ 2 Database Description ‣ dEchorate: a Calibrated Room Impulse Response Database for Echo-aware Signal Processing"),
the proposed annotation was found to be sufficiently consistent both in
the geometric and in the echo/signal space. Thus, no further refinement
was done. This database can be used as a first basis to develop better
[AER](#Sx4.2) methods which could be used to iteratively improve the
annotation, for instance including 2$`{}^{\text{nd}}`$ order
reflections.

### 2.5 The dEchorate package

The dataset comes with both data and code to parse and process it. The
data are presented in 2 modalities: the raw data, that is, the
collection of recorded wave files, are organized in folders and can be
retrieved by querying a simple database table; the processed data, which
comprise the estimated [RIR](#Sx4.15) and the geometrical and signal
annotations, are organized in tensors directly importable in Matlab or
Python (e.g. all the [RIR](#Sx4.15) are stored in a tensor of dimension
$`L\times I\times J\times D`$, respectively corresponding to the RIR
length in samples, the number of microphones, of sources and of room
configurations).  
Together with the data a Python package is available on the same
website. This includes wrappers, GUI, examples as well as the code to
reproduce this study. In particular, all the scripts used for estimating
the [RIR](#Sx4.15) and annotating them are available and can be used to
further improve and enrich the annotation or as baselines for future
works.

## 3 Analysing the Data

In this section we will illustrate some characteristics of the collected
data in term of acoustic descriptors, namely the $`\mathtt{RT}_{60}`$,
the [DRR](#Sx4.6) and the [DER](#Sx4.5) ([DER](#Sx4.5)). While the
former two are classical acoustic descriptors used to evaluate
[SE](#Sx4.20) and [ASR](#Sx4.3) technologies \[[45](#bib.bib45)\], the
latter is less common and used in strongly echoic
situations \[[46](#bib.bib46), [47](#bib.bib47)\].

### 3.1 Reverberation Time

The $`\mathtt{RT}_{60}`$ is the time required for the sound level in a
room to decrease by 60 dB after the source is turned off, thus, it
measures reverberation level. This value is one the most common acoustic
descriptor for room acoustics. Besides, as reverberation affects
detrimentally the performances of speech processing technologies, the
robustness against $`\mathtt{RT}_{60}`$ has become a common evaluation
metric in [SE](#Sx4.20) and [ASR](#Sx4.3).

[Table 5](#S3.T5 "In 3.1 Reverberation Time ‣ 3 Analysing the Data ‣ dEchorate: a Calibrated Room Impulse Response Database for Echo-aware Signal Processing")
reports estimated $`\mathtt{RT}_{60}(b)`$ values per octave band
$`b\in\{500,1000,2000,4000\}`$ (Hz) for each of the room in the dataset.
These values were estimated using the Schroeder’s integration
methods \[[48](#bib.bib48), [49](#bib.bib49), [50](#bib.bib50)\] in each
octave band. For the octave bands centred at 125 Hz and 250 Hz, the
measured [RIR](#Sx4.15) did not exhibit sufficient power for a reliable
estimation. This observation found confirmation in the frequency
response provided by the loudspeakers’ manufacturer, which decays
exponentially from 300 Hz downwards.

Ideally, for the $`\mathtt{RT}_{60}`$ to be reliably estimated, the
Schroeder curve, i.e. the log of the square-integrated,
octave-band-passed [RIR](#Sx4.15), would need to feature a linear decay
for 60 dB of dynamic range, which would occur in an ideal diffuse sound
regime. However, such range is never observable in practice, due to the
presence of noise and possible non-diffuse effects. Hence, a common
technique is to compute, e.g., the $`\mathtt{RT}_{10}`$ on the range
$`[-5,-15]`$ dB of the Schroeder curve and to extrapolate the
$`\mathtt{RT}_{60}`$ by multiplying it by 6. We visually inspected all
the [RIR](#Sx4.15) of the dataset corresponding to directional sources
1, 2 and 3, i.e., 90 [RIR](#Sx4.15) in each of the 10 rooms. Then, two
sets were created. Set $`\mathcal{A}`$ features all the Schroeder curves
featuring linear log-energy decays allowing for reliable
$`\mathtt{RT}_{10}`$ estimates. Set $`\mathcal{B}`$ contains all the
other curves. In practice, $`49\%`$ of the 3600 Schroeder curves were
placed in the set $`\mathcal{B}`$. These mostly correspond to the
challenging measurement conditions purposefully included in our dataset,
i.e., strong early echoes, loudspeakers facing towards reflectors or
receivers close to reflectors. Finally, the $`\mathtt{RT}_{60}`$ value
of each room and octave band was calculated from the median of
$`\mathtt{RT}_{10}`$ corresponding to Schroeder curves in
$`\mathcal{A}`$ only.

As can be seen in

described in

### 3.2 Direct To Early and Reverberant Ratio

In order to characterize an acoustic environment, it is common to
provide the ratio between the energy of the direct and the indirect
propagation paths. In particular, one can compute the so-called
[DRR](#Sx4.6) directly from a measured [RIR](#Sx4.15)
$`h(l)`$ \[[45](#bib.bib45)\] as

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\mathtt{DRR}=10\log_{10}\frac{\sum_{l\in\mathcal{D}}h^{2}(l)}{\sum_{l\in\mathcal{R}}h^{2}l)}\quad[\text{dB}],
``` |  | (2) |

where $`\mathcal{D}`$ denotes the time support comprising the direct
propagation path (set to $`\pm`$120 samples around its time of arrival,
blue part in
[Figure 1](#S1.F1 "In 1 Introduction ‣ dEchorate: a Calibrated Room Impulse Response Database for Echo-aware Signal Processing")),
and $`\mathcal{R}`$ comprises the remainder of the [RIR](#Sx4.15),
including both echoes and late reverberation (orange and green parts in
[Figure 1](#S1.F1 "In 1 Introduction ‣ dEchorate: a Calibrated Room Impulse Response Database for Echo-aware Signal Processing")).

Similarly, the [DER](#Sx4.5) defines the ratio between the energy of the
direct path and the early echoes only, that is,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathtt{DER}=10\log_{10}\frac{\sum_{l\in\mathcal{D}}h^{2}(l)}{\sum_{l\in\mathcal{E}}h^{2}(l)}\quad[\text{dB}],
``` |  | (3) |

where $`\mathcal{E}`$ is the time support of the early echoes only
(green part in
[Figure 1](#S1.F1 "In 1 Introduction ‣ dEchorate: a Calibrated Room Impulse Response Database for Echo-aware Signal Processing")).

Differently from the $`\mathtt{RT}_{60}`$ which mainly describes the
diffuse regime, both [DER](#Sx4.5) and [DRR](#Sx4.6) are highly
dependent on the position of the source and receiver in the room.
Therefore, for each room, wide ranges of these parameters were
registered. For the loudspeakers facing the microphones, the
[DER](#Sx4.5) ranges from 2 dB to 6 dB in one-hot room configurations
and from -2 dB to 6 dB in the most reverberant rooms. Similarly, the
[DRR](#Sx4.6) has a similar trend featuring lower values, such as -2 dB
in one-hot rooms and down to -7.5 dB for the most reverberant ones. A
complete annotation of these metrics is available in the database.

## 4 Using the Data

The dEchorate database is now used to investigate the performance of
state-of-the-art methods on two echo-aware acoustic signal processing
applications on both synthetic and measured data, namely, spatial
filtering and room geometry estimation.

### 4.1 Application: Echo-aware Beamforming

Let $`I`$ microphones acquire to a single static point sound source,
contaminated by noise sources. In the short-time Fourier transform
(STFT) domain, we stack the $`I`$ complex-valued microphone observations
at frequency $`f`$ and time $`t`$ into a vector
$`\mathbf{x}(f,t)\in\mathbb{C}^{I}`$. Let us denote
$`s(f,t)\in\mathbb{C}`$ and $`\mathbf{n}(f,t)\in\mathbb{C}^{I}`$ the
source signal and the noise signals at microphones, which are assumed to
be statistically independent. By denoting
$`\mathbf{h}\in\mathbb{C}^{I}`$ the Fourier transforms of the
[RIR](#Sx4.15), the observed microphone signals in the STFT domain can
be expressed a follows:

|     |                                                      |     |     |
|-----|------------------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \mathbf{x}(f,t)=\mathbf{h}(f)s(f,t)+\mathbf{n}(f,t).
       ```                                                   |     | (4) |

Here, the STFT windows are assumed long enough so that the discrete
convolution-to-multiplication approximation holds well.

Beamforming is one of the most widely used techniques for enhancing
multichannel microphone recordings. The literature on this topic spans
several decades of array processing and a recent review can be found
in \[[51](#bib.bib51)\]. In the frequency domain, the goal of
beamforming is to estimate a set of coefficients
$`\mathbf{w}(f)\in\mathbb{C}^{I}`$ that are applied to
$`\mathbf{x}(f,t)`$, such that
$`s(f,t)\approx\mathbf{w}^{H}\mathbf{x}(f,t)`$. Hereinafter, we will
consider only the distortionless beamformers aiming at retrieving the
clean target speech signal, as it is generated at the source position.

As mentioned throughout the paper, the knowledge of early echoes is
expected to boost spatial filtering performances. However, estimating
these elements is difficult in practice. To quantify this, we compare
echo-agnostic and echo-aware beamformers. In order to study their
empirical potential, we will evaluate their performance using both
synthetic and measured data, as available in the presented dataset.

Echo-agnostic beamformers do not need any echo-estimation step: they
either ignore their contributions, as in the direct-path delay-and-sum
beamformer ($`\mathtt{DS}`$) \[[52](#bib.bib52)\], or they consider
coupling filters between pairs of microphones, called
[ReTF](#Sx4.16) \[[7](#bib.bib7)\]. Note that contrary to
[RIR](#Sx4.15), there exist efficient methods to estimate
[ReTF](#Sx4.16) from multichannel recordings of unknown sources
(see \[[51](#bib.bib51), Section VI.B\] for a review). The
[ReTF](#Sx4.16) can then be naturally incorporated in powerful
beamforming algorithms achieving speech dereverberation and noise
reduction in static \[[53](#bib.bib53)\] and dynamic
scenarios \[[54](#bib.bib54)\]. In this work, [ReTF](#Sx4.16) are
estimated using [GEVD](#Sx4.9) ([GEVD](#Sx4.9))
method \[[55](#bib.bib55)\], using the approach illustanted
in \[[56](#bib.bib56)\].

Echo-aware beamformers fall in the category of rake receivers, borrowing
the idea from telecommunication where an antenna rakes (i.e., combines)
coherent signals arriving from different propagation
paths \[[4](#bib.bib4), [5](#bib.bib5), [6](#bib.bib6)\]. To this end,
they typically consider that for each [RIR](#Sx4.15) $`i`$, the delays
and frequency-independent attenuation coefficients of $`R`$ early echoes
are known, denoted here as $`\tau_{i}^{(r)}`$ and $`\alpha_{i}^{(r)}`$.
In the frequency domain, this translates into the following:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbf{h}(f)=\left[\sum_{r=0}^{R-1}\alpha_{i}^{(r)}\,\exp\left(2\pi jf\tau_{i}^{(r)}\right)\right]_{i},
``` |  | (5) |

where $`r=0,\ldots,R-1`$ denotes the reflection order,

Recently, these methods have been used for noise and interferer
suppression in \[[9](#bib.bib9), [57](#bib.bib57)\] and for noise and
reverberation reduction in \[[58](#bib.bib58), [10](#bib.bib10)\]. The
main limitation of these works is that echo properties, or alternatively
the position of image sources, must be known a priori. Hereafter, we
will assume these properties known by using the annotations of the
dEchorate dataset, as described
in [Section 2.3](#S2.SS3 "2.3 Dataset annotation ‣ 2 Database Description ‣ dEchorate: a Calibrated Room Impulse Response Database for Echo-aware Signal Processing").
In particular, we will assume that the [RIR](#Sx4.15) follow the echo
model ([5](#S4.E5 "Equation 5 ‣ 4.1 Application: Echo-aware Beamforming ‣ 4 Using the Data ‣ dEchorate: a Calibrated Room Impulse Response Database for Echo-aware Signal Processing"))
with $`R=4`$, corresponding to the 4 strongest echoes. Knowing the echo
delays, the associated attenuation coefficients are retrieved from the
[RIR](#Sx4.15) using a simple maximum-likelihood approach, as
in \[[59](#bib.bib59), Eq. 10\].

We evaluate the performance of both types of beamformers on the task of
noise and late reverberation suppression. Different [MVDR](#Sx4.12)
([MVDR](#Sx4.12)) beamformers are considered, assuming either spatially
white noise (i.e., classical $`\mathtt{DS}`$ design), diffuse noise
(i.e., the Capon filter) or diffuse noise plus the late
reverberation \[[60](#bib.bib60)\]. In the latter case, the late
reverberation statistics are modeled by a spatial coherence
matrix \[[61](#bib.bib61)\] weighted by the late reverberation power,
which is estimated using the procedure described
in \[[60](#bib.bib60)\].

Overall, the different [RIR](#Sx4.15) models considered are direct
propagation ($`\mathtt{DP}`$, i.e., ignoring echoes), multipath
propagation ($`\mathtt{Rake}`$, i.e., using 4 known early
echoes) \[[9](#bib.bib9), [10](#bib.bib10)\] or the full reverberant
propagation ($`\mathtt{ReTF}`$) \[[7](#bib.bib7),
[56](#bib.bib56)\].[Table 6](#S4.T6 "In 4.1 Application: Echo-aware Beamforming ‣ 4 Using the Data ‣ dEchorate: a Calibrated Room Impulse Response Database for Echo-aware Signal Processing")
summarizes the considered beamformers designs.

| Acronym | Steering Vectors | Noise Model |
|----|----|----|
| DS \[[52](#bib.bib52)\] | Direct Path AOA | Spatially white n. |
| MVDR-DP \[[52](#bib.bib52)\] | Direct Path AOA | Diffuse n. |
| MVDR-ReTF \[[7](#bib.bib7)\] | [ReTF](#Sx4.16) | Diffuse n. |
| MVDR-Rake\* \[[9](#bib.bib9)\] | 4 Echoes/chan. | Diffuse n. |
| MVDR-DP-Late \[[10](#bib.bib10)\] | Direct Path AOA | Spat.ly white n.+lr. |

| MVDR-Rake-Late\* \[[10](#bib.bib10)\] | 4 Echoes/chan. | Diffuse n. + lr. |

Performances of the different designs are compared on the task of
enhancing a target speech signal in a 5-channel mixture using the
[nULA](#Sx4.13) in the dEchorate dataset. They are tested in scenarios
featuring high reverberation and diffuse babble noise, appropriately
scaled to pre-defined signal-to-noise ratios
$`\mathtt{SNR}\in\{0,10,20\}`$. Using the dEchorate data, we consider
the room configuration $`\mathtt{011111}`$
($`\mathtt{RT}_{60}\approx 600`$ ms) and all possible combinations of
(target, array) positions. Both real and corresponding synthetic
[RIR](#Sx4.15) are used, which are then convolved with anechoic
utterances from the [WSJ](#Sx4.26) corpus \[[36](#bib.bib36)\] and
corrupted by recorded diffuse babble noise. The synthetic [RIR](#Sx4.15)
are computed with the Python library
[pyroomacoustics](https://github.com/LCAV/pyroomacoustics) \[[62](#bib.bib62)\],
based purely on the [ISM](#Sx4.19). Hence, on synthetic [RIR](#Sx4.15),
the known echo timings perfectly match the components in their early
part (no model mismatch).

The evaluation is conducted similarly to the one in \[[10](#bib.bib10)\]
where the following metrics are considered:

- •
  the [iSNRR](#Sx4.23) ([iSNRR](#Sx4.23)) in dB, computed as the
  difference between the input $`\mathtt{SNRR}`$ at the reference
  microphone and the $`\mathtt{SNRR}`$ at the filter output;
- •
  the [iSRMR](#Sx4.24) ([iSRMR](#Sx4.24)) \[[63](#bib.bib63)\] to
  measure dereverberation;
- •
  the [iPESQ](#Sx4.22) ([iPESQ](#Sx4.22)) score \[[64](#bib.bib64)\] to
  assess the perceptual quality of the signal and indirectly the amount
  of artifacts.

Implementations of the SRMR and [PESQ](#Sx4.14) ([PESQ](#Sx4.14))
metrics are available in the Python library
[speechmetrics](https://github.com/aliutkus/speechmetrics/). Both the
[SNRR](#Sx4.21) ([SNRR](#Sx4.21)) and the [PESQ](#Sx4.14) are relative
metrics, meaning they require a target reference signal. Here we
consider the clean target signal as the dry source signal convolved with
the early part of the [RIR](#Sx4.15) (up to $`R`$-th echo) of the
reference (first) microphone. On the one hand, this choice numerically
penalizes both direct-path-based and [ReTF](#Sx4.16)-based beamformers,
which respectively aim at extracting the direct-path signal and the full
reverberant signal in the reference microphone. On the other hand,
considering only the direct path or the full reverberant signal would be
equally unfair for the other beamformers. Moreover, including early
echoes in the target signal is perceptually motivated since they are
known to contribute to speech intelligibility \[[65](#bib.bib65)\].

Numerical results are reported in
[Figure 7](#S4.F7 "In 4.1 Application: Echo-aware Beamforming ‣ 4 Using the Data ‣ dEchorate: a Calibrated Room Impulse Response Database for Echo-aware Signal Processing").
On synthetic data, as expected, one can see that the more information is
used, the better performances are. Including late reverberation
statistics considerably boosts performance in all cases. Both the
[ReTF](#Sx4.16)-based and the echo-aware beamformers significantly
outperform the simple designs based on direct path only. While the two
designs perform comparably in terms of [iSNRR](#Sx4.23) and
[iPESQ](#Sx4.22), the former has a slight edge over the latter in terms
of median [iSRMR](#Sx4.24). A possible explanation is that
[GEVD](#Sx4.9) methods tend to consider the stronger and more stable
components of the [ReTF](#Sx4.16), which in the considered scenarios may
identify with the earlier portion of the [RIR](#Sx4.15). Moreover, since
it is not constrained by a fixed echo model, the [ReTF](#Sx4.16) can
capture more information, e.g., frequency-dependent attenuation
coefficients. Finally, one should consider the compacity of the
model ([5](#S4.E5 "Equation 5 ‣ 4.1 Application: Echo-aware Beamforming ‣ 4 Using the Data ‣ dEchorate: a Calibrated Room Impulse Response Database for Echo-aware Signal Processing"))
with respect to the [ReTF](#Sx4.16) model in terms of the number of
parameters to be estimated. In fact, when considering 4 echoes, only 8
parameters per channel are needed, as opposed to several hundreds for
the [ReTF](#Sx4.16) (ideally, as many as the number of frequency bins
per channel).

When it comes to measured [RIR](#Sx4.15), however, the trends are
different. Here, the errors in echo timings due to calibration mismatch
and the richness of real acoustic propagation lead to a drop in
performance for echo-aware methods, both in terms of means and
variances. This is clearest when considering the [iPESQ](#Sx4.22)
metric, which also accounts for artifacts. the echo-agnostic beamformer
considering late reverberation $`\mathtt{MVDR\text{-}ReTF\text{-}Late}`$
outperforms the other methods, maintaining the trend exhibited on
simulated data. Finally, conversely to the
$`\mathtt{MVDR\text{-}ReTF\text{-}Late}`$, the
$`\mathtt{MVDR\text{-}Rake\text{-}Late}`$ yields a significant portion
of negative performances. As already observed in \[[10](#bib.bib10)\],
this is probably due to the tiny annotation mismatches in echo timings
as well as the fact that their frequency-dependent strengths, induced by
reflective surfaces, are not modeled in rake beamformers. This suggests
that in order to be applicable to real conditions, future work in
echo-aware beamforming should include finer blind estimates of early
echo properties from signals, as investigated in, e.g.,
\[[66](#bib.bib66), [35](#bib.bib35)\].

### 4.2 Application: Room Geometry Estimation

The shape of a convex room can be estimated knowing the positions of
first-order image sources. Several methods have been proposed which take
into account different levels of prior information and noise
(see \[[23](#bib.bib23), [67](#bib.bib67)\] for a review). When the
echoes’ [TOA](#Sx4.17) and their labeling are known for 4 non-coplanar
microphones, one can perform this task using simple geometrical
reasoning as in \[[21](#bib.bib21)\]. In details, the 3D coordinates of
each image source can be retrieved solving a multilateration problem
 \[[68](#bib.bib68)\], namely the extension of the trilateration problem
to 3D space, where the goal is to estimate the relative position of an
object based on the measurement of it distance with respect to anchor
points. Finally, the position and orientation of each room facet can be
easily derived from the [ISM](#Sx4.19) equations as the plane bisecting
the line joining the real source position and the position of its
corresponding image (see
[Figure 8](#S4.F8 "In 4.2 Application: Room Geometry Estimation ‣ 4 Using the Data ‣ dEchorate: a Calibrated Room Impulse Response Database for Echo-aware Signal Processing"))

geometry estimation results in 1.15 cm [DE](#Sx4.4) and $`2.6^{\circ}`$

The small errors are due to a concurrency of multiple factors, such as
tiny offsets in the annotations and the ideal shoebox approximation. In
the real recording room, some gaps were present between revolving panels
in the room facet. In addition, it is possible that for some (image
source, receiver) pairs the far-field assumption is not verified,
causing inaccuracies when inverting the [ISM](#Sx4.19). The 2 outliers
for source $`\#3`$ are due to a wrong annotation caused by the source
directivity which induced an echo mislabeling. When a wall is right
behind a source, the energy of the related $`1^{\text{st}}`$ reflection
is very small and might not appear in the [RIR](#Sx4.15). This happened
for the eastern wall and a second order image was taken instead.
Finally, the contribution of multiple reflections arriving at the same
time can result in large late spikes in estimated [RIR](#Sx4.15). This
effect is particularly amplified when the microphone and loudspeakers
exhibit long impulse responses. As a consequence, some spikes can be
miss-classified. This happened for the southern-wall where again a
second-order image was taken instead. Note that such echo mislabelings
can either be corrected manually or using Euclidean distance matrix
criteria as proposed in \[[21](#bib.bib21)\]. Overall, this experiment
illustrates well the interesting challenge of estimating and exploiting
acoustic echoes in [RIR](#Sx4.15) when typical sources and receivers
with imperfect characteristics are used.

## 5 Conclusions and Perspectives

This paper introduced a new database of room impulse responses featuring
accurate annotation of early echo timings that are consistent with
source, microphone and room facet positions. These data can be used to
test methods in the room geometry estimation pipeline and in echo-aware
audio signal processing. In particular, robustness of these methods can
be validated against different levels of $`\mathtt{RT}_{60}`$,
$`\mathtt{SNR}`$, surface reflectivity, proximity, or early echo
density.

This dataset paves the way to a number of interesting future research
directions. By making this dataset freely available to the audio signal
processing community, we hope to foster research in [AER](#Sx4.2) and
echo-aware signal processing in order to improve the performance of
existing methods on real data. Moreover, the dataset could be updated by
including more robust annotations derived from more advanced algorithms
for calibration and [AER](#Sx4.2).

In addition, the data analysis conducted in this work brings the
attention to exploring the impact of mismatch between simulated and real
[RIR](#Sx4.15) on audio signal processing methods. Finally, by using the
pairs of simulated vs. real [RIR](#Sx4.15) available in the dataset, it
should be possible to develop techniques to convert one to the other,
using style transfer or domain adaptation techniques, thus opening the
way to new types of learning-based acoustic simulators.
