# BINAURAL SPEECH ENHANCEMENT USING COMPLEX CONVOLUTIONAL RECURRENT NETWORKS

Vikas Tokala    Eric Grinstein    Mike Brookes    Simon Doclo    Jesper
Jensen    Patrick A. Naylor \sthanksThis work was supported by funding
from the European Union’s Horizon 2020 research and innovation programme
under the Marie Skłodowska-Curie grant agreement No 956369 and the UK
Engineering and Physical Sciences Research Council \[grant number
EP/S035842/1\]

###### Abstract

From hearing aids to augmented and virtual reality devices, binaural
speech enhancement algorithms have been established as state-of-the-art
techniques to improve speech intelligibility and listening comfort. In
this paper, we present an end-to-end binaural speech enhancement method
using a complex recurrent convolutional network with an encoder-decoder
architecture and a complex LSTM recurrent block placed between the
encoder and decoder. A loss function that focuses on the preservation of
spatial information in addition to speech intelligibility improvement
and noise reduction is introduced. The network estimates individual
complex ratio masks for the left and right-ear channels of a binaural
hearing device in the time-frequency domain. We show that, compared to
other baseline algorithms, the proposed method significantly improves
the estimated speech intelligibility and reduces the noise while
preserving the spatial information of the binaural signals in acoustic
situations with a single target speaker and isotropic noise of various
types.

Binaural speech enhancement, Complex Convolutional Neural Networks,
recurrent networks, interaural cues, noise reduction.

## 1 Introduction

Binaural speech enhancement has gained significant attention as a
state-of-the-art approach for enhancing speech in various applications,
including hearing aids and augmented/virtual reality devices
\[[1](#bib.bib1), [2](#bib.bib2)\]. Binaural signals preserve the
spatial characteristics of sounds which help listeners in noisy acoustic
environments achieve better speech intelligibility and accurate sound
source localization \[[3](#bib.bib3)\]. The fundamental spatial cues
that help in localizing sounds and improving intelligibility are
[ILD](#id8) ([ILD](#id8)) and [IPD](#id9) ([IPD](#id9)) or [ITD](#id10)
([ITD](#id10)) \[[4](#bib.bib4)\]. Previously proposed methods for
binaural speech enhancement include multichannel Wiener filters
\[[5](#bib.bib5), [6](#bib.bib6)\], beamforming-based
\[[1](#bib.bib1)\], and mask-based enhancement methods \[[7](#bib.bib7),
[8](#bib.bib8)\]. Binaural speech separation using time-domain
[CED](#id41) ([CED](#id41)) was proposed in \[[9](#bib.bib9)\]. Recent
advancements in deep learning techniques have led to remarkable
improvements in monaural speech enhancement. These methods, whether
applied in the time domain \[[10](#bib.bib10), [11](#bib.bib11)\] or the
[TF](#id23) ([TF](#id23)) domain \[[12](#bib.bib12), [13](#bib.bib13),
[14](#bib.bib14)\], demonstrate impressive results.

Spectrograms are commonly used as input to networks in the [TF](#id23)
domain for speech enhancement \[[7](#bib.bib7), [14](#bib.bib14),
[15](#bib.bib15)\]. Many methods in this domain focus on enhancing only
the magnitude of the spectrogram while using the noisy phase information
for reconstructing the enhanced speech signal \[[7](#bib.bib7),
[16](#bib.bib16)\]. To address optimal phase estimation and signal
reconstruction, some approaches jointly estimate both magnitude and
phase by utilizing complex-valued spectrograms. These methods have shown
promising results and can outperform real-valued networks in monaural
speech enhancement \[[16](#bib.bib16), [15](#bib.bib15)\].

The [CRN](#id43) ([CRN](#id43)) introduced in \[[13](#bib.bib13)\]
employed a [CED](#id41) ([CED](#id41)) architecture with [LSTM](#id44)
([LSTM](#id44)) blocks placed in between the encoder and decoder. In
\[[15](#bib.bib15)\], a deep complex [CRN](#id43) was trained to
optimize the [SI-SNR](#id60) ([SI-SNR](#id60)) for monaural speech
signals. However, using a similar approach for binaural signals could be
damaging to the interaural cues. More specifically, for the case of
binaural signals, phase information is vital for preserving the
[IPD](#id9) values and the enhanced signals should preserve level
differences as the noisy signal to retain [ILD](#id8). While the model
may effectively reduce noise and enhance speech intelligibility,
modifying the level and phase information could potentially impact the
spatial information of the target, leading to a compromised ability for
localization and spatial awareness \[[4](#bib.bib4), [17](#bib.bib17)\].
In \[[18](#bib.bib18)\], a complex convolutional attention-based
transformer network has been proposed which uses a similar loss function
based on interaural cues.

Our proposed method, [BCCRN](#id61) ([BCCRN](#id61)), uses a
complex-valued [CED](#id41)-based recurrent network for binaural speech
enhancement and introduces terms in the loss function to improve speech
intelligibility while preserving the interaural cues based on human
perception of the target speech signal with a smaller complex recurrent
network compared to \[[18](#bib.bib18)\].

## 2 [BCCRN](#id61) Model Architecture

Figure 1: Model architecture of [BCCRN](#id61) ([BCCRN](#id61)).

The proposed [BCCRN](#id61) is trained to estimate individual
[CRM](#id30) ([CRM](#id30)) for each channel. The block diagram of the
model architecture is shown in
Fig. [1](#S2.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 2 Model Architecture ‣ BINAURAL SPEECH ENHANCEMENT USING COMPLEX CONVOLUTIONAL RECURRENT NETWORKS").
The [STFT](#id7) ([STFT](#id7)) blocks transform the input signal into
the [TF](#id23) domain. The encoder block is made of 6 complex
convolutional layers with [PReLU](#id54) ([PReLU](#id54)) activation and
employs batch normalization. Separate encoder and decoder blocks are
used for the left and right-ear channels to estimate the individual
[CRM](#id30)s. The decoder block consists of 6 complex convolutional
layers which are symmetric in design to the layers in the encoder to
reconstruct the signal. The encoder extracts high-level features from
the input spectrograms and reduces the resolution of the input. The
decoder rebuilds the low-resolution features back to the original size.
The encoded information from the left and right encoder blocks is
concatenated and provided to the complex LSTM block. The [LSTM](#id44)
layers are designed to model the frequency dependencies. Skip
connections are placed between the encoder and decoder layers based on
the [CRN](#id43) architecture which improves the information flow and
facilitates network optimization \[[13](#bib.bib13)\]. The individual
decoders output individual [CRM](#id30)s that are applied to the left
and right channels of the noisy binaural signal for enhancement. The
[ISTFT](#id22) ([ISTFT](#id22)) blocks transform the signal back into
the time domain.

## 3 Signal Model and Loss Function

The noisy time-domain input signal for the right channel, $`y_{R}`$, is
given by

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       y_{R}(t)=s_{R}(t)+v_{R}(t),
       ```                          |     | (1) |

where $`s_{R}`$ is the anechoic clean speech signal, $`v_{R}`$ is the
noise and $`t`$ is the time index. The [STFT](#id7) is used to transform
the signals into the [TF](#id23) domain and the respective [TF](#id23)
representations are $`Y_{R}(k,\ell)`$, $`S_{R}(k,\ell)`$ and
$`V_{R}(k,\ell)`$ with $`k`$ and $`\ell`$ being the frequency and time
frame indices respectively. The left channel is described similarly with
an $`L`$ subscript. For brevity, the $`L`$ and $`R`$ indices are omitted
from the remainder of this paper. The estimated [CRM](#id30),
$`M(k,\ell)`$ is applied to the noisy signal $`Y(k,\ell)`$ to obtain the
enhanced speech signal $`\hat{S}`$. Individual channels are enhanced by
applying the estimated [CRM](#id30), $`\left({M}_{r}+j{M}_{i}\right)`$
to the complex-valued noisy signal $`\left({Y}_{r}+j{Y}_{i}\right)`$ in
the [TF](#id23) domain (omitting $`k`$ and $`\ell`$ indices),

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\hat{S}_{r}+j\hat{S}_{i}=\left({M}_{r}+j{M}_{i}\right)\cdot\left({Y}_{r}+j{Y}_{i}\right),
``` |  | (2) |

where $`r`$ and $`i`$ indicate the real and imaginary parts. The
computed [CRM](#id30) is given by

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
{M}_{r}+j{M}_{i}=\frac{\hat{S}_{r}+j\hat{S}_{i}}{{Y}_{r}+j{Y}_{i}}=\frac{{Y}_{r}\hat{S}_{r}+{Y}_{i}\hat{S}_{i}}{Y_{r}^{2}+Y_{i}^{2}}+j\frac{{Y}_{r}\hat{S}_{i}-{Y}_{i}\hat{S}_{r}}{Y_{r}^{2}+Y_{i}^{2}}.
``` |  | (3) |

### 3.1 Loss Function

The loss function for model training consists of four terms and
optimizes the network for noise reduction, intelligibility improvement,
and interaural cue preservation. The loss function $`\mathcal{L}`$ is
given by

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}=\alpha\mathcal{L}_{SNR}+\beta\mathcal{L}_{STOI}+\gamma\mathcal{L}_{ILD}+\kappa\mathcal{L}_{IPD},
``` |  | (4) |

where $`\mathcal{L}_{SNR}`$ is the [SNR](#id21) ([SNR](#id21)) loss,
$`\mathcal{L}_{STOI}`$ is the [STOI](#id4) ([STOI](#id4))
\[[19](#bib.bib19)\] loss, and $`\mathcal{L}_{ILD}`$ and
$`\mathcal{L}_{IPD}`$ are the proposed [ILD](#id8) and [IPD](#id9) error
losses which are functions of both $`\hat{S}_{L}`$ and $`\hat{S}_{R}`$.
The parameters $`\alpha`$, $`\beta`$, $`\gamma`$, and $`\kappa`$ are the
weights applied to each term.

$`\mathcal{L}_{SNR}`$ is defined as the mean of the left and right-ear
channel values and append a negative sign to maximize the [SNR](#id21)
value, such that
$`\mathcal{L}_{SNR}=-\left(\acs{SNR}_{L}+\acs{SNR}_{R}\right)/2`$. The
[SNR](#id21) of the enhanced signal, $`\hat{\mathbf{s}}`$, is defined as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\acs{SNR}(\mathbf{s},\hat{\mathbf{s}})=10\log_{10}\left(\frac{\lVert\mathbf{s}\rVert^{2}}{\lVert\mathbf{e}_{noise}\rVert^{2}}\right),
``` |  | (5) |

where $`\mathbf{e}_{noise}=\hat{\mathbf{s}}-\mathbf{s}`$ with
$`\mathbf{s}`$ and $`\hat{\mathbf{s}}`$ being the clean and enhanced
signal vectors respectively, and $`\lVert.\rVert`$ is the L2 norm.

To optimize the intelligibility of the enhanced speech signals,
[STOI](#id4) is computed for left and right channels individually and
averaged, and a negative sign is appended to maximize the [STOI](#id4)
so that
$`\mathcal{L}_{STOI}~=~-\left(\acs{STOI}_{L}+\acs{STOI}_{R}\right)/2`$\[[20](#bib.bib20)\].
Including $`\mathcal{L}_{STOI}`$ helps the network optimize individual
[CRM](#id30)s to maximize intelligibility in the enhanced signals.

To ensure the preservation of interaural cues in the enhanced binaural
speech using two separate [CRM](#id30)s, minimizing the [ILD](#id8) and
[IPD](#id9) errors of the target speech is enforced using the loss
function. The [ILD](#id8) and [IPD](#id9) for the clean speech signal
are given by

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
ILD_{S}(k,\ell)=20\log_{10}\left(\frac{|S_{L}(k,\ell)|}{|S_{R}(k,\ell)|}\right),
``` |  | (6) |

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
IPD_{S}(k,\ell)=\arctan\left(\frac{S_{L}(k,\ell)}{S_{R}(k,\ell)}\right).
``` |  | (7) |

The [ILD](#id8) and [IPD](#id9) for the enhanced speech, $`\hat{S}`$,
are calculated similarly. In order to restrict the [ILD](#id8) and
[IPD](#id9) errors to the speech-active regions, an [IBM](#id47)
([IBM](#id47)) \[[21](#bib.bib21)\] $`\mathcal{M}`$ is computed by
selecting the [TF](#id23) bins which have energy above a threshold. The
energy $`E(k,\ell)`$ of the clean signal is given by

|     |                                           |     |     |
|-----|-------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       E(k,\ell)=10\log_{10}{|{S}(k,\ell)|}^{2}.
       ```                                        |     | (8) |

The [IBM](#id47) $`\mathcal{M}(k,\ell)`$ that defines the speech active
[TF](#id23) tiles is then defined as,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{M}(k,\ell)=\begin{cases}1&E(k,\ell)>\max_{\ell}\left(E(k,\ell)\right)-\mathcal{T}\\
0&\mathrm{otherwise}.\end{cases}
``` |  | (9) |

where $`\max_{l}\left(E(k,\ell)\right)`$ is the maximum energy computed
for each frequency bin, $`k`$. Individual [IBM](#id47)s,
$`\mathcal{M}_{L}`$ and $`\mathcal{M}_{R}`$ are computed for the left
and right-ear channels. The final mask $`\mathcal{M}`$ is obtained by
choosing the bins that have energy above the threshold,
$`\max_{\ell}\left(E(k,\ell)\right)-\mathcal{T}`$, in both channels and
is given by

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{M}(k,\ell)=\mathcal{M}_{L}(k,\ell)\odot\mathcal{M}_{R}(k,\ell),
``` |  | (10) |

where $`\odot`$ denotes the Hadamard product. For training and
evaluation $`\mathcal{T}=20`$ dB was used \[[21](#bib.bib21)\]. The
human auditory system interprets [ILD](#id8)s and [IPD](#id9)s
differently based on the frequency range. Human spatial hearing relies
primarily on interaural phase difference cues for frequencies below 1500
Hz, while interaural level difference cues play a crucial role for
frequencies above 1500 Hz \[[4](#bib.bib4)\]. From the estimated
[IBM](#id47) $`\mathcal{M}(k,\ell)`$, separate masks for [ILD](#id8) and
[IPD](#id9) cues are computed based on human spatial hearing. For
choosing the [IPD](#id9) cues in the speech active bins below
$`f_{p}=1500`$ Hz, $`\mathcal{M}(k,\ell)`$ for $`k~\leqslant~K_{p}`$ is
selected where $`K_{p}=f_{p}\times N_{fft}/f_{s}`$ with $`N_{fft}`$ and
$`f_{s}`$ being the FFT length and sampling frequency respectively.
Similarly, for choosing the [ILD](#id8) cues $`\mathcal{M}(k,\ell)`$ for
$`k~>~K_{p}`$ is selected. The $`\mathcal{L}_{ILD}`$ and
$`\mathcal{L}_{IPD}`$ terms are given by

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{ILD}=\frac{1}{N_{ld}}\sum_{k>K_{p},\ell}\mathcal{M}(k,\ell)\left(|ILD_{S}(k,\ell)-ILD_{\hat{S}}(k,\ell)|\right),
``` |  | (11) |

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{IPD}=\frac{1}{N_{pd}}\sum_{k\leqslant K_{p},\ell}\mathcal{M}(k,\ell)|IPD_{S}(k,\ell)-IPD_{\hat{S}}(k,\ell)|
``` |  | (12) |

where $`N_{ld}=\sum_{k>K_{p},\ell}\mathcal{M}(k,\ell)`$ and
$`N_{pd}=\sum_{k\leqslant K_{p},\ell}\mathcal{M}(k,\ell)`$ are the total
number of speech-active frequency and time bins determined from the mask
for [ILD](#id8) and [IPD](#id9) cues respectively. To preserve the
interaural cues of the target speaker, the network optimization is
guided by using masks based on the target speech. The errors in
[ILD](#id8) and [IPD](#id9) are calculated in the time-frequency domain,
while losses related to [SNR](#id21) and [STOI](#id4) are computed in
the time domain through waveform synthesis using the [ISTFT](#id22).

## 4 Experiments

### 4.1 Datasets

To generate binaural speech data, monaural clean speech signals were
acquired from the CSTR VCTK corpus \[[22](#bib.bib22)\] and then
spatialized using [HRIRs](#id51) ([HRIRs](#id51)) from
\[[23](#bib.bib23)\]. The selected speech corpus contains approximately
13 hours of spoken English data recorded by 110 speakers with diverse
accents. From this dataset, each two-second utterance was converted into
binaural form with distinct left and right-ear channels. The dataset
consisted of 20,000 speech utterances that were divided into training,
validation, and testing sets. Diffuse isotropic noise was generated
using noise signals from the NOISEX - 92 database \[[24](#bib.bib24)\].
Uncorrelated noise sources were evenly placed at intervals of
$`5^{\circ}`$ in the azimuthal plane to create isotropic
noise\[[8](#bib.bib8)\] using [HRIRs](#id51) from \[[23](#bib.bib23)\].
For generating binaural signals, the target speech was placed randomly
within the frontal plane ($`-90^{\circ}`$ to $`+90^{\circ}`$), utilizing
[HRIRs](#id51) recorded with a HATS \[[23](#bib.bib23)\]. For training,
isotropic noise was added to the VCTK corpus\[[22](#bib.bib22)\] so that
$`(SNR_{L}+SNR_{R})/2`$ lies between -7 dB and 16 dB. The noise types
used for training are [WGN](#id24) ([WGN](#id24)), [SSN](#id52)
([SSN](#id52)), factory noise, and office noise. An unseen engine noise
type was included in the evaluation set. The datasets were generated in
the anechoic condition. The evaluation set comprises speech signals from
both the VCTK corpus \[[22](#bib.bib22)\] and the TIMIT corpus
\[[25](#bib.bib25)\]. In this set, random target azimuths in the frontal
azimuthal plane are assigned, and isotropic noise is introduced at
varying [SNR](#id21)s ranging from -6 dB to 15 dB. The speaker was
positioned at a distance of either 0.8 m or 3 m randomly at a fixed
elevation of $`0^{\circ}`$.

### 4.2 Training setup

To compute the [STFT](#id7), an FFT length of 512, a window length of
25 ms, and a hop length of 6.25 ms were employed. A sampling rate of
16 kHz was utilized for all signals.

[BCCRN](#id61) ([BCCRN](#id61)): The number of channels used in the
model’s convolutional layers for the complex-valued encoder and decoder
block layers are $`\{32,64,128,256,256,256\}`$, with a stride of 2 in
the frequency and 1 in the time dimension with a kernel size of (5,1)
and all the convolutions in these layers are causal. 8 layers of
bidirectional complex-valued LSTMs with a hidden size of 128 was used.
The model was implemented with Pytorch which provides native complex
data support for most of the functions. The linear layer placed after
the recurrent block has an input and output feature size of 1024. The
Pytorch model was trained using the Adam optimizer, an initial learning
rate of 0.001, and a multi-step learning rate scheduler to modify the
learning rate with the validation loss. The model has around 5.7 million
parameters and was trained for 100 epochs with an additional early
stopping condition of no improvement in the validation loss for three
consecutive epochs. The weights for the loss function
$`\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\kappa`$ in
([4](#S3.E4 "In 3.1 Loss Function ‣ 3 Signal Model and Loss Function ‣ BINAURAL SPEECH ENHANCEMENT USING COMPLEX CONVOLUTIONAL RECURRENT NETWORKS"))
were assigned as {1,10,1,10} respectively. These weight values were
selected to standardize the units of each individual loss function term.
The terms involving SNR and ILD are computed in dB, IPD is calculated in
radians and STOI is a bounded score ranging from 0 to 1. The model was
trained with the proposed loss function described in
([4](#S3.E4 "In 3.1 Loss Function ‣ 3 Signal Model and Loss Function ‣ BINAURAL SPEECH ENHANCEMENT USING COMPLEX CONVOLUTIONAL RECURRENT NETWORKS")),
named [BCCRN](#id61)-SILP, and for comparison, the model was trained to
maximize the [SNR](#id21), named [BCCRN](#id61)-S from
([5](#S3.E5 "In 3.1 Loss Function ‣ 3 Signal Model and Loss Function ‣ BINAURAL SPEECH ENHANCEMENT USING COMPLEX CONVOLUTIONAL RECURRENT NETWORKS")).

### 4.3 Baselines

[BSOBM](#id55) ([BSOBM](#id55)): A binaural speech enhancement method
using [STOI](#id4)-optimal masks proposed in \[[7](#bib.bib7)\]. Here a
feed-forward [DNN](#id13) ([DNN](#id13)) was trained to estimate a
[STOI](#id4)-optimal continuous-valued mask to enhance binaural signals
using dynamically programmed [HSWOBM](#id6) ([HSWOBM](#id6)) as the
training target \[[7](#bib.bib7)\]. To preserve the [ILD](#id8)s, a
better-ear mask was computed by choosing the maximum of the two masks.
The mask is used to supply [SPP](#id18) ([SPP](#id18)) to an
[OM-LSA](#id12) ([OM-LSA](#id12)) enhancer. The model was trained and
evaluated on the same dataset as the proposed model.

[BiTasNet](#id59) ([BiTasNet](#id59)): A time-domain [CED](#id41)-based
network for binaural speech separation which was introduced in
\[[9](#bib.bib9)\]. The best-performing version of the model, the
parallel encoder with mask and sum, was modified and retrained for
single-speaker binaural speech enhancement. The network was trained to
maximize [SNR](#id21)\[[9](#bib.bib9)\]. The encoder and decoders in the
model had a size of 128, a feature dimension of 128, kernel size of 3
and 12 layers. All other parameters were adapted from the original
article and the model has a size of 7 million parameters. The model was
trained and evaluated on the same dataset used for the proposed method.

noise. The model has better performance when trained to optimize the
[SNR](#id21) compared to the proposed loss function and provides an
additional 1 dB of improvement in [fwSegSNR](#id2) on average. A maximum
improvement of about 14 dB [fwSegSNR](#id2) is observed in the noisy
input [SNR](#id21)s and the amount of improvement observed tends to
decrease as the input [SNR](#id21) of the noisy signal improves for both
the [BCCRN](#id61) versions.

The proposed model and loss function have the lowest error for both
cues. The suggested model utilizing the [SNR](#id21) loss function
demonstrates comparable performance to the proposed loss function in
reducing noise, but it does not prioritize preserving the interaural
differences. The inclusion of additional terms in the loss function aids
the network in better maintaining interaural differences. The observed
[ILD](#id8) error was under 1 dB and the [IPD](#id9) error was under
$`10^{\circ}`$ for all input [SNR](#id21)s of the noisy signal. Also,
the [ILD](#id8) and [IPD](#id9) errors for the proposed method tend to
decrease with increasing input [SNR](#id21) while this is not observed
in the model with [SNR](#id21) loss function and other methods. Figure

function had the best performance for all [SNR](#id21)s and provided an

[BCCRN](#id61)-S has a lower intelligibility score even though it has
the best noise reduction performance. Despite its improved ability to

intelligibility score as measured by [MBSTOI](#id19) shown in

Informal listening tests revealed that the [BiTasNet](#id59) produced
more artefacts and reduced intelligibility of the speech. Similar to
[fwSegSNR](#id2) and the error in interaural cues, [BSOBM](#id55) has
the lowest [MBSTOI](#id19) score for enhanced signals. A common trend
observed in binaural speech enhancement methods is the degradation of
the [MBSTOI](#id19) score at high input [SNR](#id21)s due to processing
\[[7](#bib.bib7)\] as the input signals inherently have a higher
[MBSTOI](#id19) score. However, the proposed loss function does not
deteriorate the [MBSTOI](#id19) score, preserves the intelligibility,
and provides an improvement at all [SNR](#id21)s. Audio examples of all
the methods can be found online ¹¹ 1
[https://vikastokala.github.io/bccrn/](https://vikastokala.github.io/bccrn/).

## 6 Conclusion

In this paper, an end-to-end binaural speech enhancement method using a
complex convolutional recurrent network is proposed. A loss function
that optimizes the network for noise reduction, speech intelligibility,
and human perception-based interaural cue preservation is proposed. The
results of the experiments indicate that the suggested technique
successfully reduced noise while preserving [ILD](#id8) and [IPD](#id9)
information in the enhanced output. Additionally, the proposed method
yielded better estimated binaural speech intelligibility compared to the
baseline methods.
