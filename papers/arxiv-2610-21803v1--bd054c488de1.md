# Rethinking Batch Composition: Promoting feature diversity for Speech Enhancement

Mingchi Hou    Ina Kodrasi ^(†)^(†)thanks: This work was supported by
the Swiss National Science Foundation project 200021_215187 on
“Pathological Speech Enhancement”.

###### Abstract

Speech enhancement systems are commonly trained using randomly sampled
mini-batches, which may contain limited diversity in intrinsic acoustic
characteristics of the clean speech signals. Motivated by previous
findings linking clean speech characteristics to enhancement
performance, we propose a feature-stratified mini-batch sampling
strategy that divides training examples into quantiles according to a
clean-speech characteristic and enforces equal representation within
each mini-batch. Experiments across two large-scale English and Spanish
speech datasets and a smaller dataset containing neurotypical and
pathological speech demonstrate improvements over standard random
mini-batch sampling across multiple objective enhancement metrics.
Ablation studies demonstrate that the gains are robust across training
runs, while their magnitude depends on the acoustic characteristic used
for stratification. The proposed strategy requires no modification to
the enhancement model or training objective, providing a simple and
complementary approach to speech enhancement training.

speech enhancement, mini-batch composition, intrinsic acoustic feature

## 1 Introduction

Speech enhancement (SE) aims to improve the quality and intelligibility
of speech degraded by noise, reverberation, and other acoustic
distortions. A broad range of deep learning-based approaches has been
developed for this task, including discriminative and generative
approaches. Discriminative approaches learn a mapping from degraded to
clean speech through, e.g., spectral mask estimation \[1\] or spectral
coefficient estimation \[2\], whereas generative approaches formulate SE
as a probabilistic generation problem using diffusion \[3\], flow
matching \[4\], or Schrödinger Bridge \[5\]. Despite their overall
strong performance, these approaches can exhibit substantial performance
differences across input conditions, with a range of factors, such as
e.g., acoustic degradation (noise type, signal-to-noise ratio
(SNR)) \[6\] or characteristics of the target speaker \[7\],
contributing to this variability.

This challenge has motivated efforts to improve SE robustness and
generalization. For example, robustness to different acoustic conditions
has been addressed by explicitly accounting for noise type and SNR \[8,
9\] or by using mixture-of-experts architectures in which different
model components can specialize to different acoustic conditions \[10\].
To achieve consistent performance across speakers, speaker-aware and
personalized SE approaches have been proposed that condition enhancement
on speaker embeddings or that adapt models to individual speakers \[7,
11, 12, 13\]. Beyond explicitly targeting robustness to acoustic
conditions or different speakers, improvements to SE generalization have
also been pursued through alternative loss functions \[14\], training
targets \[15, 16\], and model architectures \[17, 18\], as well as
through strategies for improving the quality and scale of training
data \[19\].

Despite these advances, recent studies have shown that certain speech
samples remain challenging for state-of-the-art SE models, even when the
noise type, SNR, and speaker are fixed \[20, 21, 22\]. This suggests
that an important source of enhancement difficulty lies in the intrinsic
characteristics of the clean speech signal itself. In our previous
work \[22\], we demonstrated that clean speech characteristics such as
formant structure and loudness can be strongly correlated with SE
performance, even when the noise type, SNR, and speaker are controlled.
To the best of our knowledge, this source of variability has never been
considered in the design of SE training strategies. Specifically,
training data are typically presented to SE models through randomly
sampled mini-batches, without explicitly considering the distribution of
intrinsic speech characteristics within each batch. Although random
sampling provides unbiased coverage of the training set over many
iterations, individual mini-batches can still differ substantially in
their acoustic composition. For speech data with high intrinsic
variability, a randomly sampled batch may consequently cover only a
limited region of the acoustic feature space, causing individual
gradient updates to be dominated by a restricted range of enhancement
difficulty.

In this work, we propose to explicitly control the distribution of
intrinsic clean speech characteristics within each mini-batch to improve
SE training and performance. To this end, we propose a simple
plug-and-play feature-stratified sampling strategy. For each training
sample, we extract a user-defined clean speech characteristic (such as
e.g., the mean formant frequency or the mean loudness) and partition the
training data into quantiles based on its value. Each mini-batch is then
constructed with an equal number of samples from each quantile.
Importantly, the selected characteristic is used solely to determine the
composition of the mini-batch and is not provided as an input to the SE
model. We evaluate the proposed strategy on discriminative and
generative SE approaches across two languages, i.e., English and
Spanish, and across two speaker populations, i.e., neuortypical and
pathological. Across these various datasets, we observe consistent
improvements in multiple objective SE metrics when the proposed
feature-stratified sampling strategy is used for training.

The main contributions of this work are:

1.  1.  We introduce feature-stratified batch sampling for SE, a simple
        plug-and-play training strategy that explicitly promotes diversity
        in intrinsic clean speech characteristics within each mini-batch.
2.  2.  We demonstrate consistent performance improvements across two SE
        paradigms, two languages, and two speaker groups, using four
        objective SE metrics.
3.  3.  We show that the improvements are robust to stochastic variations in
        mini-batch sampling, and that their magnitude depends on the clean
        speech characteristic used for stratification. In particular,
        characteristics that are strongly associated with SE performance
        provide greater benefits, suggesting that stratification is most
        effective when it promotes diversity along acoustic dimensions
        related to enhancement difficulty.

## 2 Speech Enhancement

The noisy microphone signal $`y(t)`$ at time $`t`$ is given by the sum
of the clean speech signal $`s(t)`$ and the noise signal $`n(t)`$, i.e.,

```math
y(t)=s(t)+n(t). \tag{1}
```

In the short-time Fourier transform (STFT) domain, (1) is given by

```math
\displaystyle Y(f,k)=S(f,k)+N(f,k), \tag{2}
```

where $`Y(f,k)`$, $`S(f,k)`$, and $`N(f,k)`$ denote the complex-valued
STFT coefficients of the noisy, clean speech, and noise signals,
respectively, and $`f`$ and $`k`$ denote the frequency bin and time
frame indices. The objective of SE is to recover the clean speech signal
from its noisy observation. Depending on the modeling paradigm, this
objective can be formulated as a direct regression problem or as a
generative mapping problem between noisy and clean speech. In this work,
we consider representative state-of-the-art models from both paradigms,
which are briefly described below. Their implementation and training
configurations are detailed in Section 4.

### 2.1 Speech enhancement models

_Complex-valued spectrogram-based regression (CR):_  CR follows a
discriminative regression formulation in which a deep neural network
predicts the real and imaginary components of the clean speech STFT from
the corresponding noisy STFT \[23\]. The predicted complex-valued
spectrum is transformed back to the time domain, and the model is
optimized using the mean square error between the reconstructed signal
$`\hat{s}(t)`$ and the clean signal $`s(t)`$.

_Schrödinger Bridge-based model (SB):_  In contrast to direct
regression, SB formulates SE as a generative transport problem learning
a stochastic path between the noisy observation and the corresponding
clean speech representation \[24, 5\]. Unlike conventional
diffusion-based approaches which progressively perturb clean speech
toward a predefined noise distribution \[3\], SB learns this path
directly from the paired noisy and clean representations. The model is
trained with a weighted data-prediction objective defined in both the
time and STFT domains. Further details of the SB formulation are
provided in \[5\].

### 2.2 Training procedure

State-of-the-art SE models are conventionally trained using mini-batches
formed by randomly sampling training examples. We consider a training
set $`\mathcal{D}`$ consisting of $`N`$ pairs of clean-noisy examples,
i.e.,

```math
\mathcal{D}=\{(s_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N},
```

where $`s_{i}`$ denotes the $`i`$-th clean signal and $`y_{i}`$ denotes
the corresponding noisy observation. Standard SE training constructs a
mini-batch by randomly sampling $`B`$ examples from $`\mathcal{D}`$. We
use this random sampling strategy as the baseline for training the CR
and SB models and compare it against the proposed stratified sampling
strategy described in Section 3.

## 3 Proposed method

Conventional random sampling treats all training examples equally,
irrespective of their intrinsic speech characteristics. Our previous
analysis showed that intrinsic properties of clean speech are correlated
with enhancement gains \[22\], suggesting that the distribution of such
characteristics in the training data may influence how effectively an SE
model learns across different types of speech. This motivates us to
investigate whether explicitly controlling the distribution of intrinsic
speech characteristics during training can improve model generalization.
We therefore propose a stratified mini-batch sampling strategy that
balances the representation of a (user-defined) clean speech
characteristic across training batches. The approach is independent of
the specific characteristic used for stratification and modifies only
how training examples are sampled.

### 3.1 Speech characteristics

We extract intrinsic speech characteristics from the clean training
utterances using the openSMILE toolkit \[25\]. Let $`f(s_{i})`$ denote
the value of a selected characteristic for the clean signal $`s_{i}`$.
The characteristic is computed once for each training utterance and is
used solely to determine its sampling group, i.e., it is not provided as
an input to the enhancement model. In our experiments, we consider the
mean third formant frequency $`F_{3}`$ (cf. Section 5.1) and the
standard deviation of the fundamental frequency $`f_{0}`$ (cf.
Section 5.2) as speech characteristics for stratification.

### 3.2 Stratified mini-batch sampling

For a given batch size $`B`$, we propose to choose the number of
quantiles $`J`$ such that $`B`$ is divisible by $`J`$. The empirical
distribution of the selected characteristic $`f(s_{i})`$ over the
training set is divided into $`J`$ quantiles, denoted by $`Q_{1}`$,
$`\ldots`$, $`Q_{J}`$. Each training example is assigned to exactly one
quantile according to its characteristic value. Each mini-batch is
constructed by randomly sampling $`B/J`$ utterances from each quantile.
Sampling is performed without replacement within an epoch, while the
examples are reshuffled between epochs. Thus, each mini-batch contains
an equal representation of utterances spanning the range of the selected
clean speech characteristic, rather than reflecting a potentially
imbalanced distribution.

The quantile boundaries are computed once using the training set and
remain fixed throughout training. The same sampling procedure can be
applied independently to any SE model, allowing the effect of stratified
sampling to be evaluated without modifying the underlying SE
architectures, training objectives, or optimization procedures. The
proposed strategy can be implemented using a custom sampler in standard
deep learning data loading frameworks.

## 4 Experimental settings

### 4.1 Datasets

_Clean speech signals._  To assess the generalizability of our findings
across languages, we use two clean speech datasets, i.e., the English
Wall Street Journal (WSJ0) dataset \[26\] and the Crowdsourced Latin
American Spanish (CROWD) dataset \[27\]. WSJ0 is a commonly used
benchmark for SE \[3, 5\], containing $`28.6`$ hours of speech from
$`120`$ speakers. We use the standard WSJ0 split, with $`101`$, $`10`$,
and $`8`$ speakers assigned to the train, validation, and test sets,
respectively. Since the CROWD dataset is larger, we select subsets of it
with a comparable duration and the same number of speakers as the
different WSJ0 splits.

To assess the impact of the proposed batch sampling strategy across
neurotypical and pathological speaker groups, we additionally use the
PC-GITA dataset \[28\]. PC-GITA contains $`2.8`$ hours of speech from
$`50`$ individuals with Parkinson’s disease and $`50`$ neurotypical
controls. Each speaker produces $`12`$ utterances, comprising $`10`$
sentences, one read text, and one monologue. Given the limited amount of
available speech, we use speaker-independent $`10`$-fold
cross-validation for the PC-GITA dataset \[13, 22\]. In each fold,
$`80\%`$, $`10\%`$, and $`10\%`$ of the speakers are assigned to the
training, validation, and test sets, respectively.

For each dataset, all recordings are downsampled to $`16`$ kHz and the
SE models are trained and evaluated independently using only the
corresponding training, validation, and test data.

_Noisy mixtures._  Noisy mixtures are constructed using four
environmental noise categories from CHiME3 \[29\], i.e., bus, cafe,
pedestrian area, and street. Noise recordings are first downsampled to
$`16`$ kHz before mixture generation. A random segment of a randomly
selected noise category is added to a clean speech signal. The mixture
SNR is sampled uniformly from $`-6`$ dB to $`14`$ dB. This procedure
follows the noise-mixing protocol commonly adopted in previous SE
studies \[23, 5\].

### 4.2 Model architectures and training

Following \[23\], signals are transformed to the STFT domain using a
window size of $`510`$ samples, a hop length of $`128`$ samples, and a
Hann window. The STFT coefficients are then compressed using
$`\alpha=0.5`$ and $`\beta=0.33`$ as in \[5\].

Both the CR and SB models use the NCSN++M U-Net architecture, a lighter
configuration of NCSN++ \[30, 23\]. The CR model removes the
noise-conditioning layers, resulting in $`22.1`$ million trainable
parameters, whereas the SB model retains these layers, resulting in
$`25.2`$ million trainable parameters. In addition, SB uses exponential
moving average with a decay rate of $`0.999`$ \[5\], a
variance-exploding noise schedule with $`\sigma_{\min}=0.7`$ and
$`\sigma_{\max}=1.82`$, and $`50`$-step SDE sampling.

Training is performed using the Adam optimizer, a learning rate of
$`10^{-4}`$, and a maximum of $`1000`$ epochs. Training stops if the
validation loss does not decrease for $`20`$ consecutive epochs. For the
WSJ0 and CROWD dataset, models are trained on an NVIDIA H100 GPU. For
the PC-GITA dataset, models are trained on an RTX 3090 GPU.

wideband perceptual evaluation of speech quality (PESQ)\[32\],
frequency-weighted segmental signal-to-noise ratio (fwSSNR)\[33\], and
scale-invariant signal-to-distortion ratio (SI-SDR)\[34\]. The clean
speech signal is used as the reference signal for all metrics. To
facilitate comparisons across datasets, we report the improvement in
these objective metrics, i.e., $`\Delta`$ESTOI, $`\Delta`$PESQ,
$`\Delta`$fwSSNR, and $`\Delta`$SI-SDR, relative to the noisy mixture.

## 5 Experimental Results

This section evaluates the effectiveness of the proposed stratified
mini-batch sampling strategy and examines the factors that influence its
performance and robustness. We first compare the proposed strategy with
standard random mini-batch sampling across the considered SE models and
speech datasets (Section 5.1). For these main experiments, the mean
value of $`F_{3}`$ is used as the acoustic characteristic for
stratification, motivated by its strong correlation with SE performance
observed in our previous work \[22\]. We then conduct two ablation
studies. First, we investigate whether the choice of acoustic
characteristic affects the benefit of stratification by comparing mean
$`F_{3}`$ with the standard deviation of the fundamental frequency
$`f_{0}`$, which showed little or no correlation with SE performance in
our previous work (Section 5.2). Second, we assess whether the observed
improvements are robust to stochastic variations in mini-batch
composition by comparing random and stratified sampling across multiple
training runs (Section 5.3).

Table 1 compares the performance of the CR and SB models trained using
standard random mini-batch sampling and the proposed stratified
mini-batch sampling using a batch size $`B=8`$ and $`J=4`$ quantiles.
For mini-batch sampling, training utterances are stratified according to
their $`F_{3}`$ value. Models are separately trained and tested on the
English WSJ0, Spanish CROWD, and Spanish PC-GITA datasets. For PC-GITA,
test performance is reported separately for neurotypical and
pathological speakers.

For the WSJ0 dataset, stratified sampling improves the performance of
both the CR and SB models, with the largest gains observed for the CR
model, particularly in E-STOI and fwSSNR. Improvements for the SB model
are more modest across all metrics. For the CROWD dataset, stratified
sampling improves the performance of both the CR and SB models, but the
improvements are more pronounced for the SB model, particularly for
PESQ, fwSSNR, and SI-SDR. For the PC-GITA dataset, stratified sampling
improves the performance of the CR model for both neurotypical and
pathological speakers, with the largest gains observed in E-STOI and
fwSSNR. In contrast, the SB model achieves performance comparable to
random sampling, with only minor differences between the two strategies.
The consistent gains of the CR model across both speaker groups indicate
that the proposed sampling strategy also benefits pathological speech.

Overall, stratified mini-batch sampling maintains or improves
performance across both SE models, all three datasets, and both
neurotypical and pathological speaker populations, with the magnitude of
the gains depending on the dataset, model architecture, and evaluation
metric. The strategy requires virtually no additional computational cost
and leaves the enhancement model, training objective, data, and
optimization procedure unchanged, modifying only how training examples
are selected to form mini-batches. Therefore, it provides a simple and
complementary means of improving SE training and performance without
additional model complexity.

### 5.2 Effect of the acoustic characteristic used for stratification

The mean $`F_{3}`$ was selected as the stratification feature for the
results in Section 5.1 based on its strong correlation with SE
performance reported in our previous work \[22\]. To examine whether the
effectiveness of the proposed sampling strategy depends on this
correlation, we additionally consider the standard deviation of the
fundamental frequency $`f_{0}`$ as the stratification feature, which
exhibits little or no correlation with SE performance \[22\]. As an
exemplary setting for this ablation study, we consider the CR model
trained and evaluated on the English WSJ0 dataset.

Table 2 compares the performance of the CR model on WSJ0 when using mean
$`F_{3}`$ and the standard deviation of $`f_{0}`$ for stratification.
The mean $`F_{3}`$ results are repeated from Section 5.1 to facilitate a
direct comparison. It can be observed that stratification based on mean
$`F_{3}`$ yields considerable improvements over the standard deviation
of $`f_{0}`$ for E-STOI and fwSSNR, whereas PESQ and SI-SDR remain
comparable. This suggests that the effectiveness of stratification is
not determined simply by introducing greater acoustic diversity within a
mini-batch. Instead, the diversity appears to be more beneficial when it
is introduced along a dimension that reflects differences in enhancement
difficulty.

### 5.3 Robustness to stochastic mini-batch sampling

The results in Section 5.1 are obtained from a single training run.
Since stochastic mini-batch sampling can lead to different mini-batch
compositions across training runs, we additionally examine whether the
observed performance differences between random and stratified sampling
are robust to such variations. To this end, we train the CR model on the
English WSJ0 dataset using three independent training runs, with the
same random seeds applied to both sampling strategies. For stratified
sampling, the mean $`F_{3}`$ is used as the stratification feature.

Table 3 reports the resulting performance across the three runs. The
results show that stratified sampling consistently outperforms random
sampling across all four metrics. The relatively small standard
deviations across runs indicate that these improvements are consistent
across different random seeds, suggesting that the benefit of stratified
sampling is robust to stochastic variation in mini-batch composition.

## 6 Conclusion and Outlook

In this paper, we proposed a simple feature-stratified mini-batch
sampling strategy for training SE models. The strategy divides the
training data according to a clean-speech characteristic strongly
correlated with SE performance, i.e., the mean $`F_{3}`$, and enforces
equal representation of its quantiles within each mini-batch. The
proposed strategy improves SE performance across multiple models,
datasets, and evaluation metrics, although the magnitude of the gains
varies across experimental conditions. The proposed method requires no
modification to the enhancement architecture, loss function, or training
procedure, and can be readily incorporated into existing training
pipelines. Future work will explore joint diversification based on
multiple acoustic characteristics, curriculum learning strategies,
effects of different batch sizes and number of quantiles, as well as
subjective evaluation of the resulting enhanced speech.
