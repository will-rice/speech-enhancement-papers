# A variance modeling framework based on variational autoencoders for speech enhancement

This work is supported by the ERC Advanced Grant VHIA \#340113.

###### Abstract

In this paper we address the problem of enhancing speech signals in
noisy mixtures using a source separation approach. We explore the use of
neural networks as an alternative to a popular speech variance model
based on supervised non-negative matrix factorization (NMF). More
precisely, we use a variational autoencoder as a speaker-independent
supervised generative speech model, highlighting the conceptual
similarities that this approach shares with its NMF-based counterpart.
In order to be free of generalization issues regarding the noisy
recording environments, we follow the approach of having a supervised
model only for the target speech signal, the noise model being based on
unsupervised NMF. We develop a Monte Carlo expectation-maximization
algorithm for inferring the latent variables in the variational
autoencoder and estimating the unsupervised model parameters.
Experiments show that the proposed method outperforms a semi-supervised
NMF baseline and a state-of-the-art fully supervised deep learning
approach.

## 1 Introduction

Speech enhancement is a classical problem of speech processing, which
aims to recover a clean speech signal from the recording of a noisy
signal, where the noise is generally considered as additive
\[[1](#bib.bib1)\]. In this work we address single-channel speech
enhancement, which can be seen as a an under-determined source
separation problem, where the sources to be separated are of different
nature.

Statistical approaches combining a local Gaussian modeling of the
time-frequency signal coefficients with a variance model have attracted
a lot of attention in the past few years \[[2](#bib.bib2)\]. Within this
framework, non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) techniques are
especially popular to structure the time-frequency-dependent signal
variance \[[3](#bib.bib3)\]. Recently, deep neural networks were also
successfully investigated for speech enhancement, to generate either
time-frequency masks or clean power spectrograms from noisy spectrograms
\[[4](#bib.bib4)\], or to model the signal variance \[[5](#bib.bib5)\]
(here in a multichannel framework). Similarities between NMF and
standard autoencoders have been reported in \[[6](#bib.bib6)\]. The
recent approach \[[7](#bib.bib7)\] also falls into the variance modeling
framework. Very interestingly, the authors developed a semi-supervised
single-channel speech enhancement method mixing concepts of Bayesian
inference and deep learning, through the use of a variational
autoencoder \[[8](#bib.bib8)\].

In the present work, we follow a similar approach, using a
speaker-independent supervised Gaussian speech model based on a
variational autoencoder. For a given time-frequency point, it mainly
consists in modeling the speech variance as a non-linear function of a
Gaussian latent random vector, by means of a neural network. Compared
with \[[7](#bib.bib7)\], we further introduce a time-frame-dependent
gain in order to provide some robustness with respect to the loudness of
the training examples. The noise model is Gaussian and based on
unsupervised NMF, so as to be free of generalization issues regarding
the noisy recording environments. We propose a new Monte Carlo
expectation-maximization algorithm \[[9](#bib.bib9)\] for performing
semi-supervised speech enhancement. Experimental results show the
superiority of our approach compared with a baseline method using NMF,
and also compared with a state-of-the-art fully supervised deep learning
approach \[[10](#bib.bib10)\].

We first provide in
Section [2](#S2 "2 Variance Modeling Framework ‣ A variance modeling framework based on variational autoencoders for speech enhancement")
a brief presentation of the NMF-based variance modeling framework
commonly used in audio source separation. This will help us emphasizing
the conceptual similarities shared with the speech model used in this
work. In
Section [3](#S3 "3 Model ‣ A variance modeling framework based on variational autoencoders for speech enhancement")
we present the model. Our inference algorithm is detailed in
Section [4](#S4 "4 Inference ‣ A variance modeling framework based on variational autoencoders for speech enhancement").
The optimization problem related to the variational autoencoder training
is presented in
Section [5](#S5 "5 Supervised Training of the Speech Model ‣ A variance modeling framework based on variational autoencoders for speech enhancement").
Experiments are described in
Section [6](#S6 "6 Experiments ‣ A variance modeling framework based on variational autoencoders for speech enhancement")
and we finally conclude in
Section [7](#S7 "7 Conclusion ‣ A variance modeling framework based on variational autoencoders for speech enhancement").

## 2 Variance Modeling Framework

We work in the short-term Fourier transform (STFT) domain. For all
$`(f,n)\in\mathbb{B}={\{0,...,F-1\}}\times{\{0,...,N-1\}}`$, where $`f`$
denotes the frequency index and $`n`$ the time-frame index, the
single-channel speech enhancement problem consists in recovering the
clean speech STFT coefficients $`s_{fn}\in\mathbb{C}`$ from the noisy
observed ones $`x_{fn}=s_{fn}+b_{fn}`$, where $`b_{fn}\in\mathbb{C}`$ is
a noise signal. The source STFT coefficients are modeled as complex
circularly symmetric Gaussian random variables \[[2](#bib.bib2)\]:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
s_{fn}\sim\mathcal{N}_{c}(0,v_{s,fn});\hskip 18.49988ptb_{fn}\sim\mathcal{N}_{c}(0,v_{b,fn}),
``` |  | (1) |

where $`\mathcal{N}_{c}(\mu,\sigma^{2})`$ denotes the complex proper
Gaussian distribution, which is circularly symmetric (rotational
invariant in the complex plane) if $`\mu=0`$. Under a local stationarity
assumption \[[11](#bib.bib11)\], the variances in
([1](#S2.E1 "In 2 Variance Modeling Framework ‣ A variance modeling framework based on variational autoencoders for speech enhancement"))
characterize the short-term power spectral density of the audio source
signals. Note that this Gaussian model has been recently extended to
other complex circularly symmetric and elliptically contoured
distributions \[[12](#bib.bib12)\], such as the symmetric alpha-stable
\[[13](#bib.bib13)\] or the Student’s t \[[14](#bib.bib14)\]
distributions.

A widely used linear variance model for the source signals relies on
NMF. For $`j\in\{s,b\}`$, let
$`\mathbf{W}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{F\times K_{j}}`$ be a matrix
containing $`K_{j}`$ spectral patterns, and let
$`\mathbf{H}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{K_{j}\times N}`$ be a matrix
containing the activations of these spectral patterns over the time
frames. At each time-frequency point $`(f,n)\in\mathbb{B}`$, the
variance $`v_{j,fn}`$ is modeled as follows:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
v_{j,fn}=\left(\mathbf{W}_{j}\mathbf{H}_{j}\right)_{f,n}=\mathbf{w}_{j,f}^{\top}\mathbf{h}_{j,n},
``` |  | (2) |

where $`\mathbf{w}^{\top}_{j,f}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{K_{j}}`$ is $`f`$-th
row of $`\mathbf{W}_{j}`$, $`\mathbf{h}_{j,n}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{K_{j}}`$
is $`n`$-th column of $`\mathbf{H}_{j}`$, and $`\cdot^{\top}`$ denotes
the transposition operator. Without any additional constraint on the
model parameters, the NMF rank $`K_{j}`$ is generally chosen such that
$`K_{j}(F+N)\ll FN`$. Compared with the unconstrained Gaussian model in
([1](#S2.E1 "In 2 Variance Modeling Framework ‣ A variance modeling framework based on variational autoencoders for speech enhancement")),
the number of parameters to be estimated is therefore considerably
reduced, which is of great interest for solving an under-determined
problem.

In a semi-supervised speech enhancement setting \[[15](#bib.bib15)\],
$`\mathbf{W}_{s}`$ is learned on a clean speech database. The variance
model in
([2](#S2.E2 "In 2 Variance Modeling Framework ‣ A variance modeling framework based on variational autoencoders for speech enhancement"))
is therefore seen as a linear function of the activations. Then, in the
speech enhancement algorithm, only the speech activation matrix
$`\mathbf{H}_{s}`$ and the noise NMF parameters $`\mathbf{W}_{b}`$ and
$`\mathbf{H}_{b}`$ are estimated from the observation of the noisy
mixture signal. It has been shown in \[[3](#bib.bib3)\] that maximum
likelihood estimation of the NMF parameters under this Gaussian model
amounts to solving an optimization problem involving the Itakura-Saito
divergence. An optimal estimate of the speech signal in the minimum mean
square error sense is then obtained by Wiener filtering.

## 3 Model

### 3.1 Supervised Speech Model

In a supervised setting, the variance model in
([2](#S2.E2 "In 2 Variance Modeling Framework ‣ A variance modeling framework based on variational autoencoders for speech enhancement"))
is limited by its linear nature. Moreover, the number of trainable
parameter is restricted by the factorization rank, which is usually
chosen to be small. In this work we consider a supervised and non-linear
modeling of the speech variances. Independently for all
$`(f,n)\in\mathbb{B}`$, we have the following generative model,
involving a latent random vector $`\mathbf{z}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{L}`$:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathbf{z}_{n}`$ | $`\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I});`$ |  | (3) |
|  | $`\displaystyle s_{fn}\mid\mathbf{z}_{n};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s}`$ | $`\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}_{c}(0,\sigma_{f}^{2}(\mathbf{z}_{n})),`$ |  | (4) |

where $`\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})`$ denotes the
multivariate Gaussian distribution for a real-valued random vector,
$`\mathbf{I}`$ is the identity matrix of appropriate size, and
$`\sigma_{f}^{2}:\mathbb{R}^{L}\mapsto\mathbb{R}_{+}`$,
$`f\in\{0,...,F-1\}`$, represents a non-linear function parametrized by
$`\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s}`$. The output of this function is provided by
one neuron in the $`F`$-dimensional output layer of a neural network,
whose weights and biases are denoted by $`\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s}`$. In
the context of variational autoencoders, the model
([3](#S3.E3 "In 3.1 Supervised Speech Model ‣ 3 Model ‣ A variance modeling framework based on variational autoencoders for speech enhancement"))-([4](#S3.E4 "In 3.1 Supervised Speech Model ‣ 3 Model ‣ A variance modeling framework based on variational autoencoders for speech enhancement"))
is called the *generative network* or *probabilistic decoder*
\[[8](#bib.bib8)\].

As in the case of an NMF-based supervised variance model, we assume that
the parameters $`\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s}`$ are learned on a clean speech
database. This is done using a *recognition network* or *probabilistic
encoder* in addition to the probabilistic decoder, as will be explained
in
Section [5](#S5 "5 Supervised Training of the Speech Model ‣ A variance modeling framework based on variational autoencoders for speech enhancement").
Only the latent random vectors $`\{\mathbf{z}_{n}\}_{n=0}^{N-1}`$ will
have to be inferred from the observed data. Moreover, the latent
dimension $`L`$ is set much smaller than $`F`$.

### 3.2 Unsupervised Noise Model

As introduced in
Section [2](#S2 "2 Variance Modeling Framework ‣ A variance modeling framework based on variational autoencoders for speech enhancement"),
we use an unsupervised NMF-based Gaussian model for the noise.
Independently for all $`(f,n)\in\mathbb{B}`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
b_{fn};\mathbf{w}_{b,f},\mathbf{h}_{b,n}\sim\mathcal{N}_{c}\left(0,\left(\mathbf{W}_{b}\mathbf{H}_{b}\right)_{f,n}\right).
``` |  | (5) |

### 3.3 Mixture Model

The observed mixture signal is modeled as follows for all
$`(f,n)\in\mathbb{B}`$:

|     |                                   |     |     |
|-----|-----------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       x_{fn}=\sqrt{g_{n}}s_{fn}+b_{fn},
       ```                                |     | (6) |

where $`g_{n}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}`$ represents a frame-dependent but
frequency-independent gain. We introduce this term in order to provide
some robustness with respect to the loudness of the training examples
used to learn the variational autoencoder parameters
$`\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s}`$ (this will be illustrated in
Section [6](#S6 "6 Experiments ‣ A variance modeling framework based on variational autoencoders for speech enhancement")).
The speech and noise signals are further supposed to be mutually
independent given the latent random vectors
$`\{\mathbf{z}_{n}\}_{n=0}^{N-1}`$, such that for all
$`(f,n)\in\mathbb{B}`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
x_{fn}\mid\mathbf{z}_{n};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{u,fn}\sim\mathcal{N}_{c}\left(0,g_{n}\sigma_{f}^{2}(\mathbf{z}_{n})+\left(\mathbf{W}_{b}\mathbf{H}_{b}\right)_{f,n}\right),
``` |  | (7) |

where
$`\boldsymbol{\theta}_{u,fn}=\{\mathbf{w}_{b,f},\mathbf{h}_{b,n},g_{n}\}`$
is the set of unsupervised model parameters at time-frequency point
$`(f,n)`$.

## 4 Inference

Let us introduce the following notations:

- $`\triangleright`$
  $`\mathbf{x}_{n}=\{x_{fn}\}_{f=0}^{F-1}`$: The set of mixture STFT
  coefficients for a given time frame $`n\in\{0,...,N-1\}`$;
- $`\triangleright`$
  $`\mathbf{x}=\{x_{fn}\}_{(f,n)\in\mathbb{B}}`$: The set of all mixture
  STFT coefficients;
- $`\triangleright`$
  $`\mathbf{z}=\{\mathbf{z}_{n}\}_{n=0}^{N-1}`$: The set of all latent
  variables;
- $`\triangleright`$
  $`\boldsymbol{\theta}_{u}\hskip-1.42271pt=\hskip-1.42271pt\{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{u,fn}\}_{(f,n)\in\mathbb{B}}`$:
  The set of unsupervised model parameters.

From the set of observations $`\mathbf{x}`$, our primary goal is to
estimate the unsupervised model parameters $`\boldsymbol{\theta}_{u}`$,
which will serve in the final inference of the speech STFT coefficients.
Unfortunately, straightforward maximum likelihood estimation is here
intractable. A common alternative then consists in exploiting the latent
variable structure of the model to derive an expectation-maximization
(EM) algorithm \[[16](#bib.bib16)\]. From an initialization
$`\boldsymbol{\theta}_{u}^{\star}`$ of the model parameters, it consists
in iterating the two following steps until convergence:

- $`\triangleright`$
  E-Step: Compute
  $`Q(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{u};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{u}^{\star})\hskip-1.42271pt=\hskip-1.42271pt\mathbb{E}_{p(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{u}^{\star})}\hskip-2.56064pt\left[\ln p(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{u})\right]`$;
- $`\triangleright`$
  M-Step: Update
  $`\boldsymbol{\theta}_{u}^{\star}\leftarrow\argmax_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{u}}\,Q(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{u};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{u}^{\star})`$.

### 4.1 E-Step

Due to the non-linear relation between the observations and the latent
variables
([7](#S3.E7 "In 3.3 Mixture Model ‣ 3 Model ‣ A variance modeling framework based on variational autoencoders for speech enhancement")),
we cannot compute the posterior distribution
$`{p(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{u}^{\star})}`$
in an analytical form. Therefore we cannot compute the expectation
involved in the definition of
$`Q(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{u};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{u}^{\star})`$ at the
E-Step. We consequently approximate it by the following empirical
average (Monte Carlo approximation):

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle Q(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{u};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{u}^{\star})`$ | $`\displaystyle\approx\tilde{Q}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{u};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{u}^{\star})`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\overset{c}{=}-\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\sum_{(f,n)\in\mathbb{B}}\bigg[\ln\left(g_{n}\sigma_{f}^{2}\left(\mathbf{z}_{n}^{(r)}\right)+\left(\mathbf{W}_{b}\mathbf{H}_{b}\right)_{f,n}\right)`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt+\frac{|x_{fn}|^{2}}{g_{n}\sigma_{f}^{2}\left(\mathbf{z}_{n}^{(r)}\right)+\left(\mathbf{W}_{b}\mathbf{H}_{b}\right)_{f,n}}\bigg],`$ |  | (8) |

where $`\overset{c}{=}`$ denotes equality up to additive constants with
respect to $`\boldsymbol{\theta}_{u}`$ and
$`\boldsymbol{\theta}_{u}^{\star}`$, and
$`\{\mathbf{z}_{n}^{(r)}\}_{r=1,...,R}`$ is a sequence of samples drawn
from the posterior
$`p(\mathbf{z}_{n}\mid\mathbf{x}_{n};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{u}^{\star})`$
using a Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) method. Here we use the
Metropolis-Hastings algorithm \[[17](#bib.bib17)\]. This approach forms
the basis of the Monte Carlo EM (MCEM) algorithm \[[9](#bib.bib9)\].
Note that unlike the standard EM algorithm, it does not ensure that the
likelihood increases at each iteration. Nevertheless, some convergence
results in terms of stationary point of the likelihood can be obtained
under suitable conditions \[[18](#bib.bib18)\].

At the $`m`$-th iteration of the Metropolis-Hastings algorithm and
independently for all $`n\in\{0,...,N-1\}`$, we first draw a sample
$`\mathbf{z}_{n}`$ from a proposal random walk distribution:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbf{z}_{n}\mid\mathbf{z}_{n}^{(m-1)};\epsilon^{2}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{z}_{n}^{(m-1)},\epsilon^{2}\mathbf{I}).
``` |  | (9) |

Using the fact that this is a symmetric proposal distribution
\[[17](#bib.bib17)\], we then compute the acceptance probability
$`\alpha`$ defined by:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\alpha=`$ | $`\displaystyle\min\left(1,\frac{p\left(\mathbf{x}_{n}\mid\mathbf{z}_{n};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{u}^{\star}\right)p\left(\mathbf{z}_{n}\right)}{p\left(\mathbf{x}_{n}\mid\mathbf{z}_{n}^{(m-1)};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{u}^{\star})\right)p\left(\mathbf{z}_{n}^{(m-1)}\right)}\right),`$ |  | (10) |

where
$`p\left(\mathbf{x}_{n}\mid\mathbf{z}_{n};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{u}^{\star}\right)=\prod_{f=0}^{F-1}p(x_{fn}\mid\mathbf{z}_{n};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{u,fn}^{\star})`$
with
$`p(x_{fn}\mid\mathbf{z}_{n};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{u,fn}^{\star})`$
defined in
([7](#S3.E7 "In 3.3 Mixture Model ‣ 3 Model ‣ A variance modeling framework based on variational autoencoders for speech enhancement"))
and $`p\left(\mathbf{z}_{n}\right)`$ defined in
([3](#S3.E3 "In 3.1 Supervised Speech Model ‣ 3 Model ‣ A variance modeling framework based on variational autoencoders for speech enhancement")).
Then we draw $`u`$ from the uniform distribution $`\mathcal{U}([0,1])`$.
If $`u<\alpha`$, we accept the sample and set
$`\mathbf{z}_{n}^{(m)}=\mathbf{z}_{n}`$, otherwise we reject the sample
and set $`\mathbf{z}_{n}^{(m)}=\mathbf{z}_{n}^{(m-1)}`$. We only keep
the last $`R`$ samples for computing
$`\tilde{Q}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{u};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{u}^{\star})`$
in
([8](#S4.E8 "In 4.1 E-Step ‣ 4 Inference ‣ A variance modeling framework based on variational autoencoders for speech enhancement")),
i.e. we discard the samples drawn during a so called burn-in period.

### 4.2 M-Step

At the M-step of the MCEM algorithm, we want to maximize
$`\tilde{Q}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{u};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{u}^{\star})`$
in
([8](#S4.E8 "In 4.1 E-Step ‣ 4 Inference ‣ A variance modeling framework based on variational autoencoders for speech enhancement"))
with respect to the unsupervised model parameters
$`\boldsymbol{\theta}_{u}`$. As usual in the NMF literature
\[[19](#bib.bib19)\], we adopt a block-coordinate approach by
successively and individually updating $`\mathbf{H}_{b}`$,
$`\mathbf{W}_{b}`$ and $`\mathbf{g}=[g_{0},...,g_{N-1}]^{\top}`$, using
the auxiliary function technique. We will provide derivation details
only for $`\mathbf{H}_{b}`$, as the procedure is very similar for the
other parameters.

Let $`\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{H}_{b})`$ be the opposite of
$`\tilde{Q}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{u};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{u}^{\star})`$
in
([8](#S4.E8 "In 4.1 E-Step ‣ 4 Inference ‣ A variance modeling framework based on variational autoencoders for speech enhancement")),
seen as a function of $`\mathbf{H}_{b}`$ only (other parameters are
fixed). Our goal is to minimize $`\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{H}_{b})`$ under a
non-negativity constraint.

###### Definition 1 (Auxiliary function).

The
$`\mathbb{R}_{+}^{K_{b}\times N}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{K_{b}\times N}\mapsto\mathbb{R}_{+}`$
mapping $`\mathcal{G}(\mathbf{H}_{b},\tilde{\mathbf{H}}_{b})`$ is an
auxiliary function to $`\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{H}_{b})`$ if and only if

|  |  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|----|
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\forall(\mathbf{H}_{b},\tilde{\mathbf{H}}_{b})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{K_{b}\times N}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{K_{b}\times N},`$ | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{H}_{b})\leq\mathcal{G}(\mathbf{H}_{b},\tilde{\mathbf{H}}_{b});`$ |  | (11) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\forall\mathbf{H}_{b}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{K_{b}\times N},`$ | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{H}_{b})=\mathcal{G}(\mathbf{H}_{b},\mathbf{H}_{b}).`$ |  | (12) |

In other words, $`\mathcal{G}(\mathbf{H}_{b},\tilde{\mathbf{H}}_{b})`$
is an upper bound of $`\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{H}_{b})`$ which is tight for
$`\tilde{\mathbf{H}}_{b}=\mathbf{H}_{b}`$. The original minimization
problem can be replaced with an alternate minimization of this upper
bound. From an initial point $`\mathbf{H}_{b}^{\star}`$ we iterate:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbf{H}_{b}^{\star}\leftarrow\argmin\limits_{\mathbf{H}_{b}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{K_{b}\times N}}\,\mathcal{G}(\mathbf{H}_{b},\mathbf{H}_{b}^{\star}).
``` |  | (13) |

This procedure corresponds to the majorize-minimize (MM) algorithm
\[[20](#bib.bib20)\], which by construction leads to a monotonic
decrease of $`\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{H}_{b})`$. Moreover, its convergence
properties are the same as the ones of the EM algorithm
\[[21](#bib.bib21)\].

###### Proposition 1 (Auxiliary function to $`\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{H}_{b})`$).

The function $`\mathcal{G}(\mathbf{H}_{b},\tilde{\mathbf{H}}_{b})`$
defined below is an auxiliary function to
$`\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{H}_{b})`$.

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(\mathbf{H}_{b},\tilde{\mathbf{H}}_{b})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\sum_{(f,n)\in\mathbb{B}}\Bigg[\ln\left(g_{n}\sigma_{f}^{2}\left(\mathbf{z}_{n}^{(r)}\right)+\left(\mathbf{W}_{b}\tilde{\mathbf{H}}_{b}\right)_{f,n}\right)`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle+\frac{\left(\mathbf{W}_{b}\mathbf{H}_{b}\right)_{f,n}-\left(\mathbf{W}_{b}\tilde{\mathbf{H}}_{b}\right)_{f,n}}{g_{n}\sigma_{f}^{2}\left(\mathbf{z}_{n}^{(r)}\right)+\left(\mathbf{W}_{b}\tilde{\mathbf{H}}_{b}\right)_{f,n}}`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle+|x_{fn}|^{2}\left(\frac{g_{n}\sigma_{f}^{2}\left(\mathbf{z}_{n}^{(r)}\right)}{\left(g_{n}\sigma_{f}^{2}\left(\mathbf{z}_{n}^{(r)}\right)+\left(\mathbf{W}_{b}\tilde{\mathbf{H}}_{b}\right)_{f,n}\right)^{2}}\right.`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\left.+\sum_{k=1}^{K_{b}}\frac{w_{b,fk}\tilde{h}_{b,kn}^{2}}{h_{b,kn}\left(g_{n}\sigma_{f}^{2}\left(\mathbf{z}_{n}^{(r)}\right)+\left(\mathbf{W}_{b}\tilde{\mathbf{H}}_{b}\right)_{f,n}\right)^{2}}\right)\Bigg],`$ |  | (14) |

where for all $`(f,n)\in\mathbb{B}`$ and $`k\in\{1,...,K_{b}\}`$,
$`w_{b,fk}=(\mathbf{W}_{b})_{f,k}`$ $`h_{b,kn}=(\mathbf{H}_{b})_{k,n}`$
and $`\tilde{h}_{b,kn}=(\tilde{\mathbf{H}}_{b})_{k,n}`$.

###### Proof.

The proof is provided in \[[22](#bib.bib22)\] due to space limitation. ∎

The auxiliary function
$`\mathcal{G}(\mathbf{H}_{b},\tilde{\mathbf{H}}_{b})`$ is separable in
convex functions of the individual coefficients $`h_{b,kn}`$,
$`k\in\{1,...,K_{b}\}`$, $`n\in\{0,...,N-1\}`$, which is convenient for
updating those parameters. By canceling the partial derivative of
$`\mathcal{G}(\mathbf{H}_{b},\tilde{\mathbf{H}}_{b})`$ with respect to
$`h_{b,kn}`$, we obtain an update which depends on $`\tilde{h}_{b,kn}`$.
According to the MM algorithm, we can use the fact that
$`\tilde{h}_{b,kn}`$ is equal to the previous value of $`h_{b,kn}`$ for
obtaining the following multiplicative update in matrix form:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbf{H}_{b}\leftarrow\mathbf{H}_{b}\odot\left[\frac{\mathbf{W}_{b}^{\top}\left(\mid\mathbf{X}\mid^{\odot 2}\odot\sum\limits_{r=1}^{R}\left(\mathbf{V}_{x}^{(r)}\right)^{\odot-2}\right)}{\mathbf{W}_{b}^{\top}\sum\limits_{r=1}^{R}\left(\mathbf{V}_{x}^{(r)}\right)^{\odot-1}}\right]^{\odot 1/2},
``` |  | (15) |

where $`\odot`$ denotes element-wise multiplication and exponentiation,
matrix division is also element-wise,
$`\mathbf{V}_{x}^{(r)}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{F\times N}`$ is the matrix of
entries
$`\left(\mathbf{V}_{x}^{(r)}\right)_{f,n}=g_{n}\sigma_{f}^{2}\left(\mathbf{z}_{n}^{(r)}\right)+\left(\mathbf{W}_{b}\mathbf{H}_{b}\right)_{f,n}`$,
and $`\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{C}^{F\times N}`$ is the matrix of entries
$`\left(\mathbf{X}\right)_{f,n}=x_{fn}`$. We can straightforwardly apply
the same procedure for maximizing
$`\tilde{Q}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{u};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{u}^{\star})`$
with respect to $`\mathbf{W}_{b}`$. The resulting multiplicative update
rule is given by:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbf{W}_{b}\leftarrow\mathbf{W}_{b}\odot\left[\frac{\left(\mid\mathbf{X}\mid^{\odot 2}\odot\sum\limits_{r=1}^{R}\left(\mathbf{V}_{x}^{(r)}\right)^{\odot-2}\right)\mathbf{H}_{b}^{\top}}{\sum\limits_{r=1}^{R}\left(\mathbf{V}_{x}^{(r)}\right)^{\odot-1}\mathbf{H}_{b}^{\top}}\right]^{\odot 1/2}.
``` |  | (16) |

Again, in a very similar fashion, the vector of speech gains is updated
as follows:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbf{g}^{\top}\leftarrow\mathbf{g}^{\top}\odot\left[\frac{\mathbf{1}^{\top}\left[\mid\mathbf{X}\mid^{\odot 2}\odot\sum\limits_{r=1}^{R}\left(\mathbf{V}_{s}^{(r)}\odot\left(\mathbf{V}_{x}^{(r)}\right)^{\odot-2}\right)\right]}{\mathbf{1}^{\top}\left[\sum\limits_{r=1}^{R}\left(\mathbf{V}_{s}^{(r)}\odot\left(\mathbf{V}_{x}^{(r)}\right)^{\odot-1}\right)\right]}\right]^{\odot 1/2},
``` |  | (17) |

where $`\mathbf{1}`$ is an all-ones column vector of dimension $`F`$ and
$`\mathbf{V}_{s}^{(r)}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{F\times N}`$ is the matrix of
entries
$`\left(\mathbf{V}_{s}^{(r)}\right)_{f,n}=\sigma_{f}^{2}\left(\mathbf{z}_{n}^{(r)}\right)`$.
The non-negativity of $`\mathbf{H}_{b}`$, $`\mathbf{W}_{b}`$ and
$`\mathbf{g}`$ is ensured provided that those parameters are initialized
with non-negative entries. In practice, at the M-step of the MCEM
algorithm, we perform only one iteration of updates
([15](#S4.E15 "In 4.2 M-Step ‣ 4 Inference ‣ A variance modeling framework based on variational autoencoders for speech enhancement")),
([16](#S4.E16 "In 4.2 M-Step ‣ 4 Inference ‣ A variance modeling framework based on variational autoencoders for speech enhancement"))
and
([17](#S4.E17 "In 4.2 M-Step ‣ 4 Inference ‣ A variance modeling framework based on variational autoencoders for speech enhancement")).

### 4.3 Speech reconstruction

In the following
$`\boldsymbol{\theta}_{u}^{\star}=\{\mathbf{W}_{b}^{\star},\mathbf{H}_{b}^{\star},\mathbf{g}^{\star}\}`$
denotes the set of parameters estimated by the above MCEM algorithm. For
all $`(f,n)\in\mathbb{B}`$, let
$`\tilde{s}_{fn}=\sqrt{g_{n}^{\star}}s_{fn}`$ be the scaled version of
the speech STFT coefficients as introduced in
([6](#S3.E6 "In 3.3 Mixture Model ‣ 3 Model ‣ A variance modeling framework based on variational autoencoders for speech enhancement")),
with $`g_{n}^{\star}=(\mathbf{g}^{\star})_{n}`$. Our final goal is to
estimate those coefficients according to their posterior mean:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\hat{\tilde{s}}_{fn}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p(\tilde{s}_{fn}\mid x_{fn};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{u}^{\star})}[\tilde{s}_{fn}]`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p(\mathbf{z}_{n}\mid\mathbf{x}_{n};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{u}^{\star})}\left[\mathbb{E}_{p(\tilde{s}_{fn}\mid\mathbf{z}_{n},\mathbf{x}_{n};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{u}^{\star})}[\tilde{s}_{fn}]\right]`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p(\mathbf{z}_{n}\mid\mathbf{x}_{n};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{u}^{\star})}\left[\frac{g_{n}^{\star}\sigma_{f}^{2}(\mathbf{z}_{n})}{g_{n}^{\star}\sigma_{f}^{2}(\mathbf{z}_{n})+(\mathbf{W}_{b}^{\star}\mathbf{H}_{b}^{\star})_{f,n}}\right]x_{fn}.`$ |  | (18) |

This estimation corresponds to a soft-masking of the mixture signal,
similarly as the standard Wiener filtering. Exactly as before, this
expectation cannot be computed in an analytical form, but we can
approximate it using the same Metropolis-Hastings algorithm as detailed
in
Section [4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 E-Step ‣ 4 Inference ‣ A variance modeling framework based on variational autoencoders for speech enhancement").
Note that this estimation procedure is different than the one proposed
in \[[7](#bib.bib7)\]. Here the STFT source coefficients are estimated
from their true posterior distribution, without conditioning on the
latent variables. The time-domain estimate of the speech signal is
finally obtained by inverse STFT and overlap-add.

## 5 Supervised Training of the Speech Model

For supervised training of the speech model, we use a large dataset of
independent clean-speech STFT time frames
$`\mathbf{s}^{tr}=\{\mathbf{s}_{n}\in\mathbb{C}^{F}\}_{n=0}^{N_{tr}-1}`$.
In our speech enhancement method detailed above, we are only interested
in using the generative model defined in
([3](#S3.E3 "In 3.1 Supervised Speech Model ‣ 3 Model ‣ A variance modeling framework based on variational autoencoders for speech enhancement"))
and
([4](#S3.E4 "In 3.1 Supervised Speech Model ‣ 3 Model ‣ A variance modeling framework based on variational autoencoders for speech enhancement")).
However, in order to learn the parameters $`\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s}`$ of
this generative model, we also need to introduce a *recognition network*
or *probabilistic encoder*
$`q(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{s}^{tr};\boldsymbol{\phi})`$, which is an
approximation of the true posterior
$`p(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{s}^{tr};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s})`$
\[[8](#bib.bib8)\]. Independently for all $`l\in\{0,...,L-1\}`$ and
$`n\in\{0,...,N_{tr}-1\}`$,
$`q(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{s}^{tr};\boldsymbol{\phi})`$ is defined
similarly as in \[[7](#bib.bib7)\] by:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
z_{l,n}\mid\mathbf{s}_{n};\boldsymbol{\phi}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\tilde{\mu}_{l}\left(|\mathbf{s}_{n}|^{\odot 2}\right),\tilde{\sigma}_{l}^{2}\left(|\mathbf{s}_{n}|^{\odot 2}\right)\right),
``` |  | (19) |

where $`z_{l,n}=(\mathbf{z}_{n})_{l}`$ and
$`\tilde{\mu}_{l}:\mathbb{R}_{+}^{F}\mapsto\mathbb{R}`$,
$`\tilde{\sigma}_{l}^{2}:\mathbb{R}_{+}^{F}\mapsto\mathbb{R}_{+}`$ are
non-linear functions parametrized by $`\boldsymbol{\phi}`$. The output
of each function is provided by one neuron in the $`2L`$-dimensional
output layer of a neural network, whose weights and biases are denoted
by $`\boldsymbol{\phi}`$.

As explained in \[[8](#bib.bib8)\], the recognition network parameters
$`\boldsymbol{\phi}`$ and the generative network parameters
$`\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s}`$ can be jointly trained by maximizing the
evidence lower-bound (ELBO) defined by:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s},\boldsymbol{\phi}\right)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q\left(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{s}^{tr};\boldsymbol{\phi}\right)}\left[\ln p\left(\mathbf{s}^{tr}\mid\mathbf{z};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s}\right)\right]`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\hskip 42.67912pt-D_{KL}\left(q\left(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{s}^{tr};\boldsymbol{\phi}\right)\parallel p(\mathbf{z})\right),`$ |  | (20) |

where $`D_{KL}(q\parallel p)=\mathbb{E}_{q}[\ln(q/p)]`$ is the
Kullback-Leibler divergence. The first term in the right-hand side of
([20](#S5.E20 "In 5 Supervised Training of the Speech Model ‣ A variance modeling framework based on variational autoencoders for speech enhancement"))
is a reconstruction accuracy term, while the second one is a
regularization term. From
([3](#S3.E3 "In 3.1 Supervised Speech Model ‣ 3 Model ‣ A variance modeling framework based on variational autoencoders for speech enhancement")),
([4](#S3.E4 "In 3.1 Supervised Speech Model ‣ 3 Model ‣ A variance modeling framework based on variational autoencoders for speech enhancement"))
and
([19](#S5.E19 "In 5 Supervised Training of the Speech Model ‣ A variance modeling framework based on variational autoencoders for speech enhancement")),
the ELBO can be developed as follows:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s},\boldsymbol{\phi}\right)\overset{c}{=}`$ | $`\displaystyle-\sum_{f=0}^{F-1}\sum_{n=0}^{N_{tr}-1}\mathbb{E}_{q\left(\mathbf{z}_{n}\mid\mathbf{s}_{n};\boldsymbol{\phi}\right)}\left[d_{IS}\left(\left|s_{fn}\right|^{2};\sigma_{f}^{2}(\mathbf{z}_{n})\right)\right]`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\hskip-45.52458pt+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{l=0}^{L-1}\sum_{n=0}^{N_{tr}-1}\left[\ln\tilde{\sigma}_{l}^{2}\left(\left|\mathbf{s}_{n}\right|^{\odot 2}\right)-\tilde{\mu}_{l}\left(\left|\mathbf{s}_{n}\right|^{\odot 2}\right)^{2}-\tilde{\sigma}_{l}^{2}\left(\left|\mathbf{s}_{n}\right|^{\odot 2}\right)\right],`$ |  | (21) |

where $`d_{IS}(x;y)=x/y-\ln(x/y)-1`$ is the Itakura-Saito (IS)
divergence. We note that maximizing the ELBO with respect to the
parameters $`\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s}`$ of the generative speech model
amounts to minimizing a cost function that involves the IS divergence
between the observed power spectrogram $`|s_{fn}|^{2}`$ and the variance
model $`\sigma_{f}^{2}(\mathbf{z}_{n})`$. It is interesting to highlight
the similarity with the Gaussian generative speech model
([1](#S2.E1 "In 2 Variance Modeling Framework ‣ A variance modeling framework based on variational autoencoders for speech enhancement"))-([2](#S2.E2 "In 2 Variance Modeling Framework ‣ A variance modeling framework based on variational autoencoders for speech enhancement")),
where maximum likelihood estimation of its parameters also amounts to
minimizing the IS divergence between $`|s_{fn}|^{2}`$ and the NMF-based
variance parametrization $`(\mathbf{W}_{s}\mathbf{H}_{s})_{f,n}`$, as
proved in \[[3](#bib.bib3)\].

Finally, as the expectation in
([21](#S5.E21 "In 5 Supervised Training of the Speech Model ‣ A variance modeling framework based on variational autoencoders for speech enhancement"))
cannot be computed in an analytical form, we again use a Monte Carlo
approximation:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{E}_{q\left(\mathbf{z}_{n}\mid\mathbf{s}_{n};\boldsymbol{\phi}\right)}\left[d_{IS}\left(\left|s_{fn}\right|^{2};\sigma_{f}^{2}(\mathbf{z}_{n})\right)\right]\approx\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}d_{IS}\left(\left|s_{fn}\right|^{2};\sigma_{f}^{2}\left(\mathbf{z}_{n}^{(r)}\right)\right),
``` |  | (22) |

where for all $`r\in\{1,...,R\}`$, $`\mathbf{z}_{n}^{(r)}`$ is
independently drawn from
$`q\left(\mathbf{z}_{n}\mid\mathbf{s}_{n};\boldsymbol{\phi}\right)`$ in
([19](#S5.E19 "In 5 Supervised Training of the Speech Model ‣ A variance modeling framework based on variational autoencoders for speech enhancement")),
using the so-called *reparametrization trick* \[[8](#bib.bib8)\].
Independently for all $`l\in\{0,...,L-1\}`$, it consists in sampling:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
z_{l,n}^{(r)}\mid\mathbf{s}_{n};\boldsymbol{\phi}\sim\tilde{\mu}_{l}\left(|\mathbf{s}_{n}|^{\odot 2}\right)+\tilde{\sigma}_{l}\left(|\mathbf{s}_{n}|^{\odot 2}\right)\mathcal{N}(0,1).
``` |  | (23) |

Injecting
([22](#S5.E22 "In 5 Supervised Training of the Speech Model ‣ A variance modeling framework based on variational autoencoders for speech enhancement"))
in
([21](#S5.E21 "In 5 Supervised Training of the Speech Model ‣ A variance modeling framework based on variational autoencoders for speech enhancement")),
we obtain an objective function which is differentiable with respect to
both $`\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s}`$ and $`\boldsymbol{\phi}`$, and which
can be optimized using gradient-ascent-based algorithms.

## 6 Experiments

Fig. 1: Structure of our variational autoencoder. All layers are fully
connected. Next to each layer we indicate its size along with the
activation function that is used.

Database: The supervised speech model parameters are learned from the
training set of the TIMIT database \[[23](#bib.bib23)\]. It contains
almost 4 hours of speech signals at a 16-kHz sampling rate, distributed
over 462 speakers. For the evaluation of the speech enhancement
algorithm, we mixed clean speech signals from the test set of the TIMIT
database and noise signals from the DEMAND database
\[[24](#bib.bib24)\], with various noisy environments: domestic, nature,
office, indoor public spaces, street and transportation. We created 168
mixtures at a 0 dB signal-to-noise ratio (one mixture per speaker in the
TIMIT test set). Note that both speakers and sentences are different
than in the training set.

Baseline methods: Considering that both approaches belong to the
variance modeling framework, we first compare our method with a
semi-supervised NMF baseline as described in
Section [2](#S2 "2 Variance Modeling Framework ‣ A variance modeling framework based on variational autoencoders for speech enhancement").
The speech NMF dictionary is learned using the training set of the TIMIT
database, and only the speech activation matrix and the noise NMF model
parameters are estimated from the noisy mixture signals. The speech
signal is then recovered by Wiener filtering. We also compare our
approach with the fully supervised deep learning method proposed in
\[[10](#bib.bib10)\]. In this work, a deep neural network is trained to
map noisy speech log-power spectrograms to clean speech log-power
spectrograms. The authors showed that their mapping-based approach
outperformed other deep learning techniques that rely on the estimation
of time-frequency masks. Moreover, the authors used more than 100
different noise types to train their system, which showed to be quite
effective in handling unseen noise types. It is therefore a very
relevant method to compare with. We used the Python code provided by the
authors.^(†)^(†) https://github.com/yongxuUSTC/sednn

Parameter settings: The STFT is computed using a 64-ms sine window
(i.e. $`F=513`$) with 75%-overlap. We compare the performance of our
method with the semi-supervised NMF baseline according to the dimension
of the latent space $`L`$ and the rank $`K_{s}`$ of the NMF speech
model. For fair comparison, we set $`L=K_{s}\in\{8,16,32,64,128\}`$. The
rank of the noise model is arbitrarily fixed to $`K_{b}=10`$.
Unsupervised NMF parameters are randomly initialized. For the proposed
method, the gain vector $`\mathbf{g}`$ is initialized with an all-ones
vector. The iterative algorithms of those two methods are stopped if the
improvement in terms of objective function is smaller that $`10^{-4}`$
(for our method we monitored
$`\tilde{Q}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{u};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{u}^{\star})`$).
The parameters of our MCEM algorithm are the following ones: At each
E-Step (see
Section [4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 E-Step ‣ 4 Inference ‣ A variance modeling framework based on variational autoencoders for speech enhancement")),
we run 40 iterations of the Metropolis-Hastings algorithm using
$`\epsilon^{2}=0.01`$ for the proposal distribution, and we discard the
first 30 samples as the burn-in period. At the first iteration of the
MCEM algorithm we need to initialize the Markov chain of the
Metropolis-Hastings algorithm. For that purpose we use the recognition
network considering the mixture signal as input because the clean speech
signal is not observed: for all $`l\in\{0,...,L-1\}`$,
$`z_{l,n}^{(0)}=\tilde{\mu}_{l}\left(|\mathbf{x}_{n}|^{\odot 2}\right)`$.
Then, at each new E-Step, we use the last sample drawn at the previous
E-Step to initialize the Markov chain. For estimating the speech STFT
coefficients (see
Section [4.3](#S4.SS3 "4.3 Speech reconstruction ‣ 4 Inference ‣ A variance modeling framework based on variational autoencoders for speech enhancement")),
we run the Metropolis-Hastings algorithm for 100 iterations and discard
the first 75 samples.

Variational autoencoder: The structure of our variational autoencoder is
represented in
Fig. [1](#S6.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 6 Experiments ‣ A variance modeling framework based on variational autoencoders for speech enhancement").
As in \[[8](#bib.bib8)\], we use hyperbolic tangent ($`\tanh(\cdot)`$)
activation functions except for the encoder/decoder output layers, which
use identity activation functions ($`I_{d}(\cdot)`$). The values of
these output layers thus lie in $`\mathbb{R}`$, which is the reason why
we output logarithm of variances. For learning the parameters
$`\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s}`$ and $`\boldsymbol{\phi}`$ (see
Section [5](#S5 "5 Supervised Training of the Speech Model ‣ A variance modeling framework based on variational autoencoders for speech enhancement")),
we used the Adam optimizer \[[25](#bib.bib25)\] with a step size of
$`10^{-3}`$, exponential decay rates for the first and second moment
estimates of $`0.9`$ and $`0.999`$ respectively, and an epsilon of
$`10^{-7}`$ for preventing division by zero. 20% of the TIMIT training
set was kept as a validation set, and early stopping with a patience of
10 epochs was used. Weights were initialized using the uniform
initializer described in \[[26](#bib.bib26)\]. For reproducibility, a
Python implementation of our algorithm is available online.^(†)^(†)
https://github.com/sleglaive/MLSP-2018

Results: We evaluate the enhanced speech signal in terms of
signal-to-distortion ratio (SDR) \[[27](#bib.bib27)\] and perceptual
evaluation of speech quality (PESQ) measure \[[28](#bib.bib28)\]. The
SDR is expressed in decibels (dB) and the PESQ score lies between
$`-0.5`$ and $`4.5`$. We computed the SDR using the mir_eval Python
library.^(†)^(†) https://github.com/craffel/mir_eval We first illustrate
the interest of having a time-frame dependent gain $`g_{n}`$ as
introduced in
([6](#S3.E6 "In 3.3 Mixture Model ‣ 3 Model ‣ A variance modeling framework based on variational autoencoders for speech enhancement")).
Inevitably, the variational autoencoder is trained using examples with a
limited loudness range. If we force $`g_{n}=1`$ for all
$`n\in\{0,...,N-1\}`$, we can therefore expect the speech enhancement
quality to depend on the speech power in the observed noisy mixture
signal. This problem is illustrated in
Fig. [2](#S6.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 6 Experiments ‣ A variance modeling framework based on variational autoencoders for speech enhancement"):
The SDR indicated by black dots suddenly drops for a scaling factor of
the mixture signal greater than 12 dB. It indicates that the power of
the underlying clean speech signal in this mixture went out of the power
range observed during the training. One solution to this problem could
be to perform some kind of data augmentation as in \[[7](#bib.bib7)\].
Here we exploited the statistical modeling framework, by introducing
parameters in order to handle some inherent drawbacks of learning-based
approaches. The gray diamonds in
Fig. [2](#S6.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 6 Experiments ‣ A variance modeling framework based on variational autoencoders for speech enhancement")
indeed show that the time-frame-dependent gains provide a solution to
this robustness issue.

The speech enhancement results are presented in
Fig. [3](#S6.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 6 Experiments ‣ A variance modeling framework based on variational autoencoders for speech enhancement").
We first observe that while the NMF baseline requires a sufficiently
large rank $`K_{s}=64`$, our method obtains quite stable results for a
latent dimension $`L`$ greater than or equal to $`16`$. Moreover it
always performs better than the NMF baseline in terms of both SDR and
PESQ measures. We also see that it outperforms the fully supervised deep
learning approach \[[10](#bib.bib10)\]. It is indeed difficult for such
kind of approaches to generalize to unseen noises, which strongly
justifies the interest of semi-supervised approaches. Audio examples
illustrating those results are available online.^(†)^(†)
https://sleglaive.github.io/demo-mlsp18.html

We conclude this section with some information about the computational
time for enhancing a 2.6 seconds-long mixture, using a central
processing unit at 2.5 GHz. One iteration of the proposed MCEM algorithm
(with $`L=64`$) takes around 2.9 seconds. For the semi-supervised NMF
baseline (with $`K_{s}=64`$), one iteration of update rules takes around
4 milliseconds. The fully supervised deep-learning approach, which is
non iterative, takes around 7 seconds.

## 7 Conclusion

In this paper we showed that within the variance modeling framework,
variational autoencoders are a suitable alternative to supervised NMF.
We proposed a semi-supervised speech enhancement method based on an MCEM
algorithm. Experimental results demonstrated that it outperforms both a
semi-supervised NMF baseline and a fully supervised deep learning
approach. We used a relatively shallow architecture for the variational
autoencoder, we can therefore expect even better results by going
deeper. Future works include using variational autoencoders for
separating similar sources, e.g. multiple speakers. This is a much
harder problem and we may have to exploit spatial information provided
by multichannel mixtures to address it. Another interesting perspective
would be to introduce a temporal model for the latent variables of the
variational autoencoder.
````
