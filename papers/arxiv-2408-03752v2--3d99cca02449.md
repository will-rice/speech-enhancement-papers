###### Abstract

A one-shot algorithm called iterationless DANSE (iDANSE) is introduced
to perform distributed adaptive node-specific signal estimation (DANSE)
in a fully connected wireless acoustic sensor network (WASN) deployed in
an environment with non-overlapping latent signal subspaces. The iDANSE
algorithm matches the performance of a centralized algorithm in a single
processing cycle while devices exchange fused versions of their
multichannel local microphone signals. Key advantages of iDANSE over
currently available solutions are its iterationless nature, which favors
deployment in real-time applications, and the fact that devices can
exchange fewer fused signals than the number of latent sources in the
environment. The proposed method is validated in numerical simulations
including a speech enhancement scenario.

## 1 Introduction

The recent increase in acoustic sensor availability enables the creation
of wireless acoustic sensor networks (WASNs), which can surpass the
individual device performance and be more flexible than centralized
systems \[1\]. Their use relies on distributed algorithms with low
latency and low bandwidth usage. Most existing solutions focus on the
estimation of parameter vectors \[2, 3\] or a single globally defined
quantity \[4\], even though many applications require estimation of
node-specific target signals \[5\]. The exploration of this problem has
led to the distributed adaptive node-specific signal estimation (DANSE)
algorithm \[6\] and variants thereof \[7, 8, 9\]. If the target sound
sources are all observable by all nodes, their latent subspaces fully
overlap and DANSE reaches the performance of an equivalent centralized
algorithm. Remarkably, DANSE reaches optimality while nodes only
exchange dimensionally reduced (so-called fused) versions of their local
sensor signals.

In general, however, the target signals of different nodes may span
different latent subspaces which may not mutually overlap. For example,
signals recorded in an airport or a train station may exhibit
non-overlapping latent subspaces if some sound sources produce signals
that are of interest to node $`k`$ but that cannot be captured by node
$`q`$. This may be the case although $`k`$ and $`q`$ can exchange data
and may simultaneously aim at estimating a signal stemming from a shared
fully overlapping latent subspace, e.g., a public address (PA) signal.

This scenario has been addressed in \[9\], where it was concluded that
each node should broadcast as many fused signals as the number of latent
sources observed by the entire WASN for DANSE to remain optimal. Even
with such increased communication bandwidth, DANSE still requires
several iterations to converge, a clear limiting factor for applications
requiring real-time processing \[8\]. To address both of these
shortcomings, we present the iterationless DANSE algorithm (iDANSE),
which matches the centralized signal estimation performance in a single
processing cycle (i.e., in one shot) even if non-overlapping latent
subspaces are present. Unlike in \[9\], nodes within iDANSE do not need
to exchange more fused signals than the dimension of the fully
overlapping latent subspace shared by all nodes.

## 2 Problem Statement

### 2.1 Signal model

Consider a WASN with $`K`$ nodes and $`M_{k}`$ sensors at node $`k`$,
where $`k\in\mathcal{K}\coloneqq\{1,\dots,K\}`$ and
$`M\coloneqq\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}M_{k}`$. The WASN is deployed in a
scene composed of $`S`$ desired sources and $`N`$ noise sources, all
mutually uncorrelated. A source is said to be “global” if its signal is
captured by all nodes (e.g., a PA system) or “local” if its signal is
only captured by one node (e.g., a nearby conversation partner). The
proposed algorithm can be extended to include cases where a latent
signal is captured by more than one but not all nodes, although this is
not exposed here. Among the $`S`$ desired sources,
$`\accentset{\circ}{S}`$ are global, grouped in the set
$`\accentset{\circ}{\mathcal{S}}`$. The other
$`\dot{S}\coloneqq S-\accentset{\circ}{S}`$ sources are local, grouped
in $`\dot{\mathcal{S}}`$. This notation (“circle $`\circ`$” or “dot
$`\cdot`$”) is adopted for clarity as the dot represents a single node
and the circle the whole WASN. For any $`k\in\mathcal{K}`$, there exist
$`\dot{S}_{k}`$ local desired sources, grouped in
$`\dot{\mathcal{S}}_{k}`$, that contribute to its local sensor signals.
Analogous notation is adopted for the noise, replacing $`S`$ and
$`\mathcal{S}`$ by $`N`$ and $`\mathcal{N}`$, respectively. This results
in the signal model for the $`M_{k}`$ local sensor signals
$`\mathbf{y}_{k}`$ of node $`k`$:

```math
\mathbf{y}_{k}\coloneqq\mathbf{s}_{k}+\mathbf{n}_{k}\coloneqq\accentset{\circ}{\mathbf{s}}_{k}+\dot{\mathbf{s}}_{k}+\accentset{\circ}{\mathbf{n}}_{k}+\dot{\mathbf{n}}_{k}~\in\mathbb{C}^{M_{k}}, \tag{1}
```

where
$`\mathbf{s}_{k}\coloneqq\accentset{\circ}{\mathbf{s}}_{k}+\dot{\mathbf{s}}_{k}`$
is the desired signal component with
$`\accentset{\circ}{\mathbf{s}}_{k}`$ and $`\dot{\mathbf{s}}_{k}`$ the
global and local contributions, respectively, and analogous notation is
used for the noise
$`\mathbf{n}_{k}\coloneqq\accentset{\circ}{\mathbf{n}}_{k}+\dot{\mathbf{n}}_{k}`$.
Note that sensor noise is included in $`\dot{\mathbf{n}}_{k}`$. Signals
are assumed complex-valued to allow frequency-domain representations.

![](arxiv-2408-03752v2--3d99cca02449.figures/figure-1.webp)

Fig. 1: Scenario with 4 nodes, 1 global and 2 local desired sources
(red), 2 global and 3 local noise sources (grey).

All local sensor signals can be stacked into a centralized
$`M`$-dimensional vector
$`\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{s}+\mathbf{n}=\accentset{\circ}{\mathbf{s}}+\dot{\mathbf{s}}+\accentset{\circ}{\mathbf{n}}+\dot{\mathbf{n}}`$,
where
$`\mathbf{y}\coloneqq[{\mathbf{y}}^{\mathrm{T}}_{1}\dots{\mathbf{y}}^{\mathrm{T}}_{K}]^{\mathrm{T}}`$
with $`\cdot^{\mathrm{T}}`$ the transpose operator, and the other
vectors are analogously defined from (1). The relevant terms in this
centralized model can be further expanded as:

```math
\mathbf{y}=\accentset{\circ}{\mathbf{A}}\accentset{\circ}{\mathbf{s}}^{\mathrm{lat}}+\dot{\mathbf{A}}\dot{\mathbf{s}}^{\mathrm{lat}}+\accentset{\circ}{\mathbf{n}}+\dot{\mathbf{n}}\in\mathbb{C}^{M}, \tag{2}
```

where
$`\accentset{\circ}{\mathbf{A}}\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times\accentset{\circ}{S}}`$
and $`\dot{\mathbf{A}}\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times\dot{S}}`$ are steering
matrices between the $`M`$ sensors and the sources in
$`\accentset{\circ}{\mathcal{S}}`$ producing the latent signals
$`\accentset{\circ}{\mathbf{s}}^{\mathrm{lat}}`$ and those in
$`\dot{\mathcal{S}}`$ producing $`\dot{\mathbf{s}}^{\mathrm{lat}}`$,
respectively. An example is depicted in Fig.1, where the same notation
is adopted for noise latent signals
$`\accentset{\circ}{\mathbf{n}}^{\mathrm{lat}}`$ (global) and
$`\dot{\mathbf{n}}^{\mathrm{lat}}`$ (local). The matrix
$`\dot{\mathbf{A}}`$ has a sparse block structure since any node $`k`$
only captures the signals of the local sources in
$`\dot{\mathcal{S}}_{k}`$. Let the signals in
$`\accentset{\circ}{\mathbf{s}}^{\mathrm{lat}}`$ be ordered from the
latent signals generated by the sources in $`\dot{\mathcal{S}}_{1}`$
down to $`\dot{\mathcal{S}}_{K}`$. Then $`\dot{\mathbf{A}}`$ can be
written as:

```math
\dot{\mathbf{A}}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}\dot{\mathbf{A}}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\dot{\mathbf{A}}_{K}\end{bmatrix}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}\dot{\mathbf{A}}_{1}^{\prime}&\dots&\mathbf{0}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\mathbf{0}&\dots&\dot{\mathbf{A}}_{K}^{\prime}\end{bmatrix}, \tag{3}
```

where
$`\dot{\mathbf{A}}_{k}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{C}^{M_{k}\times\dot{S}_{k}}`$
is the non-zero part of
$`\dot{\mathbf{A}}_{k}\in\mathbb{C}^{M_{k}\times\dot{S}}`$.

### 2.2 Centralized solution

Consider the centralized case where each node $`k\in\mathcal{K}`$ aims
at estimating a $`J`$-channel target signal
$`{\mathbf{d}}_{k}\coloneqq{{\mathbf{E}}}_{{k}{k}}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{s}_{k}`$,
where $`{{\mathbf{E}}}_{{k}{k}}`$ is an $`M_{k}\times J`$ selection
matrix. The value of $`J`$ is assumed equal for all nodes without loss
of generality, although it may in princple differ between nodes. Node
$`k`$ solves the linear minimum mean square error (LMMSE) problem:

```math
\mathbf{W}_{k}\coloneqq\underset{\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times J}}{\mathrm{arg\,min}}\>\mathbb{E}\{{\|{\mathbf{d}}_{k}-\mathbf{W}^{\mathrm{H}}\mathbf{y}\|^{2}_{2}}\},\>\forall\>k\in\mathcal{K}, \tag{4}
```

where $`\mathbb{E}\{{\cdot}\}`$ denotes the expectation operator,
$`\|\cdot\|_{2}`$ the Euclidean norm, and $`\cdot^{\mathrm{H}}`$ the
Hermitian transpose. The solution to this centralized signal estimation
problem is the multichannel Wiener filter (MWF):

```math
\mathbf{W}_{k}=\left(\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{yy}}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{yd}_{k}},\>\forall\>k\in\mathcal{K}, \tag{5}
```

with the spatial covariance matrices (SCMs)
$`\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{yy}}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\{{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}^{\mathrm{H}}}\}`$
and
$`\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{yd}_{k}}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\{{\mathbf{y}{\mathbf{d}}^{\mathrm{H}}_{k}}\}`$.
The target signal is then estimated as
$`{\hat{\mathbf{d}}}_{k}\coloneqq\mathbf{W}_{k}^{\mathrm{H}}\mathbf{y}`$.
Note that, since the desired and noise sources are uncorrelated,
$`\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{yd}_{k}}\coloneqq\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{ss}}\mathbf{E}_{k}`$
where
$`\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{ss}}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\{{\mathbf{s}\mathbf{s}^{\mathrm{H}}}\}`$
and $`\mathbf{E}_{k}`$ is an $`M\times J`$ matrix extracting
$`{\mathbf{d}}_{k}`$ from $`\mathbf{s}`$.

Note that $`\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{yy}}`$ can be directly estimated from
the microphone signals via averaging techniques as described in,
e.g., \[6, Sec.V-A\]. In the case of speech as desired signal, a voice
activity detector (VAD) can be obtained using various methods \[3, 10,
11\] and used to compute $`\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{ss}}`$ as
$`\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{yy}}-\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{nn}}`$.

### 2.3 Review of the DANSE algorithm

The DANSE algorithm \[6\] is an iterative distributed algorithm that can
be used to solve the estimation problem (4) in a fully connected WASN.
At DANSE iteration $`i`$, node $`k`$ defines a so-called fusion matrix
$`\mathbf{P}_{k}^{i}\in\mathbb{C}^{M_{k}\times J}`$ to compute the fused
signal
$`{\mathbf{z}}^{i}_{k}\coloneqq\mathbf{P}_{k}^{i\mathrm{H}}\mathbf{y}_{k}`$.
Each node broadcasts its fused signal to all other nodes. Node $`k`$
then has access to:

```math
{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}}^{i}_{k}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}{\mathbf{y}}^{\mathrm{T}}_{k}&{\mathbf{z}}^{i\mathrm{T}}_{-k}\end{bmatrix}^{\mathrm{T}}\in\mathbb{C}^{\widetilde{M}_{k}}, \tag{6}
```

where
$`{\mathbf{z}}^{i}_{-k}\coloneqq[{\mathbf{z}}^{i\mathrm{T}}_{1}\>\dots\>{\mathbf{z}}^{i\mathrm{T}}_{k-1}\>{\mathbf{z}}^{i\mathrm{T}}_{k+1}\>\dots\>{\mathbf{z}}^{i\mathrm{T}}_{K}]^{\mathrm{T}}`$
and $`\widetilde{M}_{k}\coloneqq M_{k}+J(K-1)`$. In practice, iterations
are spread over time, with a different frame of signal
$`{\mathbf{y}}_{k}`$ at each $`i`$. Any updating node $`k`$ solves the
following LMMSE problem:

```math
\begin{split}{\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}}^{i+1}_{k}&\coloneqq\underset{\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{C}^{\widetilde{M}_{k}\times J}}{\mathrm{arg\,min}}\>\mathbb{E}\{{\|{\mathbf{d}}_{k}-\mathbf{W}^{\mathrm{H}}{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}}^{i}_{k}\|^{2}_{2}}\}\\
&=({\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}}_{\mathbf{y}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}}^{i})^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{R}}_{\mathbf{y}_{k}{\mathbf{d}}_{k}}^{i},\end{split} \tag{7}
```

where
$`{\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}}_{\mathbf{y}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}}^{i}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\{{{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}}^{i}_{k}{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}}^{i\mathrm{H}}_{k}}\}`$
and
$`\tilde{\mathbf{R}}_{\mathbf{y}_{k}{\mathbf{d}}_{k}}^{i}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\{{{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}}^{i}_{k}{\mathbf{d}}^{\mathrm{H}}_{k}}\}`$.
These SCMs must be estimated using, e.g., the techniques mentioned
in Section 2.2. The target signal estimate is then obtained as
$`{\hat{\mathbf{d}}}^{i+1}_{k}\coloneqq{\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}}^{i+1,\mathrm{H}}_{k}{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}}^{i}_{k}`$.
Nodes may use (7) to update sequentially \[6\], simultaneously, or
asynchronously \[12\]. The filters
$`{\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}}^{i+1}_{k}`$ can be partitioned as
$`[{\mathbf{W}}^{i+1,\mathrm{T}}_{{k}{k}}\>|\>{\mathbf{G}}_{{k},-{k}}^{i+1,\mathrm{T}}]^{\mathrm{T}}`$,
where $`{\mathbf{W}}^{i+1}_{{k}{k}}\in\mathbb{C}^{M_{k}\times J}`$ is
applied to $`\mathbf{y}_{k}`$. An iteration is completed by setting
$`\mathbf{P}_{k}^{i+1}={\mathbf{W}}^{i+1}_{{k}{k}}`$.

### 2.4 Discussion on DANSE optimality

The DANSE algorithm reaches optimality when $`{\mathbf{d}}_{k}`$ spans
the entire latent subspace defined by the sources that contribute to it.
A too low $`J`$ leads to an ill-posed problem \[6, Sec.II-B\]. For (2),
supposing $`J`$ equal for all nodes, this implies:

```math
J\geq\accentset{\circ}{S}+\underset{k\in\mathcal{K}}{\mathrm{max}}\>\dot{S}_{k}, \tag{8}
```

since the desired latent subspace may contain contributions from both
global and local sources.

The condition (8) is necessary but not sufficient to ensure convergence.
In fact, extensive simulations have shown that DANSE does not reach the
centralized solution when local desired sources and global noise sources
are present, i.e., when $`\dot{S}_{k}>0`$ for at least one node $`k`$
and $`\accentset{\circ}{N}>0`$, even if (8) holds. This is stated as an
observation since a formal convergence proof in those conditions is not
available.

## 3 The iDANSE Algorithm

Iterations may be avoided for distributed signal estimation while
exchanging fused signals between nodes. Section 3.1 provides an
intuition for this fact through a mathematical proof for a simple signal
model. Extending this reasoning to (2), it can be shown that iDANSE
(defined in Section 3.2) is optimal in one-shot, though the full proof
is ommitted.

### 3.1 One-shot estimation: an example

Suppose $`\accentset{\circ}{S}{\geq}1`$, $`\accentset{\circ}{N}{=}0`$,
$`\dot{S}{=}0`$, and $`J{=}\accentset{\circ}{S}`$, i.e., (2) reduces to
$`\mathbf{y}=\accentset{\circ}{\mathbf{A}}\accentset{\circ}{\mathbf{s}}^{\mathrm{lat}}+\dot{\mathbf{n}}`$.
Since $`\accentset{\circ}{\mathbf{s}}^{\mathrm{lat}}`$ and
$`\dot{\mathbf{n}}`$ are uncorrelated,
$`\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{yd}_{k}}=\accentset{\circ}{\mathbf{A}}\accentset{\circ}{\mathbf{R}}_{\mathbf{ss}}^{\mathrm{lat}}\accentset{\circ}{\mathbf{A}}^{\mathrm{H}}\mathbf{E}_{k}`$
where
$`\accentset{\circ}{\mathbf{R}}_{\mathbf{ss}}^{\mathrm{lat}}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\{{\accentset{\circ}{\mathbf{s}}^{\mathrm{lat}}\accentset{\circ}{\mathbf{s}}^{\mathrm{lat},\mathrm{H}}}\}`$.
Furthermore, $`\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{yy}}`$ can be separated as
$`\accentset{\circ}{\mathbf{A}}\accentset{\circ}{\mathbf{R}}_{\mathbf{ss}}^{\mathrm{lat}}\accentset{\circ}{\mathbf{A}}^{\mathrm{H}}+\dot{\mathbf{R}}_{\mathbf{nn}}`$
where
$`\dot{\mathbf{R}}_{\mathbf{nn}}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\{{\dot{\mathbf{n}}\dot{\mathbf{n}}^{\mathrm{H}}}\}`$
is block-diagonal. Making use of the matrix inversion lemma, we can
re-write (5) as (details omitted):

```math
\displaystyle\mathbf{W}_{k} \displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\accentset{\circ}{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{D}_{k}, \tag{9}
```

```math
\displaystyle\text{with}\>\>\>\mathbf{D}_{k} \displaystyle\coloneqq\left(\mathbf{I}_{J}-\mathbf{X}^{-1}\accentset{\circ}{\mathbf{A}}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\accentset{\circ}{\mathbf{A}}\right)\accentset{\circ}{\mathbf{R}}_{\mathbf{ss}}^{\mathrm{lat}}\accentset{\circ}{\mathbf{A}}^{\mathrm{H}}\mathbf{E}_{k}, \tag{10}
```

```math
\displaystyle\mathrm{and}\>\>\>\mathbf{X} \displaystyle\coloneqq(\accentset{\circ}{\mathbf{R}}_{\mathbf{ss}}^{\mathrm{lat}})^{-1}+\accentset{\circ}{\mathbf{A}}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\accentset{\circ}{\mathbf{A}}\in\mathbb{C}^{J\times J}, \tag{11}
```

where
$`\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\coloneqq(\dot{\mathbf{R}}_{\mathbf{nn}})^{-1}`$
and $`\mathbf{D}_{k}\in\mathbb{C}^{J\times J}`$ is thus a full-rank
matrix. The centralized MWF can be partitioned as
$`\mathbf{W}_{k}=:[\mathbf{W}_{k1}^{\mathrm{T}}\>\dots\>\mathbf{W}_{kK}^{\mathrm{T}}]^{\mathrm{T}}`$
where $`\mathbf{W}_{kq}`$ is applied to $`\mathbf{y}_{q}`$. Since
$`\boldsymbol{\Gamma}`$ is block-diagonal with blocks
$`\{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{K}\}`$, it holds
that:

```math
\mathbf{W}_{kq}=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{q}\accentset{\circ}{\mathbf{A}}_{q}\mathbf{D}_{k},\>\forall\>(k,q)\in\mathcal{K}^{2}, \tag{12}
```

with
$`\accentset{\circ}{\mathbf{A}}=:[\accentset{\circ}{\mathbf{A}}_{1}^{\mathrm{T}}\>\dots\>\accentset{\circ}{\mathbf{A}}_{K}^{\mathrm{T}}]^{\mathrm{T}}`$
similarly to (3). Now consider the local MWF at node $`q`$ aiming at
estimating $`{\mathbf{d}}_{q}`$ from $`\mathbf{y}_{q}`$, i.e.,
$`\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{q}\coloneqq(\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{y}_{q}\mathbf{y}_{q}})^{-1}\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{y}_{q}\mathbf{d}_{q}}`$
where
$`\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{y}_{q}\mathbf{d}_{q}}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\{{\mathbf{y}_{q}\mathbf{d}_{q}^{\mathrm{H}}}\}`$.
As for (9), it can be shown that (details omitted):

```math
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{q}=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{q}\accentset{\circ}{\mathbf{A}}_{q}\mathbf{D}_{{q}{q}}, \tag{13}
```

with $`\mathbf{D}_{{q}{q}}\in\mathbb{C}^{J\times J}`$ a full-rank
matrix. From (12) and (13), it can be seen that the local MWF at any
node $`q\in\mathcal{K}`$ is in the same column space as the
corresponding part of $`\mathbf{W}_{k}`$. The local estimate
$`{\mathbf{z}}_{q}=\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{q}^{\mathrm{H}}\mathbf{y}_{q}`$
can then be broadcasted to all other nodes, such that node $`k\neq q`$
has access to:

```math
{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}}_{k}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}{\mathbf{y}}^{\mathrm{T}}_{k}&{\mathbf{z}}^{\mathrm{T}}_{-k}\end{bmatrix}^{\mathrm{T}}, \tag{14}
```

where
$`{\mathbf{z}}_{-k}\coloneqq[{\mathbf{z}}^{\mathrm{T}}_{1}\>\dots\>{\mathbf{z}}^{\mathrm{T}}_{k-1}\>{\mathbf{z}}^{\mathrm{T}}_{k+1}\>\dots\>{\mathbf{z}}^{\mathrm{T}}_{K}]^{\mathrm{T}}`$.
Node $`k`$ can compute an MWF to estimate $`{\mathbf{d}}_{k}`$ from
$`{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}}_{k}`$ as:

```math
{\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}}_{k}\coloneqq({\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}}_{\mathbf{y}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}})^{-1}{\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}}_{\mathbf{s}_{k}\mathbf{s}_{k}}\widetilde{\mathbf{E}}_{k}\in\mathbb{C}^{\widetilde{M}_{k}\times J}, \tag{15}
```

where
$`{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}}_{k}=:{\widetilde{\mathbf{s}}}_{k}+{\widetilde{\mathbf{n}}}_{k}`$,
$`{\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}}_{\mathbf{y}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}}:=\mathbb{E}\{{{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}}_{k}{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}}^{\mathrm{H}}_{k}}\}`$,
$`{\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}}_{\mathbf{y}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}}:=\mathbb{E}\{{{\widetilde{\mathbf{s}}}_{k}{\widetilde{\mathbf{s}}}^{\mathrm{H}}_{k}}\}`$,
and $`\widetilde{\mathbf{E}}_{k}`$ is a selection matrix. We can
partition $`{\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}}_{k}`$ as
$`[\mathbf{W}_{kk}^{\mathrm{T}}\>|\>\mathbf{G}_{k,-k}^{\mathrm{T}}]^{\mathrm{T}}`$
where $`{\mathbf{W}}_{{k}{k}}\in\mathbb{C}^{M_{k}\times J}`$ and
$`{\mathbf{G}}_{{k},-{k}}\in\mathbb{C}^{J(K-1)\times J}`$ contains
$`{\mathbf{G}}_{{k}{q}}\in\mathbb{C}^{J\times J}`$ matrices,
$`\forall\>q\in\mathcal{K}\backslash\{k\}`$. The network-wide
parametrization of (15) is then:

```math
\mathbf{W}_{k}^{\mathrm{NW}}=[(\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{1}\mathbf{G}_{k1})^{\mathrm{T}}\>\dots\>\mathbf{W}_{kk}^{\mathrm{T}}\>\dots\>(\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{K}\mathbf{G}_{kK})^{\mathrm{T}}]^{\mathrm{T}}, \tag{16}
```

where it can be verified that
$`{\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}}^{\mathrm{H}}_{k}{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}}_{k}=\mathbf{W}_{k}^{\mathrm{NW},\mathrm{H}}\mathbf{y}`$.
Comparing (12) and (13), it can be seen that the centralized filters
$`\mathbf{W}_{k}`$ are included in the solution space of (16) and are
obtained with
$`\mathbf{G}_{kq}=\mathbf{D}_{{q}{q}}^{-1}\mathbf{D}_{k},\>\forall\>(k,q)\in\mathcal{K}^{2}`$.
No iterations are needed to reach optimality since
$`\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{k}`$ is independent of
$`{\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}}_{k}`$.

### 3.2 Algorithm definition

We now consider the complete signal model of (2), where
$`\accentset{\circ}{N}`$ and $`\dot{S}`$ may be non-zero. The iDANSE
algorithm can be used to perform optimal one-shot distributed signal
estimation in that case. In iDANSE, each node $`k`$ transmits a
$`J`$-dimensional fused signal $`{\mathbf{z}}_{k}`$ which is an estimate
of the global component in their local sensor signals. Each node then
uses all the local estimates it receives from other nodes along with its
own local sensor signals to reach the centralized solution.

1:  Define
$`{\mathbf{g}}_{k}\coloneqq{{\mathbf{E}}}_{{k}{k}}^{\mathrm{T}}\accentset{\circ}{\mathbf{y}}_{k}\coloneqq{{\mathbf{E}}}_{{k}{k}}^{\mathrm{T}}(\accentset{\circ}{\mathbf{s}}_{k}+\accentset{\circ}{\mathbf{n}}_{k})\in\mathbb{C}^{J}`$.

2:  Compute the solution to:

```math
{\accentset{\circ}{\mathbf{W}}}_{k}\coloneqq\underset{\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{C}^{M_{k}\times J}}{\mathrm{arg\,min}}\>\mathbb{E}\{{\|{\mathbf{g}}_{k}-\mathbf{W}^{\mathrm{H}}\mathbf{y}_{k}\|^{2}_{2}}\}. \tag{17}
```

3:  Send
$`{\mathbf{z}}_{k}\coloneqq{\accentset{\circ}{\mathbf{W}}}^{\mathrm{H}}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}`$
to all other nodes.

4:  Receive $`\{{\mathbf{z}}_{q}\}_{q\in\mathcal{K}\backslash\{k\}}`$
and build $`{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}}_{k}`$ as (14).

5:  Compute $`{\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}}_{k}`$ via (15).

6:  Obtain desired signal estimate as
$`{\hat{\mathbf{d}}}_{k}\coloneqq{\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}}^{\mathrm{H}}_{k}{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}}_{k}`$.

The solution to (17) is the MWF
$`{\accentset{\circ}{\mathbf{W}}}_{k}=({\mathbf{R}}_{\mathbf{y}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}})^{-1}\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{y}_{k}{\mathbf{g}}_{k}}`$
where
$`{\mathbf{R}}_{\mathbf{y}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\{{\mathbf{y}_{k}\mathbf{y}^{\mathrm{H}}_{k}}\}`$
and
$`\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{y}_{k}{\mathbf{g}}_{k}}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\{{\mathbf{y}_{k}{\mathbf{g}}_{k}^{\mathrm{H}}}\}`$.
Since all sources are mutually uncorrelated,
$`\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{y}_{k}{\mathbf{g}}_{k}}\coloneqq\accentset{\circ}{\mathbf{R}}_{\mathbf{y}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}}{{\mathbf{E}}}_{{k}{k}}`$
with
$`\accentset{\circ}{\mathbf{R}}_{\mathbf{y}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\{{\accentset{\circ}{\mathbf{y}}_{k}\accentset{\circ}{\mathbf{y}}^{\mathrm{H}}_{k}}\}`$.
The SCMs $`{\mathbf{R}}_{\mathbf{y}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}}`$ and
$`\accentset{\circ}{\mathbf{R}}_{\mathbf{y}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}}`$ must be
estimated from the data, which can be achieved using a VAD
distinguishing between the contributions of local and global
sources \[3, 10, 11\]. Note that this VAD estimation problem may be less
straightforward to solve than the traditional case encountered in
DANSE \[6\].

### 3.3 iDANSE optimality and comparison with DANSE

In iDANSE, the fused signals $`{\mathbf{z}}_{k}`$ must be of
sufficiently high dimension to span the latent subspace formed by the
global source signals $`\accentset{\circ}{\mathbf{s}}^{\mathrm{lat}}`$
and $`\accentset{\circ}{\mathbf{n}}^{\mathrm{lat}}`$, i.e.,
$`J\geq\accentset{\circ}{S}+\accentset{\circ}{N}`$. In that case, iDANSE
is optimal in the general case (2) as formulated in Theorem 1 (proof is
an extension of Section 3.1, omitted due to space constraints). Note
that this differs from \[9\], which states that $`J`$ must be at least
equal to the dimension of the latent desired subspace spanned by the
entire WASN.

#### Theorem 1.

Let
$`{\mathbf{d}}_{k}=\accentset{\circ}{\mathbf{A}}_{k}\accentset{\circ}{\mathbf{s}}^{\mathrm{lat}}+\dot{\mathbf{A}}_{k}\dot{\mathbf{s}}^{\mathrm{lat}}`$
for all $`k\in\mathcal{K}`$ with
$`\accentset{\circ}{\mathbf{s}}^{\mathrm{lat}}`$ and
$`\dot{\mathbf{s}}^{\mathrm{lat}}`$ complex
$`\accentset{\circ}{S}`$-channel and $`\dot{S}`$-channel signals,
respectively, $`\accentset{\circ}{\mathbf{A}}_{k}`$ a
$`J\times\accentset{\circ}{S}`$ matrix of rank $`\accentset{\circ}{S}`$,
and $`\dot{\mathbf{A}}_{k}`$ a $`J\times\dot{S}`$ matrix of rank
$`\dot{S}_{k}<\dot{S}`$. Let there be $`\accentset{\circ}{N}`$ global
noise sources in the environment. If
$`J\geq\accentset{\circ}{S}+\accentset{\circ}{N}`$, then the iDANSE
algorithm reaches the centralized LMMSE solution (5) for any $`k`$.

If $`\dot{S}{=}0`$, both DANSE and iDANSE reach the centralized
solution. A trade-off then appears between convergence speed and
bandwidth usage, as DANSE requires $`\accentset{\circ}{S}`$ fused
signals but multiple iterations to converge, while iDANSE reaches the
optimum in a single cycle but requires
$`\accentset{\circ}{S}{+}\accentset{\circ}{N}`$ fused signals.

## 4 Numerical results

### 4.1 Convergence assessment

In order to assess the convergence of iDANSE, a general case where
latent signal samples and steering matrix entries are drawn from
standard normal distributions is first considered. A fully connected
WASN with $`K{=}10`$ nodes and $`M_{k}{=}8,\>\forall\>k`$ is deployed in
a static scene composed of $`\accentset{\circ}{S}{=}1`$ global desired
source, $`\dot{S}_{k}{=}5,\>\forall\>k`$ local desired sources,
$`\accentset{\circ}{N}{=}1`$ global noise source, and
$`\dot{N}_{k}{=}3,\>\forall\>k`$ local noise sources. All localized
sources are given the same power to obtain a signal-to-noise ratio (SNR)
of 0 dB on average over all nodes. Sensor noise is scaled to 10% of the
first sensor signal power at all nodes. The performance of (i) iDANSE is
compared to that of (ii) centralized processing, (iii) DANSE with
sequential node-updating using
$`J=\accentset{\circ}{S}+\mathrm{max}_{k}\dot{S}_{k}`$ or (iv)
$`J=\accentset{\circ}{S}`$ and (v) processing using only the $`M_{k}`$
local sensor signals. The mean square error (MSE) defined as
$`\mathrm{MSE}_{d}[l]=\frac{1}{KJB}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\sum_{n=lB}^{(l+1)B-1}\|\hat{\mathbf{d}}_{k}[n]-\mathbf{d}_{k}[n]\|_{2}^{2}`$
is computed between the true desired signals and the estimates obtained
via each of the mentioned algorithms. This is first done once using the
true SCMs computed from the steering matrices themselves. To simulate
online-mode processing, frame-based SCM estimation via exponential
averaging is employed by frame size $`B=250`$ samples and forgetting
factor 0.995. The value of $`J`$ is indicated as $`x`$ in legend entries
of the type (i)DANSE*(x). The results in Fig.2 show that iDANSE reaches
the centralized performance instantly with true SCMs and very quickly in
online-mode while DANSE is unable to reach the optimum, even with
$`J=\accentset{\circ}{S}+\mathrm{max}*{k}\dot{S}\_{k}`$.

Fig. 2: MSE\_(d) as function of time frame index (iteration index for
DANSE). True SCMs: dashed lines. Online SCMs estimates: solid lines.

### 4.2 Speech enhancement scenario

Consider a scenario where a fully connected WASN with $`K{=}3`$ nodes
$`M_{k}{=}6,\>\forall\>k`$ is deployed in a static scene with a
reverberation time of 0.15 s. The node-specific target signals include a
speech signal produced by a single PA system
($`\accentset{\circ}{S}{=}1`$) and may (scenario “G”:
$`\dot{S}_{k}{=}1,\>\forall\>k`$) or may not (scenario “G+L”:
$`\dot{S}_{k}{=}0,\>\forall\>k`$) include a local desired speech source.
Speech signals consist of LibriSpeech \[13\] snippets. The scene
includes $`\accentset{\circ}{N}{=}2`$ global and
$`\dot{N}_{k}{=}1,\>\forall\>k`$ local babble noise sources. Processing
is done over 10 s of signal using the weighted overlap-add (WOLA)
framework \[14, 15\] with 1024 samples per frame, 50% frame-overlap, and
a forgetting factor of 0.995. The performance is evaluated in terms of
extended short-term objective intelligibility (eSTOI) \[16\] and SNR of
the first desired signal estimate channel on average over all nodes, for
algorithms (i), (ii), (iii), and (v) – as defined in Section 4.1. The
results in Fig.3 show that iDANSE outperforms DANSE in both “G” and
“G+L” scenarios. Indeed, even when $`\dot{S}_{k}{=}0,\>\forall\>k`$, a
better performance is achieved by iDANSE due to its faster convergence.

## 5 Conclusions

In this paper, the iDANSE algorithm has been introduced for
iterationless LMMSE estimation of node-specific signals in a fully
connected WASN where latent desired subspaces may be non-overlapping.
Future work will address the generalization of iDANSE to partially
overlapping latent subspaces and a topology-independent iDANSE
formulation.
