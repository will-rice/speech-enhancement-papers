# On the Importance of Super-Gaussian Speech Priors for Machine-Learning Based Speech Enhancement

Robert Rehr    Timo Gerkmann ^(†)^(†)thanks: R. Rehr and T. Gerkmann are
with the Computer Science Department, Signal Processing (SP),
Universität Hamburg, Germany (e-mail: robert.rehr@uni-hamburg.de;
timo.gerkmann@uni-hamburg.de)

###### Abstract

For enhancing noisy signals, machine-learning based single-channel
speech enhancement schemes exploit prior knowledge about typical speech
spectral structures. To ensure a good generalization and to meet
requirements in terms of computational complexity and memory
consumption, certain methods restrict themselves to learning speech
spectral envelopes. We refer to these approaches as machine-learning
spectral envelope-based approaches.

In this paper we show by means of theoretical and experimental analyses
that for machine-learning spectral envelope-based approaches,
super-Gaussian priors allow for a reduction of noise between speech
spectral harmonics which is not achievable using Gaussian estimators
such as the Wiener filter. For the evaluation, we use a deep neural
network-based phoneme classifier and a low-rank nonnegative matrix
factorization framework as examples of machine-learning spectral
envelope-based approaches. A listening experiment and instrumental
measures confirm that while super-Gaussian priors yield only moderate
improvements for classic enhancement schemes, for machine-learning
spectral envelope-based approaches super-Gaussian priors clearly make an
important difference and significantly outperform Gaussian priors.

###### Index Terms: 

Super-Gaussian PDF, nonnegative matrix factorization, neural networks,
speech enhancement.

## I Introduction

In the presence of background noise, speech may be corrupted such that
the perceived quality and possibly also the intelligibility are
deteriorated. Similarly, also human-machine interaction by means of
automatic speech recognition systems may suffer from additional
background noises. Hence, the enhancement of corrupted speech signals is
an important task for many applications, e.g., in telecommunications,
for speech recognition, and for hearing aids. In this paper, we consider
single-channel methods that either assume that the noisy speech signal
has been captured by a single microphone or process the output of a
beamformer.

Single-channel speech enhancement has been a topic of research for
decades and has given rise to many different approaches,
e.g., \[[1](#bib.bib1), [2](#bib.bib2), [3](#bib.bib3), [4](#bib.bib4),
[5](#bib.bib5), [6](#bib.bib6)\]. Many approaches are formulated in the
short-time Fourier transform domain where a multiplicative gain function
is applied to the complex spectra to suppress the bands which mainly
contain noise. A common approach is to estimate the clean speech
coefficients blindly from the noisy observation. For this, many
different approaches have been proposed in the literature,
e.g., \[[3](#bib.bib3), [4](#bib.bib4), [7](#bib.bib7), [8](#bib.bib8),
[9](#bib.bib9), [1](#bib.bib1), [10](#bib.bib10), [11](#bib.bib11)\].
These methods often require an estimate of the speech power spectral
density and the noise power spectral density which are also estimated
blindly from the noisy observation, e.g., using \[[12](#bib.bib12),
[3](#bib.bib3), [5](#bib.bib5), [6](#bib.bib6)\]. These methods
generally track the speech and noise power spectral densitys over time,
i.e., a time-varying estimate is returned. Special attention has been
turned to super-Gaussian clean speech estimators \[[7](#bib.bib7),
[8](#bib.bib8), [9](#bib.bib9), [1](#bib.bib1), [10](#bib.bib10),
[2](#bib.bib2)\] as studies indicate that the complex Fourier
coefficients are rather super-Gaussian than Gaussian
distributed \[[13](#bib.bib13), [14](#bib.bib14)\].

Another approach to estimate the clean speech power spectral density and
possibly also the noise power spectral density is to employ
machine-learning based methods, where the structure of speech and noise
is learned before the processing takes place. In this paper, a specific
type of machine-learning-based algorithm is considered, where the
learned speech models only represent the spectral envelope,
e.g., \[[15](#bib.bib15), [16](#bib.bib16), [17](#bib.bib17),
[18](#bib.bib18), [19](#bib.bib19), [20](#bib.bib20),
[21](#bib.bib21)\]. This means that harmonic structures caused by the
vibrating vocal cords are not included. This increases the
generalizability and also reduces the computational complexity and the
amount of data required for training. This type of enhancement is
referred to as machine-learning spectral envelope-based in this paper.
Contrarily, to distinguish this type of enhancement schemes from the
classic estimation schemes considered above, we refer to the latter as
non-machine-learning spectral envelope-based enhancement schemes. While
machine-learning spectral envelope approaches exploit prior knowledge
about typical speech spectral structures, the envelope representation
also limits the quality of the enhanced signal. Due to the coarse
representation of speech, residual noise may remain especially between
spectral harmonics. To reduce the undesired residual noise between
harmonics, different solutions have been proposed.
In \[[19](#bib.bib19)\], a harmonic model has been used to attenuate the
remaining noise component between harmonics. Contrarily, an estimate of
the speech presence probability is employed in \[[20](#bib.bib20),
[21](#bib.bib21)\] to attain a suppression of the residual noise.

In this paper, we show that if super-Gaussian clean speech estimators
are used, postprocessing as in \[[19](#bib.bib19), [20](#bib.bib20),
[21](#bib.bib21)\] is not necessary. For this, we consider the
parameterized clean speech estimator proposed in \[[1](#bib.bib1)\]. An
analysis of this estimator shows that, under a super-Gaussian speech
model, the background noise can be reduced even if the speech power
spectral density is overestimated, e.g., between spectral harmonics when
modeling only the envelope. Furthermore, the estimator
in \[[1](#bib.bib1)\] is employed in two machine-learning spectral
envelope-based enhancement schemes. Both methods serve as examples and
can be considered as variants of previously proposed methods in the
literature. The first one is a deep neural network-based scheme similar
to \[[20](#bib.bib20)\] which is chosen due to its similarities to other
machine-learning spectral envelope-based enhancement methods,
e.g., \[[15](#bib.bib15), [16](#bib.bib16), [17](#bib.bib17),
[18](#bib.bib18), [19](#bib.bib19), [21](#bib.bib21)\]. To demonstrate
the effectiveness of super-Gaussian estimators also for other
machine-learning spectral envelope-based enhancement methods, the
estimator in \[[1](#bib.bib1)\] is additionally embedded in a
supervised, sparse nonnegative matrix factorization enhancement scheme
based on \[[22](#bib.bib22), [23](#bib.bib23)\]. Here, a low amount of
basis vectors is employed such that mainly spectral envelopes are
represented by the nonnegative matrix factorization basis vectors. We
show that for the used non-machine-learning spectral envelope-based
enhancement scheme, which is capable of estimating the spectral fine
structure of speech, the super-Gaussian speech model yields only small
improvements. However, for the considered machine-learning spectral
envelope-based enhancement schemes, where only a speech envelope model
is employed, the super-Gaussian model has a very beneficial effect as it
allows to remove disturbing residual noises. Besides the
machine-learning spectral envelope approaches addressed here, also
machine-learning spectral envelope approaches with LogMax (also known as
MixMax) mixing models benefit from this effect \[[24](#bib.bib24)\].
Super-Gaussian speech models have also been previously employed in
machine-learning based speech enhancement algorithms,
e.g., \[[25](#bib.bib25), [26](#bib.bib26), [27](#bib.bib27)\]. However,
none of the papers provides an explicit analysis of the obtained
improvements over Gaussian estimators in terms of the gain functions
that result under super-Gaussian speech models. Furthermore, the
advantages of these estimators in combination with spectral speech
envelope models have not been highlighted.

After that, we describe the considered machine-learning spectral

and

In

and
Section [VI](#S6 "VI Instrumental Evaluation ‣ On the Importance of Super-Gaussian Speech Priors for Machine-Learning Based Speech Enhancement"),

## II Signal Model and Speech Estimators

In this section, we revisit the clean speech
estimator \[[1](#bib.bib1)\]. This estimator is parameterized such that
various known estimators, e.g., \[[3](#bib.bib3), [4](#bib.bib4),
[7](#bib.bib7), [8](#bib.bib8), [10](#bib.bib10)\], result as special
cases. In particular, it allows to incorporate super-Gaussian speech
models and the estimation of compressed amplitudes. As
in \[[28](#bib.bib28)\], we use the name (M)MSE estimation with
(o)ptimizable (s)peech (m)odel and (i)nhomogeneous (e)rror criterion for
the estimator in \[[1](#bib.bib1)\].

In this paper, we employ input signals with a sampling rate of 16 kHz.
As (M)MSE estimation with (o)ptimizable (s)peech (m)odel and
(i)nhomogeneous (e)rror criterion operates in the short-time Fourier
transform-domain, the sampled noisy input signal is split into
overlapping segments and each segment is transformed to the Fourier
domain after an analysis window has been applied. The segment length of
the short-time Fourier transform is set to 32 ms and a segment overlap
of 50 % is employed. This yields the noisy spectra $`{Y}_{{k,\ell}}`$,
where $`k`$ denotes the frequency index and $`\ell`$ the segment index.
The physically plausible additive corruption model is used where the
noisy coefficients $`{Y}_{{k,\ell}}`$ are described as

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       {Y}_{{k,\ell}}={S}_{{k,\ell}}+{N}_{{k,\ell}}.
       ```                                            |     | (1) |

Here, $`{S}_{{k,\ell}}`$ and $`{N}_{{k,\ell}}`$ represent the clean
speech and noise spectral coefficients, respectively. The estimate of
the clean speech spectral coefficients $`\hat{{S}}_{{k,\ell}}`$ is
obtained from the noisy observation $`{Y}_{{k,\ell}}`$
using \[[1](#bib.bib1)\]. Afterwards, the estimated clean speech
spectra $`\hat{{S}}_{{k,\ell}}`$ are transformed back to the time-domain
and a synthesis window is applied to the obtained time-domain segments.
For the analysis and the synthesis a square-root Hann window is used.
Finally, an overlap-add method is used to reconstruct the complete
time-domain signal. The short-time Fourier transform framework is shared
among all enhancement schemes including the deep neural network-based
scheme in
Section [III](#S3 "III DNN-Based Speech Enhancement Scheme ‣ On the Importance of Super-Gaussian Speech Priors for Machine-Learning Based Speech Enhancement")
and the nonnegative matrix factorization-based scheme in
Section [IV](#S4 "IV NMF-Based Speech Enhancement Scheme ‣ On the Importance of Super-Gaussian Speech Priors for Machine-Learning Based Speech Enhancement").

(M)MSE estimation with (o)ptimizable (s)peech (m)odel and
(i)nhomogeneous (e)rror criterion \[[1](#bib.bib1)\] is a statistically
optimal estimator in the sense of the mean-squared error. Such
estimators consider the quantities
in ([1](#S2.E1 "In II Signal Model and Speech Estimators ‣ On the Importance of Super-Gaussian Speech Priors for Machine-Learning Based Speech Enhancement"))
as random variables, where the involved probability density functions
are assumed to be known. In \[[1](#bib.bib1)\], the estimate
$`\hat{{S}}_{{k,\ell}}`$ that minimizes the mean-squared error given by
$`\mathbb{E}\{(|{S}_{{k,\ell}}|^{\beta}-|\hat{{S}}_{{k,\ell}}|^{\beta})^{2}\}`$
has been derived. Here, $`\mathbb{E}\{\cdot\}`$ denotes the expectation
operator and $`|\cdot|^{\beta}`$ allows to incorporate perceptually
motivated compression functions. Here, $`\beta`$ denotes the compression
factor. In general, the mean-squared error optimal estimator of
$`{S}_{{k,\ell}}`$ depends on the probability density functions of the
speech spectral coefficients $`{S}_{{k,\ell}}`$ and the noise spectral
coefficients $`{N}_{{k,\ell}}`$.

In \[[1](#bib.bib1)\], the complex noise coefficients $`{N}_{{k,\ell}}`$
are assumed to follow a circular-symmetric complex Gaussian
distribution. This assumption is often motivated by the Fourier sum and
the central limit theorem \[[14](#bib.bib14)\]. However, due to the
strong correlation of speech in the time-domain, a Gaussian distribution
does not appropriately describe the speech spectral coefficients
$`{S}_{{k,\ell}}`$ \[[13](#bib.bib13), [14](#bib.bib14),
[9](#bib.bib9)\]. Accordingly, a parametrizable circular-symmetric
possibly heavy-tailed super-Gaussian distribution is employed to
describe $`{S}_{{k,\ell}}`$ in \[[1](#bib.bib1)\]. Given the mixing
model
in ([1](#S2.E1 "In II Signal Model and Speech Estimators ‣ On the Importance of Super-Gaussian Speech Priors for Machine-Learning Based Speech Enhancement"))
and the statistical assumptions about the noise and speech coefficients,
the estimate of the amplitude $`\hat{A}_{{k,\ell}}`$ is given
by \[[1](#bib.bib1)\]

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\hat{A}_{{k,\ell}}=\sqrt{\frac{\Lambda^{n}_{k,\ell}\xi_{{k,\ell}}}{\xi_{{k,\ell}}+\mu}}{\left[\frac{\Gamma(\mu+\beta/2)}{\Gamma(\mu)}\frac{\mathcal{M}(\mu+\beta/2,1;\zeta_{{k,\ell}})}{\mathcal{M}(\mu,1;\zeta_{{k,\ell}})}\right]}^{\frac{1}{\beta}}.
``` |  | (2) |

Here, $`\xi_{{k,\ell}}=\Lambda^{s}_{k,\ell}/\Lambda^{n}_{k,\ell}`$
denotes the *a priori* signal-to-noise ratio. The quantities
$`\Lambda^{s}_{k,\ell}=\mathbb{E}\{|{S}_{{k,\ell}}|^{2}\}`$ and
$`\Lambda^{n}_{k,\ell}=\mathbb{E}\{|{N}_{{k,\ell}}|^{2}\}`$ are the
speech power spectral density and the noise power spectral density,
respectively. Further, $`\zeta_{{k,\ell}}`$ is given by
$`\gamma_{{k,\ell}}\xi_{{k,\ell}}/(\mu+\xi_{{k,\ell}})`$ where
$`\gamma_{{k,\ell}}=|{Y}_{{k,\ell}}|^{2}/\Lambda^{n}_{k,\ell}`$ is the
*a posteriori* signal-to-noise ratio. The symbol
$`\mathcal{M}(\cdot,\cdot;\cdot)`$ represents the confluent
hypergeometric function. The parameter $`\mu>0`$ determines the shape of
speech prior probability density function where $`\mu<1`$ corresponds to
a super-Gaussian distribution while $`\mu=1`$ corresponds to a Gaussian
distribution. To obtain an estimate of the complex speech coefficients
$`\hat{{S}}_{{k,\ell}}`$, the estimated amplitude
in ([2](#S2.E2 "In II Signal Model and Speech Estimators ‣ On the Importance of Super-Gaussian Speech Priors for Machine-Learning Based Speech Enhancement"))
is combined with the noisy phase $`\Phi^{y}_{{k,\ell}}`$ as
$`\hat{{S}}_{{k,\ell}}=\hat{A}_{{k,\ell}}\exp(j\Phi^{y}_{{k,\ell}})`$,
where $`j=\sqrt{-1}`$.

It is interesting to note that (M)MSE estimation with (o)ptimizable
(s)peech (m)odel and (i)nhomogeneous (e)rror
criterion \[[1](#bib.bib1)\], generalizes existing clean speech
estimators. For example, if $`\beta=1`$ and $`\mu=1`$, (M)MSE estimation
with (o)ptimizable (s)peech (m)odel and (i)nhomogeneous (e)rror
criterion \[[1](#bib.bib1)\] is equivalent to Ephraim and Malah’s
short-term spectral amplitude estimator \[[3](#bib.bib3)\] and, for very
small values of $`\beta`$ and $`\mu=1`$, the log-spectral amplitude
estimator \[[4](#bib.bib4)\] is approximated. Super-Gaussian estimators
are obtained for $`\mu<1`$.
Table [I](#S2.T1 "TABLE I ‣ II Signal Model and Speech Estimators ‣ On the Importance of Super-Gaussian Speech Priors for Machine-Learning Based Speech Enhancement")
gives an overview over the related estimators.

| $`\mu`$ | $`\beta`$ | Related estimator |
|----|----|----|
| 1 | 1 | Gaussian STSA \[[3](#bib.bib3)\] |
| 1 | $`\beta\rightarrow 0`$ | Gaussian LSA \[[4](#bib.bib4)\] |
| $`\mu<1`$ | 1 | super-Gaussian short-term spectral amplitude estimator \[[9](#bib.bib9), [8](#bib.bib8)\] |
| $`\mu<1`$ | $`\beta\rightarrow 0`$ | super-Gaussian log-spectral amplitude estimator \[[10](#bib.bib10)\] |

TABLE I: List of clean speech estimators that (M)MSE estimation with
(o)ptimizable (s)peech (m)odel and (i)nhomogeneous (e)rror
criterion \[[1](#bib.bib1)\] generalizes.

To evaluate the expression
in ([2](#S2.E2 "In II Signal Model and Speech Estimators ‣ On the Importance of Super-Gaussian Speech Priors for Machine-Learning Based Speech Enhancement")),
estimates of the speech power spectral density $`\Lambda^{s}_{k,\ell}`$
and the noise power spectral density $`\Lambda^{n}_{k,\ell}`$ are
required. These can be obtained from non-machine-learning spectral
envelope-based speech power spectral density and noise power spectral
density estimators. In this paper, the noise power spectral
density $`\Lambda^{n}_{k,\ell}`$ is estimated using \[[6](#bib.bib6)\].
The speech power spectral density of the non-machine-learning spectral
envelope-based enhancement scheme is estimated using temporal cepstrum
smoothing as proposed in \[[5](#bib.bib5)\]. The enhancement scheme that
results from using these speech and noise power spectral density
estimator in (M)MSE estimation with (o)ptimizable (s)peech (m)odel and
(i)nhomogeneous (e)rror criterion is referred to as non-machine-learning
spectral envelope-based enhancement scheme throughout this paper.
However, also machine-learning based estimators of the clean speech and
the noise power spectral density can be employed which are considered
next.

## III DNN-Based Speech Enhancement Scheme

As the first example of an machine-learning spectral envelope
enhancement scheme, a method using a deep neural network-based phoneme
recognizer similar to \[[20](#bib.bib20)\] is considered. Similarly,
machine-learning spectral envelope models have also been used for
enhancement schemes in \[[15](#bib.bib15), [16](#bib.bib16),
[17](#bib.bib17), [18](#bib.bib18), [19](#bib.bib19),
[21](#bib.bib21)\]. In \[[20](#bib.bib20)\], a two step procedure is
used for speech enhancement. First, the spoken phoneme is identified
from the noisy observation. After that, a learned speech power spectral
density corresponding to the recognized phoneme is used in a clean
speech estimator, e.g., (M)MSE estimation with (o)ptimizable (s)peech
(m)odel and (i)nhomogeneous (e)rror criterion \[[1](#bib.bib1)\], to
enhance the noisy observation. As speech is modeled on a phoneme level,
the speech spectral fine structures, e.g., the spectral harmonics, are
not resolved.



Fig. 1: Architecture of the employed DNN.

For phoneme recognition, a deep neural network is used with the
architecture shown in
Fig. [1](#S3.F1 "Fig. 1 ‣ III DNN-Based Speech Enhancement Scheme ‣ On the Importance of Super-Gaussian Speech Priors for Machine-Learning Based Speech Enhancement").
The deep neural network’s input is given by 13 Mel-frequency cepstral
coefficients including the $`\Delta`$ and $`\Delta\Delta`$ accelerations
which are extracted for each frame $`\ell`$. To these features, a
context is added by including the features of the three previous and
three future segments which results in the feature vector
$`\mathbf{v}_{\ell}={[v_{1,\ell},\ldots,v_{V,\ell}]}^{T}`$ with
dimensionality $`V=273`$. Here, $`v_{i,\ell}`$ denote the elements of
the feature vector $`\mathbf{v}_{\ell}`$. Further, $`\cdot^{T}`$ denotes
the vector and matrix transpose. For the employed segment length and
segment shift, the context is approximately 100 ms. To improve the
robustness of the recognition in noisy environments, the feature vectors
are normalized using cepstral mean and variance
normalization \[[29](#bib.bib29)\] before they are employed for training
or testing \[[20](#bib.bib20)\]. The cepstral mean and variance
normalization is applied per utterance.

The features are passed through two hidden layers to finally obtain a
score $`f(q|\mathbf{v}_{\ell})`$ for each phoneme
$`q\in\{1,\ldots,Q\}`$. We base the number of phonemes on the annotation
given in the TIMIT database \[[30](#bib.bib30)\] which distinguishes
between $`Q=61`$ classes including pauses and non-speech events. The
hidden layers of the deep neural network consist of $`H_{1}`$ and
$`H_{2}`$ outputs, where $`H_{1}=H_{2}=512`$ is used. Similar
to \[[31](#bib.bib31), [32](#bib.bib32), [20](#bib.bib20)\], rectified
linear units are employed as transfer functions of these two layers. For
the output layer, a softmax transfer function is used which is
interpreted as the posterior probability $`f(q|\mathbf{v}_{\ell})`$ that
phoneme $`q`$ was spoken given the features $`\mathbf{v}_{\ell}`$.

$`\Lambda^{s|q}_{k}`$.

utterance to give $`\mathbf{v}_{\ell}`$.

6:   for all phonemes $`q`$ do

7:    Obtain clean speech estimate $`\hat{{S}}_{{k,\ell}}^{(q)}`$ for
phoneme $`q`$.For this, $`\Lambda^{s|q}_{k}`$ and
$`\hat{\Lambda}^{n}_{k,\ell}`$ are employed
in ([2](#S2.E2 "In II Signal Model and Speech Estimators ‣ On the Importance of Super-Gaussian Speech Priors for Machine-Learning Based Speech Enhancement")).

8:   end for

9:   Obtain final clean speech estimate $`\hat{{S}}_{{k,\ell}}`$

Algorithm 1 DNN-based enhancement scheme.

For the enhancement, machine-learning spectral envelope-based clean
speech power spectral densitys $`\Lambda^{s|q}_{k}`$ are employed where
each $`\Lambda^{s|q}_{k}`$ represents the speech power spectral density
of a specific phoneme $`q`$. During processing, each
$`\Lambda^{s|q}_{k}`$ is used
in ([2](#S2.E2 "In II Signal Model and Speech Estimators ‣ On the Importance of Super-Gaussian Speech Priors for Machine-Learning Based Speech Enhancement"))
via $`\xi_{{k,\ell}}=\Lambda^{s|q}_{k}/\Lambda^{n}_{k,\ell}`$, which
yields the phoneme specific clean speech estimates
$`\hat{{S}}_{{k,\ell}}^{(q)}`$. For this, the noise power spectral
density $`\Lambda^{n}_{k,\ell}`$ is estimated using \[[6](#bib.bib6)\].
Similar to \[[20](#bib.bib20)\], the estimates
$`\hat{{S}}_{{k,\ell}}^{(q)}`$ are averaged based on the recognition
scores $`f(q|\mathbf{v}_{\ell})`$ to give a final estimate
$`\hat{{S}}_{{k,\ell}}`$. More specifically, the clean speech
coefficients are obtained by

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\hat{{S}}_{{k,\ell}}=\sum_{j=1}^{Q}f(q=j|\mathbf{v}_{\ell})\hat{{S}}_{{k,\ell}}^{(q)}.
``` |  | (3) |

The steps required to enhance the noisy observations $`{Y}_{{k,\ell}}`$
using the deep neural network-based enhancement scheme are summarized in
Algorithm [1](#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ III DNN-Based Speech Enhancement Scheme ‣ On the Importance of Super-Gaussian Speech Priors for Machine-Learning Based Speech Enhancement").

For the training of the deep neural network-based machine-learning
spectral envelope system, we employ 1196 gender and phonetically
balanced sentences from the TIMIT training set. As
in \[[20](#bib.bib20)\], the deep neural network is trained only using
clean speech to ensure that the phoneme recognition does not depend on
the background noise type. The target vectors for the training are given
by a one-hot encoding of the TIMIT phoneme
annotation \[[30](#bib.bib30)\]. The error function is given by the
cross-entropy which is minimized using scaled conjugate gradient
back-propagation \[[33](#bib.bib33)\]. Before back-propagation, the
weights of the deep neural network’s two hidden layers are initialized
using the Glorot method \[[34](#bib.bib34)\]. The weights of the output
layer are initialized using the Nguyen-Widrow
method \[[35](#bib.bib35)\].

Similar to the non-machine-learning spectral envelope-based enhancement
scheme, the noise power spectral density $`\Lambda^{n}_{k,\ell}`$ is
estimated using \[[6](#bib.bib6)\]. The speech power spectral
densitys $`\Lambda^{s|q}_{k}`$ that are linked to the phonemes $`q`$ are
obtained as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\Lambda^{s|q}_{k}=\frac{1}{|\mathbb{L}^{(q)}|}\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{L}^{(q)}}|{S}_{{k,\ell}}|^{2},
``` |  | (4) |

where $`\mathbb{L}^{(q)}`$ denotes the set that contains the segments
that belong to the phoneme $`q`$ in the training data.
As ([4](#S3.E4 "In III DNN-Based Speech Enhancement Scheme ‣ On the Importance of Super-Gaussian Speech Priors for Machine-Learning Based Speech Enhancement"))
is scale-dependent, we normalize the time-domain clean speech input
signal both in training and testing such that all sentences have the
same peak value. During training, the clean speech data is available,
while during testing, oracle knowledge is provided. This normalization
is also employed for the other enhancement schemes, i.e., for the
non-machine-learning spectral envelope-based and the nonnegative matrix
factorization-based enhancement scheme given in
Section [IV](#S4 "IV NMF-Based Speech Enhancement Scheme ‣ On the Importance of Super-Gaussian Speech Priors for Machine-Learning Based Speech Enhancement").
Here, however, the normalization has no influence as these approaches
are scale-independent.

## IV NMF-Based Speech Enhancement Scheme

In this part, the machine-learning spectral envelope-based enhancement
scheme that employs nonnegative matrix factorization is described. It
serves as a second example for machine-learning spectral envelope-based
enhancement schemes. nonnegative matrix factorization approximates a
nonnegative matrix $`\mathbf{Y}`$ as
$`\mathbf{Y}\approx\mathbf{{B}}\mathbf{{H}}`$, where $`\mathbf{{B}}`$
and $`\mathbf{{H}}`$ are also nonnegative matrices. The columns of
$`\mathbf{{B}}`$ are referred to as basis vectors and the columns of
$`\mathbf{{H}}`$ as activation vectors. nonnegative matrix factorization
has been used for source separation, e.g., \[[36](#bib.bib36),
[37](#bib.bib37), [22](#bib.bib22)\], and has also been applied to
speech enhancement, e.g., \[[38](#bib.bib38), [39](#bib.bib39),
[40](#bib.bib40)\].

Here, a simple, supervised, sparse nonnegative matrix factorization
approach is used which employs the Itakura-Saito divergence as the cost
function \[[22](#bib.bib22), [23](#bib.bib23)\]. As argued
in \[[22](#bib.bib22)\], if the noisy spectral coefficients
$`{Y}_{{k,\ell}}`$ are independent and follow a circular-symmetric
Gaussian distribution, minimizing the Itakura-Saito divergence for
approximating the noisy periodogram as
$`\left[|{Y}_{{k,\ell}}|^{2}\right]=\mathbf{Y}\approx\mathbf{{B}}\mathbf{{H}}`$
allows the elements of the product $`\mathbf{{B}}\mathbf{{H}}`$ to be
interpreted as the noisy power spectral
density $`\Lambda^{y}_{k,\ell}`$. The Itakura-Saito cost function
including the sparsity constraint is given by \[[23](#bib.bib23)\]

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
C=\nu|\mathbf{{H}}|_{1}+\sum_{i,j}\frac{(\mathbf{Y})_{i,j}}{(\mathbf{{B}}\mathbf{{H}})_{i,j}}+\log\left(\frac{(\mathbf{Y})_{i,j}}{(\mathbf{{B}}\mathbf{{H}})_{i,j}}\right)-1,
``` |  | (5) |

where $`(\cdot)_{i,j}`$ denotes element of the respective matrix,
$`|\cdot|_{1}`$ the $`L_{1}`$-norm, and $`\nu`$ is the factor that
controls the sparsity. This cost function can be optimized using the
multiplicative update rules in \[[23](#bib.bib23)\].

For estimating the speech and the noise power spectral density, it is
assumed that the basis matrix $`\mathbf{{B}}`$ is given by the
concatenation of a speech basis matrix $`\mathbf{{B}}^{(s)}`$ and a
noise basis matrix $`\mathbf{{B}}^{(n)}`$ as
$`\mathbf{{B}}=[\mathbf{{B}}^{(s)},\mathbf{{B}}^{(n)}]`$. The speech and
noise basis matrices are learned prior to the processing and are held
fixed during processing. This means that only the activation matrices
are updated. For obtaining an estimate of $`\Lambda^{s}_{k,\ell}`$ and
$`\Lambda^{n}_{k,\ell}`$, also the activation matrix $`\mathbf{{H}}`$ is
split into a speech and noise dependent part as
$`\mathbf{{H}}={[(\mathbf{{H}}^{(s)})^{T},(\mathbf{{H}}^{(n)})^{T}]}^{T}`$
such that
$`\mathbf{Y}\approx\mathbf{{B}}\mathbf{{H}}=[\mathbf{{B}}^{(s)},\mathbf{{B}}^{(n)}]{[(\mathbf{{H}}^{(s)})^{T},(\mathbf{{H}}^{(n)})^{T}]}^{T}`$.
With this, the speech and the noise power spectral density can be
obtained as

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\hat{\Lambda}^{s}_{k,\ell}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{I^{(s)}}(\mathbf{{B}}^{(s)})_{k,i}(\mathbf{{H}}^{(s)})_{i,\ell}`$ |  | (6) |
|  | $`\displaystyle\hat{\Lambda}^{n}_{k,\ell}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{I^{(n)}}(\mathbf{{B}}^{(n)})_{k,i}(\mathbf{{H}}^{(n)})_{i,\ell},`$ |  | (7) |

where $`I^{(s)}`$ is the number of speech basis while $`I^{(n)}`$
denotes the number of noise bases. The steps for enhancing the noisy
observations are summarized in
Algorithm [2](#alg2 "Algorithm 2 ‣ IV NMF-Based Speech Enhancement Scheme ‣ On the Importance of Super-Gaussian Speech Priors for Machine-Learning Based Speech Enhancement").

## VI Instrumental Evaluation

We evaluate the performance of the different speech estimators using
instrumental measures such as Perceptual Evaluation of Speech Quality
improvement scores \[[44](#bib.bib44)\] and segmental signal-to-noise
ratio improvements \[[14](#bib.bib14), [45](#bib.bib45)\]. The
improvements are based on the noisy signal, i.e., they are computed as
the difference between the raw scores of the enhanced signal and the
noisy signal. Additionally, the segmental speech signal-to-noise ratio
and the segmental noise reduction \[[14](#bib.bib14)\] are employed to
quantify the speech distortions and noise suppression, respectively.
Higher values for the segmental speech signal-to-noise ratio indicate
less speech distortion and higher values for the segmental noise
reduction indicates more noise reduction.

For this evaluation, we use 128 sentences from the TIMIT core set.
Again, it is ensured that the amount of audio material is balanced
between genders. The clean speech signals are artificially corrupted by
the same noise types used for training the nonnegative matrix
factorization-based enhancement scheme. The signal-to-noise ratios are
ranging from -5 dB to 20 dB in 5 dB steps. For each sentence, the
segment of the noise signal where the speech signals are embedded in is
randomly chosen. The instrumental measures are only evaluated after a
two second initialization period to avoid initialization artifacts that
may bias the results. Similarly, also the signal-to-noise ratios used
for the artificial mixing are determined based on the signal powers in
speech presence. Further, the noise segments that were used for training
the nonnegative matrix factorization-based enhancement scheme are
excluded in the evaluation for all enhancement schemes, i.e., also for
the non-machine-learning spectral envelope-based and the deep neural
network based enhancement schemes. This is done to make the enhancement
schemes more easily comparable.

signal quality predicted by Perceptual Evaluation of Speech Quality
considerably stronger.

### VI-B Comparison with Common Enhancement Schemes

In this final part of the evaluation section, we compare the
super-Gaussian estimators, i.e., (M)MSE estimation with (o)ptimizable
(s)peech (m)odel and (i)nhomogeneous (e)rror
criterion \[[1](#bib.bib1)\] to Gaussian approaches. To demonstrate that
super-Gaussian estimators considerably improve the performance of
machine-learning spectral envelope-based methods, we use the following
two parameter settings for (M)MSE estimation with (o)ptimizable (s)peech
(m)odel and (i)nhomogeneous (e)rror criterion \[[1](#bib.bib1)\]:
$`\beta=0.001,\mu=0.2`$ and $`\beta=1,\mu=0.2`$. The parameters are
chosen as a compromise such that all machine-learning spectral
envelope-based enhancement schemes yield satisfying results.

estimation with (o)ptimizable (s)peech (m)odel and (i)nhomogeneous

(o)ptimizable (s)peech (m)odel and (i)nhomogeneous (e)rror criterion
($`\mu=0.2,\beta=1`$) represent modern super-Gaussian speech estimators
(see

Fig. [8](#S6.F8 "Fig. 8 ‣ VI-B Comparison with Common Enhancement Schemes ‣ VI Instrumental Evaluation ‣ On the Importance of Super-Gaussian Speech Priors for Machine-Learning Based Speech Enhancement")
shows Perceptual Evaluation of Speech Quality improvement scores and
segmental signal-to-noise ratio measures for the considered enhancement
schemes. The results again show that for the non-machine-learning
spectral envelope-based enhancement scheme, a super-Gaussian estimator
only slightly improves the performance. Contrarily, the super-Gaussian
setup for (M)MSE estimation with (o)ptimizable (s)peech (m)odel and
(i)nhomogeneous (e)rror criterion \[[1](#bib.bib1)\] performs
considerably better than the Gaussian clean speech estimator, i.e., the
Gaussian short-term spectral amplitude estimator \[[3](#bib.bib3)\] and
the Gaussian log-spectral amplitude estimator \[[4](#bib.bib4)\], if the

shown in
Section [V](#S5 "V Importance of Super-Gaussianity for MLSE Based Speech Enhancement ‣ On the Importance of Super-Gaussian Speech Priors for Machine-Learning Based Speech Enhancement"),
the suppression capability of the Gaussian approaches is mainly
controlled by the *a priori* signal-to-noise ratio resulting in low
suppressions between harmonics for the machine-learning spectral
envelope-based enhancement schemes where the *a priori* signal-to-noise
ratio is overestimated. Here, this is reflected by the low segmental
noise reduction values observed for the deep neural network-based and
the nonnegative matrix factorization-based approach if the Gaussian
short-term spectral amplitude estimator \[[3](#bib.bib3)\] or the
Gaussian log-spectral amplitude estimator \[[4](#bib.bib4)\] are
employed. However, for the super-Gaussian estimators (M)MSE estimation
with (o)ptimizable (s)peech (m)odel and (i)nhomogeneous (e)rror
criterion $`(\mu=0.2,\beta=0.001)`$ and (M)MSE estimation with
(o)ptimizable (s)peech (m)odel and (i)nhomogeneous (e)rror criterion
$`(\mu=0.2,\beta=1)`$ the noise reduction is strongly increased and the
residual noise, e.g., the noise between harmonics, is reduced. This
comes with a slight increase in speech distortion for (M)MSE estimation
with (o)ptimizable (s)peech (m)odel and (i)nhomogeneous (e)rror
criterion $`(\mu=0.2,\beta=0.001)`$ as visible in a decrease in
segmental speech signal-to-noise ratio. For (M)MSE estimation with
(o)ptimizable (s)peech (m)odel and (i)nhomogeneous (e)rror criterion
($`\mu=0.2,\beta=1)`$, the segmental speech signal-to-noise ratio
remains unchanged or is even slightly increased. Overall, the behavior
of the super-Gaussian estimators helps to improve the quality predicted
by Perceptual Evaluation of Speech Quality and to improve the segmental
signal-to-noise ratio.

## VII Subjective Evaluation

As the results of instrumental measures cannot perfectly represent the
impressions of human listeners, we verify the results using a subjective
listening test. For this, we employ a multi-stimulus test with hidden
reference and anchor \[[46](#bib.bib46)\]. In the experiment, two
different acoustic scenarios are tested: traffic noise and babble noise
both at an signal-to-noise ratio of 5 dB. For both acoustic scenarios,
an utterance of a male and a female speaker taken from the TIMIT test
set are used. These signals are processed by the non-machine-learning
spectral envelope-based enhancement scheme, the deep neural
network-based enhancement scheme, and the nonnegative matrix
factorization-based enhancement schemes. For all enhancement schemes, a
Gaussian short-term spectral amplitude estimator ($`\mu=1,\beta=1`$) and
a super-Gaussian short-term spectral amplitude estimator
($`\mu=0.2,\beta=1`$) are compared (see
Table [I](#S2.T1 "TABLE I ‣ II Signal Model and Speech Estimators ‣ On the Importance of Super-Gaussian Speech Priors for Machine-Learning Based Speech Enhancement")).
Even though (M)MSE estimation with (o)ptimizable (s)peech (m)odel and
(i)nhomogeneous (e)rror criterion with $`\mu=0.2`$ and $`\beta=0.001`$
achieves the highest scores in most instrumental measures, we use (M)MSE
estimation with (o)ptimizable (s)peech (m)odel and (i)nhomogeneous
(e)rror criterion with $`\beta=1`$ in the subjective listening test as
this configuration produces less musical artifacts.

In each trial, four signals are presented to the listeners: the noisy
signals processed by the Gaussian and the super-Gaussian estimator, an
anchor, and a hidden reference. The trials are repeated over all
combinations of acoustic conditions, speakers and enhancement schemes.
The reference signal is a noisy signal with an signal-to-noise ratio
17 dB. Finally, for the anchor, the clean speech utterance is filtered
using a low-pass filter at a cutoff frequency of 4 kHz and mixed at an
signal-to-noise ratio of $`-5~\text{dB}`$. This signal is processed
using a non-machine-learning spectral envelope-based enhancement scheme
where the noise power spectral density is estimated
using \[[6](#bib.bib6)\] and the speech power spectral density is
obtained using the decision-directed approach \[[3](#bib.bib3)\] with a
smoothing constant set to $`0.9`$. A Wiener filter with a minimum gain
of $`-20~\text{dB}`$ is employed to obtain the anchor. The sound
examples used in the experiment are also available at
https://uhh.de/inf-sp-tasl2018a.

A total of 13 subjects have participated in the multi-stimulus test with
hidden reference and anchor. The test took place in a quiet office and
the subjects listened to diotic signals played back through headphones
(Beyerdynamic DT-770 Pro 250 Ohm) through a RME Fireface UFX+ sound
card. The test was conducted in two phases. In the first phase, the
subjects were asked to listen to a subset of the files used in test such
that they can familiarize themselves with the different signals. During
this training phase, the listeners were also asked to set the level of
the headphones to a comfortable level. In the second phase, the
listener’s task was to judge the overall quality of the signals on a
scale ranging from 0 to 100, where 0 was labeled with “bad” and 100 with
“excellent”. The order of presentations of algorithms and conditions
were randomized between all subjects.




Fig. 9: Box plot of the subjective ratings for different enhancement
schemes.

The obtained multi-stimulus test with hidden reference and anchor scores
are summarized in
Fig. [9](#S7.F9 "Fig. 9 ‣ VII Subjective Evaluation ‣ On the Importance of Super-Gaussian Speech Priors for Machine-Learning Based Speech Enhancement")
using box plots. The upper and the lower edge of the box show the upper
and lower quartile while the bar within the box is the median. The upper
whisker reaches to the largest data point that is smaller than the upper
quartile plus 1.5 times the interquartile range. The lower whisker is
defined analogously. The crosses denote outliers that do not fall in the
range spanned by both whiskers. For each box plot, the results of all
acoustic conditions and speakers are pooled, which yields 52 data
points. The result show that all participants were able to detect the
hidden reference, which had to be rated with 100, and that the anchor
was consistently given the lowest scores. Further, the results clearly
confirm that for the deep neural network and the nonnegative matrix
factorization based enhancement scheme, the sound quality of the
super-Gaussian estimator is considered better than the Gaussian
estimator. For the non-machine-learning spectral envelope-based
estimator, however, the multi-stimulus test with hidden reference and
anchor scores of the Gaussian and the super-Gaussian estimator are
nearly the same.

Finally, a brief statistical analysis of the results confirms that the
differences in multi-stimulus test with hidden reference and anchor
scores between the Gaussian and super-Gaussian estimators are
statistically significant for the machine-learning spectral
envelope-based enhancement schemes. For the used statistical tests, a
significance level of $`\alpha=0.05`$ is employed. We apply a Wilcoxon
signed-rank test to test for the difference in medians between the
multi-stimulus test with hidden reference and anchor scores of the
Gaussian and super-Gaussian estimators. This test is employed as the
Shapiro-Wilk test indicates that the data is not Gaussian distributed
for all conditions. The different enhancement schemes, i.e., the
machine-learning spectral envelope-based approaches and the
non-machine-learning spectral envelope-based approach, are treated
separately. Considering the difference between the Gaussian and
super-Gaussian clean speech estimators for the machine-learning spectral
envelope-based approaches, the differences are significant in both cases
(deep neural network: $`p<0.001`$, nonnegative matrix factorization:
$`p<0.001`$). Comparing the estimators for the non-machine-learning
spectral envelope-based algorithm reveals no significant difference
($`p=0.55`$). Hence, the subjective listening tests confirm the
previously obtained results of the instrumental measures.

## VIII Conclusions

In this paper, super-Gaussian clean speech estimators have been analyzed
in the context of machine-learning based speech enhancement approaches
that employ spectral envelope models. We refer to these approaches as
machine-learning spectral envelope. In the analysis part, we showed that
the usage of envelope models results in an overestimation of the *a
priori* signal-to-noise ratio, e.g., between speech spectral harmonics.
As a consequence, using Gaussian estimators, noise between harmonic
structures cannot be reduced such that residual noises remain after the
enhancement. However, in this paper, we show that employing
super-Gaussian clean speech estimators, such as (M)MSE estimation with
(o)ptimizable (s)peech (m)odel and (i)nhomogeneous (e)rror
criterion \[[1](#bib.bib1)\], leads to a reduction of the undesired
residual noise. This interesting result stems from the higher
attenuation that is applied by the super-Gaussian estimators if the *a
posteriori* signal-to-noise ratios are low. This allows the estimators
to compensate for the overestimated *a priori* signal-to-noise ratios
without any further post-processing steps. As a consequence, we showed
via theoretical analysis and experimental evaluation that for
machine-learning spectral envelope-based enhancement schemes,
super-Gaussian estimators have a much larger effect on improving the
enhancement performance than for classic non-machine-learning spectral
envelope-based enhancement schemes. Sound examples of the considered
algorithms are given at https://uhh.de/inf-sp-tasl2018a.
