# Employing Real Training Data for Deep Noise Suppression

Ziyi Xu    Marvin Sach    Jan Pirklbauer    Tim Fingscheidt

###### Abstract

Most deep noise suppression (DNS) models are trained with
reference-based losses requiring access to clean speech. However,
sometimes an additive microphone model is insufficient for real-world
applications. Accordingly, ways to use real training data in supervised
learning for DNS models promise to reduce a potential training/inference
mismatch. Employing real data for DNS training requires either
generative approaches or a reference-free loss without access to the
corresponding clean speech. In this work, we propose to employ an
end-to-end non-intrusive deep neural network (DNN), named PESQ-DNN, to
estimate perceptual evaluation of speech quality (PESQ) scores of
enhanced real data. It provides a reference-free perceptual loss for
employing real data during DNS training, maximizing the PESQ scores.
Furthermore, we use an epoch-wise alternating training protocol,
updating the DNS model on real data, followed by PESQ-DNN updating on
synthetic data. The DNS model trained with the PESQ-DNN employing real
data outperforms all reference methods employing only synthetic training
data. On synthetic test data, our proposed method excels the Interspeech
2021 DNS Challenge baseline by a significant 0.32 PESQ points. Both on
synthetic and real test data, the proposed method beats the baseline by
0.05 DNSMOS points – although PESQ-DNN optimizes for a different
perceptual metric.

## 1 Introduction

Deep noise suppression (DNS) methods employ deep neural networks (DNNs)
to improve perceptual quality and intelligibility of a speech signal
distorted by background noise and sometimes reverberation. Most DNS
models are trained with reference-based loss functions requiring access
to clean speech, in some cases even to additive noise. Accordingly, the
employed training data is synthetic: The clean speech and noise are
pre-processed separately and mixed by addition to simulate noisy
mixtures with different signal-to-noise ratios (SNRs), while the effect
of reverberation (if considered) is realized by convolution with either
simulated or recorded room impulse responses \[1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8\].
Strake et al. \[6\] proposed a fully convolutional recurrent neural
network (FCRN) trained with a loss function based on the mean squared
error (MSE) for joint denoising and dereverberation. Braun et al. \[8\]
investigated the contributions of various MSE-based spectral losses for
training a recurrent neural network (RNN) and proposed a loss function
combining complex spectral and compressed magnitude loss terms, which
provides state-of-the-art performance.

Beyond training, synthetic data is also prominently used to evaluate the
performance of the trained DNS model. This employs intrusive
instrumental metrics requiring access to the clean reference speech
signal, e.g., perceptual evaluation of speech quality (PESQ) \[9\] and
perceptual objective listening quality assessment (POLQA) \[10\], both
reflecting the perceived speech quality, and short-time objective
intelligibility (STOI) \[11\] for estimating the speech intelligibility.
However, the linear additive microphone model with statistically
independent speech and noise is often invalid in real-world applications
due to, e.g., the Lombard effect, where the speakers attend to speak
loudly with higher pitch under very noisy conditions, and also due to
clipping effects of the overloaded high-level microphone signal. Such
effects may lead to a potential training/inference mismatch.

In contrast, evaluation of a DNS model on real test recordings requires
either a discriminator from a GAN-based approach \[1, 2\], or
non-intrusive instrumental metrics without knowing the clean reference
signal. In Microsoft DNS Challenges \[12, 13\], Reddy et al. provided a
non-intrusive instrumental measure called DNSMOS \[14\] to predict ITU-T
P.808 \[15\] subjective rating scores employing a DNN. The latest DNSMOS
\[16\] separately estimates the quality of the speech component,
background noise, and the overall enhanced speech following ITU-T P.835
\[17\]. Mittag et al. proposed an RNN named NISQA \[18\] to estimate the
subjective rating scores focusing on coded speech under various
transmission conditions. In \[19, 20, 21\], an end-to-end non-intrusive
PESQNet DNN was proposed to estimate the PESQ scores of the enhanced
speech signal, thus providing a reference-free perceptual loss for
training a DNS model. In \[19\], the authors proposed a joint loss
combining an MSE loss and the perceptual loss offered by the PESQNet for
fine-tuning a pre-trained DNS model employing both real and synthetic
data. However, this approach revealed instabilities in the PESQNet/DNS
joint training. The same authors solved the problem with an epoch-based
alternating training protocol in their following works \[20, 21\].
Notably, the authors mentioned that the new approach “opens the door”
for using real training data, however, without providing evidence for
this hypothesis. In \[22\], an improved end-to-end non-intrusive
PESQ-DNN was proposed to estimate PESQ scores for coded speech signals
obtained from various wideband codecs considering different transmission
conditions. PESQ-DNN employs a complex spectrogram as input and yields
better performance than the PESQNet and a more stable training process.

In this work, our contributions are fourfold: First, we successfully
entered the door ”opened” in \[20, 21\] to use real training data with
their proposed training protocol. Second, we replace PESQNet with the
better performing PESQ-DNN. Third, we investigate the potential of
employing a minibatch-based PESQ-DNN/DNS alternating training protocol.
Finally, we train the FCRN proposed in \[6\] as our DNS model and
perform an extensive experimental evaluation. Even though we optimize
for PESQ during FCRN training, we provide the results showing that the
use of real training data improves both on synthetic and real test data.
It improves both on PESQ and on other non-intrusive instrumental
metrics, e.g., DNSMOS.

The rest of the paper is structured as follows: Section 2 introduces the
DNS and the employed PESQ-DNN processing. The novel PESQ-DNN/DNS joint
training employing real training data is presented in Section 3. The
experimental setup and results are discussed in Section 4, followed by
the conclusion in Section 5.

## 2 Speech Enhancement With PESQ-DNN

### 2.1 Deep Noise Suppression (DNS)

Following \[19, 20, 21\], we pre-train the FCRN proposed in \[6\] as our
DNS model with synthetic data. Accordingly, the microphone mixture
$`y(n)`$ is synthesized from the clean speech signal $`s(n)`$,
reverberated by the room impulse response (RIR) $`h(n)`$, and disturbed
by additive noise $`d(n)`$ as

```math
\vskip-2.84526pty(n)=s(n)*h(n)+d(n)=s^{\text{rev}}(n)+d(n), \tag{1}
```

with $`s^{\text{rev}}(n)`$ and $`n`$ being the reverberated clean speech
signal and the discrete-time sample index, respectively, and $`*`$
denoting a convolution operation. Subsequently, after windowing, the
signals are converted to the discrete Fourier transform (DFT) domain by:

```math
Y_{\ell}(k)=S^{\text{rev}}_{\ell}(k)+D_{\ell}(k),\vskip-2.84526pt \tag{2}
```

with frame index $`\ell`$ and frequency bin index
$`k\!\in\!\mathcal{K}\!=\!\left\{0,1,\ldots,K\!-\!1\right\}`$, and $`K`$
being the DFT size. The employed FCRN implicitly estimates a
magnitude-bounded complex mask $`M_{\ell}\left(k\right)\in\mathbb{C}`$,
with $`\left|M_{\ell}\left(k\right)\right|\in\left[0,1\right]`$, to
enhance the noisy speech spectrum by:

```math
\vskip-2.84526pt\hat{S}_{\ell}\left(k\right)=Y_{\ell}(k)\cdot M_{\ell}\left(k\right).\vskip-2.84526pt \tag{3}
```

Finally, the enhanced speech spectrum $`\hat{S}_{\ell}\left(k\right)`$
is subject to an inverse DFT (IDFT), followed by overlap add (OLA) to
reconstruct the time-domain enhanced speech signal $`\hat{s}(n)`$.

Instead of the joint denoising and dereverberation loss proposed in
\[7\] and used in \[19, 20, 21\], we employ the loss proposed by Braun
et al. \[8\] for pre-training, proven to offer state-of-the-art
performance:

```math
\displaystyle J^{\text{Braun}}_{u} \displaystyle=\!\frac{\alpha}{L_{u}\!\cdot\!K}\!\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{L}_{u}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\!\left|\hat{S}_{\ell}(k)\!-\!S_{\ell}(k)\right|^{2} \tag{4}
```

```math
\displaystyle+\!\frac{1\!-\!\alpha}{L_{u}\!\cdot\!K}\!\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{L}_{u}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\!\left|\left|\hat{S}_{\ell}(k)\right|^{c}\!-\!\left|S_{\ell}(k)\right|^{c}\right|^{2},
```

with $`\ell\!\in\!\mathcal{L}_{u}\!=\!\left\{1,2,\ldots,L_{u}\right\}`$,
and $`L_{u}`$ being the number of frames in an entire utterance indexed
by $`u`$. Following \[8\], the compression and the weighting factors
between the complex and magnitude loss terms are set to $`c=0.3`$ and
$`\alpha=0.7`$, respectively.

### 2.2 Non-Intrusive PESQ-DNN

To estimate PESQ scores of the enhanced speech signal obtained from our
FCRN DNS, we adopt the non-intrusive PESQ-DNN proposed by Xu et al.
\[22\], which employs frame-level embeddings (FLE) and average pooling.
The input of the non-intrusive PESQ-DNN is the enhanced speech spectrum
$`\hat{S}_{\ell}\left(k\right)`$, with $`\ell\!\in\!\mathcal{L}_{u}`$
defined in (4). The final output is the utterance-wise estimated PESQ
score $`\widehat{\text{PESQ}}_{u}`$, which should be close to its
corresponding ground truth $`\text{PESQ}_{u}`$ measured by ITU-T P.862.2
\[9\]. For training the PESQ-DNN, we employ the loss function \[22\]

```math
J^{\text{PESQ}}_{u}\!=\left(\widehat{\text{PESQ}}_{u}\!-\!\text{PESQ}_{u}\right)^{2}\!+\tfrac{\alpha_{u}}{B_{u}\cdot L_{b}}\!\sum\limits_{b\in\mathcal{B}_{u}}\sum\limits_{\ell\in\mathcal{L}_{b}}\!\left(q_{b}(\ell)\!-\!\text{PESQ}_{u}\right)^{2}, \tag{5}
```

with $`q_{b}(\ell)`$ being some intermediate FLE PESQ scores,
representing the predicted PESQ scores for each frame indexed by
$`\ell\!\in\!\mathcal{L}_{b}`$, and $`\mathcal{L}_{b}`$ being the set of
frames belonging to feature block $`b`$. Parameter
$`B_{u}=\left|\mathcal{B}_{u}\right|`$ represents the total number of
feature blocks for utterance $`u`$. The utterance-wise weighting factor
is represented by

```math
\alpha_{u}=0.9^{\left|\text{PESQ}_{u}-\text{PESQ}_{\text{max}}\right|}, \tag{6}
```

with $`\text{PESQ}_{\text{max}}=4.64`$ being the maximum PESQ score
defined in \[9\]. This weighting factor encourages the intermediate FLE
PESQ to be equal to the utterance-wise PESQ score for a speech utterance
with good quality: A perfect overall perceptual quality should be
reflected everywhere in the utterance, e.g., each frame should have the
same high PESQ score.

## 3 DNS Training with Real Recordings

Following the training schemes proposed in \[20, 21\], we employ
PESQ-DNN to fine-tune the pre-trained FCRN DNS, however, employing real
data to further increase the PESQ scores of the enhanced speech signal.
Accordingly, we define a loss

```math
\vskip-2.84526ptJ^{\text{PESQ-DNN}}_{u}\!=\left(\widehat{\text{PESQ}}_{u}-\text{PESQ}_{\text{max}}\right)^{2}\vskip-2.84526pt \tag{7}
```

for utterance $`u`$ provided by the PESQ-DNN to maximize the PESQ scores
of the FCRN DNS, with $`\text{PESQ}_{\text{max}}\!=\!4.64`$. We
investigate two different joint training protocols, as depicted in
Fig. 1. To keep the employed PESQ-DNN up-to-date, we adopt the epoch
(EP)-level joint training protocol, originally proposed in \[20\]: The
FCRN DNS is fine-tuned with a fixed PESQ-DNN for one epoch of real data,
followed by PESQ-DNN training employing an epoch of synthetic data,
adapting to the current updated FCRN DNS. Please note that for PESQ-DNN
training we can only apply synthetic data, since only then the clean
reference signal is available for target PESQ score calculation
following ITU-T P.862.2 \[9\].

Besides the EP-level alternating training protocol successfully used in
\[20, 21\], we also investigate a minibatch (MB) -level training
protocol, where the FCRN DNS is fine-tuned with one minibatch of real
data followed by the PESQ-DNN updating with one minibatch of synthetic
data. The DNS and PESQ-DNN are trained alternatingly on real and
synthetic data, controlled by both switches in Fig. 1 in the upper and
lower positions, respectively. The blue arrows indicate the gradient
flow back-propagated for the DNS training, while the green ones are for
PESQ-DNN.

In this work, signals have a sampling rate of $`16\,\text{kHz}`$ and we
apply a periodic Hann window with a frame length of $`384`$ with a
$`50\%`$ overlap, followed by an FFT of size $`K=512`$. The employed
FCRN DNS is adopted from \[6\] and is used in \[7, 19, 20, 21\]. The
number of input and output frequency bins of the employed FCRN DNS is
set to $`260`$. The used PESQ-DNN has exactly the same topology as
originally proposed in \[22\], employing the enhanced speech spectra
(containing $`260`$ frequency bins) from the FCRN DNS as input. The last
three frequency bins of the inputs for both FCRN DNS and PESQ-DNN are
redundant due to divisibility constraints. For the PESQ-DNN, the number
of frames in each feature block, denoted by
$`L_{b}=\left|\mathcal{L}_{b}\right|`$ in (5), is set to $`16`$.

The FCRN DNS and PESQ-DNN are firstly pre-trained and validated on
synthetic datasets $`\mathcal{D}^{\text{train}}_{\text{WSJ0}}`$ and
$`\mathcal{D}^{\text{val}}_{\text{WSJ0}}`$, respectively, which are used
in \[20, 21\] with clean speech from WSJ0 speech corpus \[24\] and noise
from DEMAND \[25\] and QUT \[26\]. The fine-tuning of the FCRN DNS and
PESQ-DNN are based on the Microsoft DNS Challenge dataset, which
features harsh conditions considering the diversities of languages,
speakers, noise types, and SNR conditions. Accordingly, we follow the
two-stage fine-tuning strategy applied in \[20, 21\]. The 1^(st)-stage
fine-tuning of the FCRN DNS and PESQ-DNN employing synthetic dataset
based on the official Interspeech 2021 DNS Challenge (dubbed DNS3)
training material \[13\], including $`100`$ hours of training set
$`\mathcal{D}^{\text{train}}_{\text{DNS3}}`$ and $`10`$ hours of
validation set $`\mathcal{D}^{\text{val}}_{\text{DNS3}}`$ generated with
the same setting as in \[20\].

In pre-training and 1^(st)-stage fine-tuning, the FCRN DNS and PESQ-DNN
are trained using losses (4) and (5) with an initial learning rate of
$`10^{-4}`$ and $`5\cdot 10^{-5}`$, respectively, employing Adam
optimizer. The learning rate is halved once the corresponding validation
loss does not improve for two consecutive epochs. The training is
stopped after the validation loss is not improving for five consecutive
epochs, and the models with the lowest validation loss are saved.
Accordingly, the 1^(st)-stage fine-tuned FCRN DNS serves as a reference
method, denoted as “FCRN, $`J^{\text{Braun}}_{u}`$” in the following
discussion, to illustrate the PESQ performance improvement obtained from
the proposed 2^(nd)-stage fine-tuning employing real data. We pre-train
and fine-tune the same FCRN DNS employing the joint denoising and
dereverberation loss proposed in \[7\] as one of the baselines, shown as
“FCRN \[7\]” in Tabs. 1, 2, and 3.

The 2^(nd)-stage fine-tuning builds upon “FCRN,
$`J^{\text{Braun}}_{u}`$” and now trains on real data
$`\mathcal{D}^{\text{real}}_{\text{DNS}}`$, which comprises the real
recordings from the preliminary test datasets of DNS3 and both
preliminary and blind test datasets of the Interspeech 2020 DNS
Challenge (DNS1) \[27\] and the ICASSP 2020 DNS Challenge (DNS2) \[12\].
For both EP- and MB-based alternating training protocols, the FCRN DNS
is trained on $`\mathcal{D}^{\text{real}}_{\text{DNS}}`$ for $`26`$
epochs, while the PESQ-DNN is updated employing the synthetic dataset
$`\mathcal{D}^{\text{train}}_{\text{DNS3}}`$, with learning rates of
$`2\cdot 10^{-5}`$ and $`5\cdot 10^{-5}`$, respectively. Our newly
trained FCRN DNS with different training protocols are marked with
either “EP” or “MB” in the following result tables. As another
reference, we run the 1^(st)-stage fine-tuning for additional $`26`$
epochs based on “FCRN, $`J^{\text{Braun}}_{u}`$”, thus illustrating that
the performance improvement in the 2^(nd)-stage fine-tuning cannot
solely be attributed to more training epochs. This reference is denoted
as “FCRN, $`J^{\text{Braun}}_{u}`$, cont.” in Tabs. 1, 2, and 3.

As further baselines employed in Tabs. 1, 2, and 3, we follow \[19, 20,
21\] and perform the 2^(nd)-stage fine-tuning on “FCRN \[7\]” with
either the non-intrusive PESQNet or the intrusive MF-PESQNet. The
baseline “FCRN/PESQNet \[19\]” represents the FCRN DNS fine-tuned on
both synthetic dataset $`\mathcal{D}^{\text{train}}_{\text{DNS3}}`$ and
real dataset $`\mathcal{D}^{\text{real}}_{\text{DNS}}`$ employing
PESQNet, but with a joint training protocol proposed in \[19\], revealed
to be unstable. Baselines “FCRN/PESQNet \[20\]” and “FCRN/MF-PESQNet
\[21\]” represent the FCRN model from \[20, 21\], which are fine-tuned
employing the old PESQNet and MF-PESQNet, respectively, using the
(successful) EP-based alternating training protocols on synthetic
dataset $`\mathcal{D}^{\text{train}}_{\text{DNS3}}`$. Furthermore, we
include the DNS3 baseline \[23\] provided by the challenge organizers,
named as “DNS3 Baseline \[23\]”.

We use the preliminary synthetic test set
$`\mathcal{D}^{\text{dev}}_{\text{DNS1}}`$ from DNS1 \[27\] for
development, see results in Tab. 1. The final evaluation is reported on
both the synthetic test set
$`\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{test}}_{\mathrm{DNS2}}`$ from DNS2 (Tab. 2) and
the real blind test set $`\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{test}}_{\mathrm{DNS3}}`$
from DNS3 (Tab. 3). We employ intrusive instrumental metrics to evaluate
the performance on synthetic test data, including PESQ \[9\], STOI
\[11\], segmental SNR improvement $`\Delta\text{SNR}_{\text{seg}}`$
\[28\], and speech-to-reverberation modulation energy ratio (SRMR)
\[29\]. Please note that $`\Delta\text{SNR}_{\text{seg}}`$ is only
employed under the conditions without reverberations to explicitly
evaluate the denoising performance, while SRMR reflects the
dereverberation effects and is only measured under reverberated
conditions. We also employ the non-intrusive instrumental measure of the
latest DNSMOS \[16\] on both synthetic and real test data, reporting
either the overall (OVRL) enhanced speech quality or the detailed ones,
including speech component quality (SIG) and background noise (BAK).

## 5 Conclusions

In this work, we show how to fine-tune a deep noise suppression (DNS)
model with real data, with the help of an end-to-end non-intrusive deep
neural network (DNN) named PESQ-DNN, which estimates perceptual
evaluation of speech quality (PESQ) scores of the enhanced speech
signal. The employed PESQ-DNN provides a differentiable reference-free
perceptual loss for the DNS training, aiming at maximizing the PESQ
scores of the trained DNS model. An epoch-level alternating training
protocol to train the DNS model with one epoch of real data, followed by
PESQ-DNN updating with one epoch of synthetic data, turned out to be
strongest. Detailed analyses show that the DNS model trained with the
PESQ-DNN and real data outperforms all reference methods employing only
synthetic data. On synthetic test data, our proposed method excels the
Interspeech 2021 DNS Challenge baseline by a highly significant $`0.32`$
PESQ points. Both on synthetic and on real test data, our baseline (not
employing real training data) is exceeded by the proposed method
(employing real training data) by $`0.05`$ DNSMOS points – although the
employed PESQ-DNN optimizes for PESQ.
