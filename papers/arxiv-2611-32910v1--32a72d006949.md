# MASK-BASED SPEECH ENHANCEMENT FOR SPATIAL AUDIO: EFFECTS OF SIGNAL REPRESENTATION AND CHANNEL-SPECIFIC MASKING

###### Abstract

Speech enhancement for spatial audio needs to improve speech quality and
intelligibility while preserving the spatial characteristics of the
acoustic scene. Time-frequency masking provides an effective approach
for speech enhancement, but its performance and spatial fidelity may
depend strongly on the signal representation in which the mask is
applied. This work systematically compares time-frequency masking in the
microphone, beamformer, and first-order Ambisonics domains in simulated
reverberant acoustic scenes. Both an ideal ratio mask (IRM) and a
lightweight deep-learning-based mask estimator are considered, with
performance evaluated in terms of speech reconstruction, perceptual
quality, intelligibility, reverberation preservation, and binaural
spatial-cue preservation. The results show that channel-specific masking
in the beamformer domain provides the highest speech quality and
intelligibility, while channel-specific masking in the Ambisonics domain
better preserves the spatial characteristics of the residual
interference. Reducing the spatial representation from third- to
first-order Ambisonics substantially reduces the performance gap between
the beamformer and Ambisonics domains due to the lower spatial
resolution of the resulting beamformers. Furthermore, a shared mask
across first-order Ambisonics channels achieves speech enhancement
performance close to that of channel-specific masking while requiring
only a single mask-estimation network. These results demonstrate that
the choice of signal representation and masking strategy should be
considered jointly, and indicate that first-order Ambisonics with shared
masking provides a practical compromise between speech enhancement
performance, spatial fidelity, and computational complexity.

## 1 Introduction

Speech enhancement aims to improve speech quality and intelligibility in
noisy environments by suppressing interfering sounds. Time-frequency
masking has become one of the most widely used approaches, particularly
when the masks are estimated using deep neural networks trained to
separate speech from background noise \[1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\]. These
methods have demonstrated substantial improvements over classical signal
processing techniques and are typically optimized and evaluated using
objectives related to speech quality or intelligibility, such as
scale-invariant signal-to-distortion ratio (SI-SDR), Perceptual
Evaluation of Speech Quality (PESQ), and Short-Time Objective
Intelligibility (STOI) \[7, 8, 9, 10, 6, 11, 12\]. Although multichannel
recordings are often used as input, the enhanced output is typically a
single-channel speech signal, and the learning objective focuses
primarily on suppressing interference while maximizing speech quality
and intelligibility.

While speech quality and intelligibility is important for speech
communication, the increasing use of immersive audio technologies,
including augmented and virtual reality (AR and VR), hearing assistance
and three-dimensional audio reproduction, has motivated the extension of
speech enhancement methods to multichannel spatial audio \[13, 14, 15,
16, 17\]. In these applications, enhancement algorithms aim to preserve
not only speech quality and intelligibility but also the spatial
information required for accurate localization, scene awareness,
listener comfort, and spatial release from masking (SRM) \[18, 19, 20,
21\]. However, masking may distort inter-channel spatial information,
thereby altering spatial perception. While previous studies have
demonstrated the feasibility of applying speech enhancement within the
context of spatial audio systems, a gap remains in the understanding of
the effective approach for achieving both a high level of speech
enhancement and high-quality spatial reproduction, in particular for
masking-based methods.

Recent work has begun to address this gap. In \[20\], masking in the
beamformer and Ambisonics domains was compared, showing that the latter
better preserves the spatial information of both the target speech and
the residual disturbance. More recently, \[22\] systematically compared
the time-frequency ideal ratio mask (IRM) applied to microphone,
beamformer, and Ambisonics representations, demonstrating distinct
trade-offs between speech enhancement performance and spatial fidelity.
AmbiSep \[13, 23\] compared the performance of Ambisonics and
beamformers channels with transformer-based neural-network architecture
to jointly perform speech separation and noise reduction, while
preserving spatial information. However, the analysis did not include
microphone channels nor spatial measures such as preservation of
reverberation, and interaural time difference (ITD) and interaural level
difference (ILD) computed using reproduced binaural signals. Despite
these advances, a gap remains: no comprehensive analysis yet provides
both insight and practical design guidance for achieving high speech
enhancement alongside spatial fidelity, including preservation of
perceived reverberation and localization of both target and interfering
sources, across oracle and practical neural-network-based masking
systems.

This work addresses this gap by providing a systematic comparison of
oracle and deep learning-based time–frequency masking across signal
representations, with the aim of deriving both insight and practical
design guidance for spatial-audio based speech enhancement systems.
Specifically, we compare time-frequency masks estimated by a lightweight
network and oracle IRM applied in three signal representations:
microphone signals, beamformer outputs, and first-order Ambisonics.
Focusing on first-order Ambisonics enables evaluation under realistic
microphone-array constraints encountered in practical immersive audio
systems. The methods are evaluated using simulated reverberant acoustic
scenes with respect to speech enhancement performance, perceptual
quality, intelligibility, reverberation preservation, and binaural cue
preservation.

The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows: (i)
showing that channel-specific masking applied to beamformer channels
best preserves speech quality and intelligibility compared to Ambisonics
and microphone channels; (ii) showing that channel-specific masking
applied to Ambisonics channels best preserves the spatial attributes of
the residual disturbance compared to beamformer and microphone channels;
and (iii) showing that a shared mask applied across Ambisonics channels
provides speech quality and intelligibility close to that of
channel-specific masking, with only a slight performance reduction,
therefore offering a more efficient implementation.

## 2 BACKGROUND

### 2.1 Signal Model

Consider a reverberant acoustic environment comprising a target speech
source, interfering sources, and stationary background noise. The
acoustic scene is observed using a rigid spherical microphone array with
$`Q`$ microphones. In the frequency domain, the microphone signals are
modeled as

```math
\mathbf{x}(f)=\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{dir}}(f)+\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{rev}}(f)+\mathbf{n}(f), \tag{1}
```

where $`\mathbf{x}(f)\in\mathbb{C}^{Q\times 1}`$ denotes the microphone
signal vector at frequency $`f`$. The microphones are located on a
sphere of radius $`r`$ at positions $`\Phi_{q}=(\theta_{q},\phi_{q})`$,
$`q=1,\ldots,Q`$. The terms $`\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{dir}}(f)`$ and
$`\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{rev}}(f)`$ correspond to the direct-path and
reverberant components of the target speech, respectively, while
$`\mathbf{n}(f)`$ represents the combined contribution of background
noise and interfering sources.

The same sound field can be represented in the spherical harmonics (SH),
or Ambisonics, domain \[20\] as

```math
\mathbf{a}_{nm}(f)=\mathbf{a}_{nm}^{\mathrm{dir}}(f)+\mathbf{a}_{nm}^{\mathrm{rev}}(f)+\mathbf{a}_{nm}^{\mathrm{noise}}(f), \tag{2}
```

where
$`\mathbf{a}_{nm}(f)=[a_{00}(f),a_{1(-1)}(f),a_{10}(f),\ldots,a_{NN}(f)]^{T}`$
$`\in\mathbb{C}^{(N+1)^{2}\times 1}`$ is the vector of the Ambisonics
coefficients of order $`N`$ \[24\]. The vectors
$`\mathbf{a}_{nm}^{\mathrm{dir}}(f)`$,
$`\mathbf{a}_{nm}^{\mathrm{rev}}(f)`$, and
$`\mathbf{a}_{nm}^{\mathrm{noise}}(f)`$ denote the direct, reverberant,
and noise components, respectively.

Finally, the acoustic scene can be represented in the beamformer domain
by applying a set of spatial beamformers:

```math
\mathbf{b}(f)=\mathbf{b}^{\mathrm{dir}}(f)+\mathbf{b}^{\mathrm{rev}}(f)+\mathbf{b}^{\mathrm{noise}}(f), \tag{3}
```

where $`\mathbf{b}(f)\in\mathbb{C}^{B\times 1}`$ contains the outputs of
$`B`$ beamformers steered toward directions represented in spherical
coordinates $`\Psi_{b}=(\theta_{b},\phi_{b})`$, $`b=1,\ldots,B`$. The
vectors $`\mathbf{b}^{\mathrm{dir}}(f)`$,
$`\mathbf{b}^{\mathrm{rev}}(f)`$, and $`\mathbf{b}^{\mathrm{noise}}(f)`$
represent the corresponding direct-path, reverberant, and noise
contributions.

### 2.2 Transformation Between Signal Representations

Throughout this work, the Ambisonics representation in Eq. (2) serves as
the reference signal representation. The signals in the
microphone-domain and the beamformer-domain are subsequently derived
from the Ambisonics coefficients following the formulation in \[24\]. In
order to transform from the SH domain to the beamformer or microphone
domain, we use:

```math
\displaystyle\mathbf{b}(f) \displaystyle=\mathbf{Y}_{\text{Beam}}(\mathbf{\Psi})\mathbf{a}_{nm}(f) \tag{4}
```

```math
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}(f) \displaystyle=\mathbf{Y}_{\text{Mic}}(\mathbf{\Phi})\mathbf{B}(fr)\mathbf{a}_{nm}(f) \tag{5}
```

where $`\mathbf{Y}_{\text{Beam}}(\Psi)`$ and
$`\mathbf{Y}_{\text{Mic}}(\Phi)`$ are $`B\times(N+1)^{2}`$ and
$`Q\times(N+1)^{2}`$ matrices of SH as in \[20, 24\].
$`\mathbf{\Psi}=[\Psi_{1},\Psi_{2},...,\Psi_{B}]`$ is the vector of the
$`B`$ look directions and
$`\mathbf{\Phi}=[\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2},...,\Phi_{Q}]`$ is the vector of the
$`Q`$ microphones locations on a sphere with radius $`r`$.
$`\mathbf{B}(fr)=\text{diag}(b_{0}(fr),b_{1}(fr),b_{1}(fr),b_{1}(fr),...,b_{N}(fr))`$
is a $`(N+1)^{2}\times(N+1)^{2}`$ diagonal matrix where $`b_{n}(fr)`$
represents the radial functions that depend on the frequency and
distance from the origin. To transform back to the SH domain we will
use:

```math
\displaystyle\mathbf{a}_{nm}(f) \displaystyle=\mathbf{Y}_{\text{Beam}}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{\Psi})\mathbf{b}(f) \tag{6}
```

```math
\displaystyle\mathbf{a}_{nm}(f) \displaystyle=(\mathbf{Y}_{\text{Mic}}(\mathbf{\Phi})\mathbf{B}(fr))^{\dagger}\mathbf{x}(f) \tag{7}
```

where $`(.)^{\dagger}`$ is pseudo-inverse, assuming that
$`Q,B\geq(N+1)^{2}`$. Because we use pseudo-inverse, Eq. (7) may
introduce an error which may be small if the $`B`$ and $`Q`$ directions
sample the sphere nearly uniformly \[24\].

### 2.3 Time-Frequency Masks

The frequency-domain signals are transformed to the time domain and then
to the time-frequency domain using the short-time Fourier transform
(STFT), yielding the representations $`\mathbf{x}(\tau,\nu)`$,
$`\mathbf{a}_{nm}(\tau,\nu)`$, and $`\mathbf{b}(\tau,\nu)`$. The desired
signal component is then estimated by applying a time-frequency mask to
each representation:

```math
\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{x}}(\tau,\nu) \displaystyle=\mathbf{M}_{\text{Mic}}(\tau,\nu)\mathbf{x}(\tau,\nu) \tag{8}
```

```math
\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{a}}_{nm}(\tau,\nu) \displaystyle=\mathbf{M}_{\text{Amb}}(\tau,\nu)\mathbf{a}_{nm}(\tau,\nu) \tag{9}
```

```math
\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{b}}(\tau,\nu) \displaystyle=\mathbf{M}_{\text{Beam}}(\tau,\nu)\mathbf{b}(\tau,\nu) \tag{10}
```

where $`\mathbf{M}_{\text{Mic}}(\tau,\nu)`$,
$`\mathbf{M}_{\text{Amb}}(\tau,\nu)`$ and
$`\mathbf{M}_{\text{Beam}}(\tau,\nu)`$ are diagonal matrices. Here the
desired signal is defined as the reverberant target. At the end of this
process, the signals are transformed back to the time domain. In this
work, we will focus on two types of masks:

_1) Ideal ratio mask (IRM):_ We will use the IRM as in \[22, 20\]:

```math
M(\text{ch},\tau,\nu)=\frac{SNR(\text{ch},\tau,\nu)}{SNR(\text{ch},\tau,\nu)+1} \tag{11}
```

```math
\mathbf{M}(\tau,\nu)=\operatorname{diag}(M(0,\tau,\nu),M(1,\tau,\nu),...) \tag{12}
```

where $`SNR(\text{ch},\tau,\nu)`$ is the ratio of the power of the
desired signal to that of the undesired signal for each channel.

_2) FT-JNF estimated mask:_ The FT-JNF (frequency–temporal joint
non-linear filter) as in \[25\] is a data-driven model that estimates
the mask directly from the noisy multichannel input.

### 2.4 Binaural Signal Representation

The enhanced signals are rendered as binaural signals by applying an
HRTF-based Ambisonics decoder in the frequency domain. The binaural
left/right ear signals are obtained as

```math
p^{l/r}(f)=\tilde{\mathbf{h}}^{l/r}_{nm}(f)^{T}\mathbf{a}_{nm}(f), \tag{13}
```

where $`\tilde{\mathbf{h}}^{l/r}_{nm}(f)`$ denotes the modified
SH-domain representation of the HRTF. It is derived from the original
SH-domain HRTF coefficients $`\mathbf{h}^{l/r}_{nm}(f)`$ according to
$`\tilde{h}^{l/r}_{nm}(f)=(-1)^{m}h^{l/r}_{n(-m)}(f)`$ \[26\]. When the
enhanced signal is obtained in the microphone or beamformer domains, it
is first converted back to the SH domain using Eqs. (6) and (7), after
which the binaural decoding is performed.

## 3 Proposed Analysis

The central objective of this work is to systematically analyze the
interaction between signal representation and channel-specific masking
application in spatial-audio speech enhancement. Rather than evaluating
speech enhancement independently of spatial reproduction, the proposed
analysis examines the trade-off between suppressing undesired signals
and preserving the spatial characteristics of the acoustic scene.

### 3.1 Research Questions

This objective is addressed through these research questions:

1.  1.  How does the signal representation affect the trade-off between
        speech enhancement and spatial fidelity?
2.  2.  To what extent does channel-specific masking improve enhancement
        compared with a computationally-efficient shared mask, and does this
        effect depend on the signal representation?

### 3.2 Analysis Dimensions: Signal Representation and Masking Strategy

To answer these questions, the analysis is built around two dimensions.
The first dimension is signal representation. Three representations are
considered: microphone channels, first-order Ambisonics channels, and
beamformer outputs. The key premise is that masking may affect these
representations differently, because their channels encode spatial
information in different ways. The analysis additionally compares first-
and third-order Ambisonics representations, to investigate the effect of
Ambisonics order on all three representations. The second dimension is
masking strategy. A shared mask uses a single mask applied to all
channels. Channel-specific masking instead estimates an independent mask
for each channel. Channel-specific masks may improve speech enhancement
over a shared mask by adapting to the characteristics of each channel,
but can also introduce channel-dependent modifications that distort
spatial cues.

### 3.3 Oracle vs. Estimated Masking

To separate the effect of masking strategy from the effect of mask
estimation itself, both the ideal ratio mask (IRM) and the lightweight
FT-JNF neural estimator are considered. The IRM provides an oracle
reference for achievable performance, while FT-JNF serves as a practical
mask estimator, used to determine whether the relationships observed
with oracle masking remain when the mask must be estimated from the
noisy multichannel signal.

### 3.4 Evaluation Framework

Finally, the analysis jointly considers speech quality and
intelligibility, reverberation preservation, and binaural cues of both
the target and residual interference. This allows the trade-off between
enhancement performance and spatial fidelity to be evaluated across
signal representations and masking strategies. The resulting framework
is evaluated under controlled acoustic conditions in the simulation
study that follows.

## 4 OBJECTIVE MEASURES OF PERFORMANCE

Performance is assessed using objective metrics that, as mentioned
above, evaluate speech intelligibility, speech quality, binaural cues
and reverberation preservation. These are detailed below.

- •
  SI-SDR: Signal reconstruction accuracy is quantified using
  SI-SDR \[8\]. The metric is calculated independently for the left and
  right binaural channels and averaged to obtain the overall performance
  measure. The reverberant target speech signal is used as the
  reference.
- •
  $`\mathbf{\Delta}`$ITD,  $`\mathbf{\Delta}`$ILD: The preservation of
  binaural spatial information is evaluated through the ITD and
  ILD \[18, 19\]. ITD is the dominant localization cue below
  approximately 1.5 kHz, whereas ILD dominates at higher
  frequencies \[18, 19\]. These measures are computed using the
  direct-path component of the target signal. The spatial distortion
  introduced by the enhancement process is measured as the absolute
  difference between the estimated and reference ITD and ILD values,
  denoted as $`\Delta\mathrm{ITD}`$ and $`\Delta\mathrm{ILD}`$.
- •
  $`\mathbf{\Delta}`$C₅₀: The influence of the enhancement process on
  reverberation is evaluated using the clarity index
  $`\mathrm{C}_{50}`$ \[27\]. This measure quantifies the ratio between
  the early arriving energy within the first 50 ms and the subsequent
  late reverberation energy. The deviation from the reference
  reverberant target signal is calculated as the absolute
  $`\mathrm{C}_{50}`$ error, averaged over the two ears, and denoted as
  $`\Delta\mathrm{C}_{50}`$.
- •
  DBSTOI: Speech intelligibility is measured using the deterministic
  binaural STOI (DBSTOI) \[28\], which accounts for binaural effects and
  non-linear auditory processing. The direct-path target speech serves
  as the reference for this evaluation.
- •
  PESQ: Speech quality is assessed using PESQ \[9\], where the
  reverberant target signal is used as the reference. PESQ scores are
  computed separately for each ear and averaged to obtain the final
  score.

## 5 Simulation Study

This section describes the simulation framework used to evaluate the
considered speech enhancement methods. The experiments are designed to
provide a controlled comparison of microphone, Ambisonics, and
beamformer representations under identical acoustic conditions. The
simulated scenes include reverberation, directional interference, and
background noise, allowing both speech enhancement performance and the
preservation of spatial information to be evaluated. The simulation
setup, signal representations, and training procedure are described
first, followed by the experimental results.

### 5.1 Setup

We generate a simulated dataset using shroom \[29\], which provides an
implementation of the source-image model \[30\]. Each scene contains a
target speaker, an interfering speaker, and a stationary
background-noise source, captured by a virtual receiver. The dataset
consists of a total of 600 examples for training, 200 examples for
validation, and 100 examples for testing, with each example of 6 seconds
duration.

For every scene, a rectangular room is randomly generated with varying
dimensions and wall absorption, while the receiver is placed at a random
position at least $`1`$ m from the walls. The target speaker is
positioned directly in front of the receiver at a random distance, and
the interfering speaker is placed at the same distance but at a random
direction. A stationary babble-noise source is placed in a randomly
selected room corner to emulate a highly reverberant noise field. The
interferer and noise source signal are scaled according to specified SNR
conditions, and the total undesired signal is the sum of the interfering
speaker and the noise. Table 1 details how source positions and other
scene parameters are selected.

Each scene is simulated at a high spherical-harmonics order with
$`N=10`$, from which all signal representations are derived so that the
three domains describe the same underlying sound field. For the main
experiments, the Ambisonics signals are then truncated to first-order.
The microphone signals are rendered from the high-order field using a
rigid spherical array of $`Q=4`$ microphones with radius $`r=0.042`$ m,
while the beamformer signals are obtained by a signal-independent
plane-wave decomposition of the first-order field onto $`B=4`$ look
directions \[24\]. The microphone locations and beamformer look
directions are distributed uniformly on the sphere, with the first
direction aligned to the target. For the third-order Ambisonics channels
comparison, the corresponding representations are instead constructed at
order $`N=3`$, using $`Q=24`$ microphones and $`B=24`$ beamformer look
directions, with the latter distributed using a near-uniform Fibonacci
grid. Channel types are denoted as follows:

- •
  TFM: Time-frequency microphones
- •
  TFA: Time-frequency Ambisonics
- •
  TFB: Time-frequency Beamforming

_Training and validation data:_ To expose the network to a wide variety
of spatial configurations, the interferer azimuth and height are
randomly sampled according to the distributions specified in Table 1.

_Testing data:_ For the evaluation set, the interferer is restricted to
two fixed azimuth directions relative to the target, providing a large
spatial separation between the target and interference. This makes the
preservation of the interference spatial cues a perceptually meaningful
task. For each test scene, additional reference signals are stored: the
direct-sound from the target ($`\mathbf{a}^{\text{dir}}_{nm}`$), the
direct-sound from the interference, and the target signal convolved with
the room ARIR truncated to $`50`$ ms after the direct arrival, used to
compute $`\text{C}_{50}`$.

### 5.2 Methodology

Two types of speech enhancement masks are computed as outlined in Sec. 3. The IRM mask is computed directly from the known desired and
undesired components, as in \[22, 20\]. For the data driven method, we
train a bidirectional LSTM (FT-JNF) as in \[25\] that estimates a
complex time-frequency mask from the noisy signal. The network input is
the one-sided STFT of the noisy multichannel mixture in a given domain,
with the real and imaginary parts of all channels concatenated to form a
$`[\,T\times F\times 2C\,]`$ tensor, where $`C=4`$ is the number of
channels in that domain. The estimated mask is applied to the STFT of a
chosen reference channel, and the masked STFT is transformed back to the
time domain. Unlike \[25\], here the network is trained to maximize the
signal-to-distortion ratio (SDR) between the reconstructed and the
desired reference signal, and not SI-SDR. We chose to use SDR to
preserve the scale between the channels, which is important for the
spatial processing that follow. The reverberant target is used as the
desired reference so that the network preserves target signal
reverberation \[22\]. The FT-JNF network is used here as a practical
mask estimator to investigate the behavior of the different signal
representations and masking strategies, rather than as an object of
architectural comparison.

Two mask application strategies are considered using the FT-JNF mask
estimator. In the first strategy, a single FT-JNF network is trained
using channel 0 as the reference channel. The resulting mask is then
applied to all channels of the multichannel signal, yielding a shared
mask across channels. This approach uses a single network and therefore
imposes the same time-frequency attenuation pattern on all channels.

In the second strategy, a separate FT-JNF network is trained for each
reference channel. Each network receives the full multichannel input but
is optimized to reconstruct its corresponding reference channel. The
resulting channel-specific masks are applied independently to their
respective channels, yielding a diagonal masking matrix as in Eqs. (8),
(9), and (10). This strategy allows the estimated mask to adapt to the
characteristics of each channel. Both masking strategies are evaluated
for all three signal representations.

In the original FT-JNF architecture, the first BLSTM layer uses 256
hidden units and the second 128 hidden units, resulting in approximately
1.2M trainable parameters. In this work, we reduce the network size by
approximately 10 times, using 80 and 40 hidden units in the first and
second BLSTM layers, respectively, resulting in approximately 122K
parameters, to simulate low-compute and more realistic edge-device
applications.

For the beamformer and microphone domains the enhanced signals are
transformed back to the SH domain using Eqs. (6) and (7) before binaural
decoding. Binaural signals are finally computed via Eq. (13) using the
Cologne HRTF compilation of the Neumann KU-100 \[31\].

#### 5.3.1 First-Order Channel-Specific Masking: Oracle and Estimated Masks

We first consider the basic configuration, based on first-order
Ambisonics representations with channel-specific masking. Two masks are
compared: the oracle IRM, which is computed independently for each
channel from the known desired and undesired components and the FT-JNF
mask, estimated separately for each channel from the noisy multichannel
input. This comparison establishes the relationship between oracle and
estimated masking before the effects of masking strategy and spatial
resolution are examined in the following sections.

As shown in Table 2 (upper two parts), the beamformer domain provides
the best speech quality and intelligibility performance for both masks,
achieving the highest SI-SDR, DBSTOI, and PESQ scores. The Ambisonics
domain provides slightly lower performance, while the microphone domain
provides the lowest speech enhancement performance. Thus, for this basic
configuration, channel-specific masking in the beamformer domain
provides the strongest speech enhancement, irrespective of whether the
mask is estimated or oracle. This can be explained by the directional
selectivity of the beamforming pre-processing, providing an initial
enhancement of the target signal. For the estimated mask, speech
enhancement performance was lower than for the IRM in all three domains,
with lower SI-SDR, PESQ and DBSTOI. While previous work shows the
capability of estimated masks to surpass that of IRM \[10\], here the
network is a scaled-down version of FT-JNF with approximately ten times
fewer parameters than the original, which may explain the superiority of
the IRM. It should be nevertheless noted that the comparison presented
here is intended to examine the relative behavior of the different
representations under practical mask estimation rather than aiming for
best achievable performance.

For the spatial cues, both masks show similar behavior. For the target
speaker, $`\Delta\mathrm{ITD}`$ and $`\Delta\mathrm{ILD}`$ remain
consistently below the JND thresholds of approximately $`100~\mu`$s for
ITD and $`1`$ dB for ILD \[33, 18\]. For the interferer, the
$`\Delta\mathrm{ITD}`$ values for both the microphone and Ambisonics
representations remain below the JND threshold for both masks, while the
beamformer representation is slightly above the threshold. Among the
three representations, Ambisonics exhibits the lowest
$`\Delta\mathrm{ILD}`$ error for the interferer, remaining below the JND
for the IRM and only slightly above it for the estimated mask, whereas
the microphone representation exhibits the highest error.

The preservation of reverberation, measured by $`\Delta C_{50}`$, is
relatively similar across the three representations and follows the same
ordering for both masks. Ambisonics provides the best preservation,
followed by the beamformer and microphone domains. Mask estimation
increases $`\Delta C_{50}`$ by approximately $`0.15`$–$`0.30`$ dB in all
three domains compared to IRM. For all representations and both masks,
$`\Delta C_{50}`$ is slightly above the JND, which is approximately 1
dB \[27\], indicating a perceptible but overall limited modification of
the reverberant characteristics of the target signal.

Overall, the basic first-order configuration reveals a trade-off between
speech enhancement and spatial preservation. Channel-specific masking in
the beamformer domain provides the strongest speech enhancement, whereas
Ambisonics provides better preservation of the spatial characteristics
of the residual interference and reverberation. Importantly, this
trade-off, and the relative ordering of the three representations, is
preserved when moving from the oracle IRM to the estimated FT-JNF mask,
with estimation reducing the absolute performance in all domains without
altering the relations between them. This indicates that the main
relative differences between the signal representations observed with
oracle masking are largely preserved under practical mask estimation.
This motivates the use of the IRM in Sec. 5.3.3, where third-order
Ambisonics is considered. Using the oracle IRM avoids the need to train
separate mask-estimation networks for the larger number of channels
introduced by the higher-order representation, while providing a
consistent basis for comparing the effect of spatial resolution.

#### 5.3.2 Effect of Shared Masking

We next investigate how using a single mask across all channels affects
the enhancement and spatial preservation compared with channel-specific
masking. The results in Table 2 (lower part, denoted “Shared”) show that
the shared mask has only a small effect on speech enhancement in the
microphone and Ambisonics domains. In contrast, the beamformer domain
shows a more pronounced reduction in speech quality and intelligibility.
This indicates that the beamformer channels are more sensitive to the
use of a shared mask, as their high directional selectivity results in
larger differences between channels.

Shared masking consistently reduces the spatial-cue errors of the
residual interference across all three representations. The reduction is
particularly pronounced for Ambisonics and beamforming, where both
$`\Delta`$ITD and $`\Delta`$ILD decrease substantially relative to
channel-specific masking, indicating improved preservation of the
spatial characteristics of the residual interference. A likely
explanation is that applying the same time-frequency attenuation to all
channels introduces fewer channel-dependent spectral modifications,
thereby reducing distortions of the relative inter-channel
relationships. Consequently, the relative spatial relationships between
channels are better preserved.

A similar but smaller improvement is observed for reverberation
preservation. The $`\Delta C_{50}`$ error is reduced by shared masking
for the Ambisonics and beamformer representations, while remaining
essentially unchanged for the microphone representation.

Overall, shared masking provides better preservation of spatial
information at the cost of reduced speech enhancement, with this
trade-off being most pronounced in the beamformer domain. In contrast,
the Ambisonics representation maintains speech enhancement performance
close to that of channel-specific masking while benefiting from improved
spatial-cue preservation.

#### 5.3.3 Effect of Ambisonics Order

We next examine the effect of increasing the spatial representation from
first to third-order using the oracle IRM, with the results presented in
Table 3. Increasing the order substantially increases the difference
between the Ambisonics and beamformer domains in terms of speech quality
and intelligibility. This is attributed to the increased spatial
resolution, which produces more directive beamformers, and denser
spatial sampling which enables stronger spatial separation of the target
and interfering sources before masking. In contrast, the first-order
representation provides broader beamformer responses and therefore
limits this spatial selectivity.

The effect of Ambisonics order is also reflected in the preservation of
the spatial characteristics of the residual interference. With
third-order representations, the difference between the Ambisonics and
beamformer domains is more pronounced, with Ambisonics providing better
preservation of the interference binaural cues. When the representation
is reduced to first-order, the spatial-cue errors of the beamformer
become closer to those of Ambisonics. The microphone representation also
shows improved preservation of the interference cues at first-order,
although its ILD error remains larger than those of the Ambisonics and
beamformer representations.

Overall, increasing the Ambisonics order improves the spatial resolution
of the beamformer representation and consequently increases its
advantage in speech enhancement and its disadvantage in preserving the
spatial cues of the interferer. At first-order, the reduced beamformer
directivity narrows the performance gap between beamforming and
Ambisonics.

#### 5.3.4 Per-Channel Performance Analysis

Table 4 provides a per-channel analysis of the SI-SDR obtained with the
shared and channel-specific FT-JNF mask architectures. Note that
first-order Ambisonics is used here so only the FT-JNF masks are
presented and not IRM as the former represents more practical values.
The results reveal substantial differences between the three signal
representations, highlighting the importance of this type of analysis.

For the beamformer representation, the choice of mask type has a strong
effect on performance. When the shared mask trained for channel 0 is
applied to the other channels, the SI-SDR decreases substantially. In
contrast, with the channel-specific mask for which a separate network is
trained for each channel, the discrepancy is not as large. This behavior
can be explained by the directional nature of the beamformer channels.
Since each beamformer is steered toward a different direction, the
target-to-interference ratio can vary substantially between channels.

A different behavior is observed for the microphone representation. The
SI-SDR values obtained with the shared and channel-specific masks are
generally similar across the four channels, although small differences
can be observed. The relatively small differences indicate that the
microphone channels have similar, but not identical, signal
characteristics, also reflected in the similar SI-SDR values of the
noisy channels. This can be attributed to the relatively small distances
between microphones in this rather compact spherical microphone array,
with the radius of $`0.042`$ m.

For the Ambisonics representation, a different behavior is observed.
Although the four channels have markedly different directivity patterns,
the SI-SDR obtained with the shared mask is only slightly lower than
that obtained with the channel-specific masks. This is also reflected in
the SI-SDR values of the noisy channels, which are similar across the
four Ambisonic channels. A possible explanation is that the zeroth-order
Ambisonics channel represents the omnidirectional component of the sound
field, and therefore its estimated mask is less tied to a particular
spatial direction, while all other channels although having a
directional response, it is only phase-dependent and not gain-dependent
along the azimuth due to the complex exponential term of the spherical
harmonics \[24\].

## 6 CONCLUSIONS

This work investigated time-frequency masking for speech enhancement in
spatial audio, comparing microphone, Ambisonics, and beamformer signal
representations. Both oracle ideal ratio masks and masks estimated using
a lightweight FT-JNF network were considered, and performance was
evaluated in terms of speech enhancement, intelligibility, perceptual
quality, reverberation preservation, and binaural spatial-cue
preservation.

The results show that reducing the Ambisonics representation from third
to first-order substantially reduces the performance gap between the
Ambisonics and beamformer domains. The reduced spatial resolution limits
the directivity of the beamformers, thereby reducing their ability to
spatially separate the target and interference. Nevertheless, with
channel-specific neural masks, the beamformer domain still provides the
highest speech quality and intelligibility, while the Ambisonics domain
provides better preservation of the spatial characteristics of the
residual interference and reverberation levels.

The comparison of mask-generation strategies further demonstrates an
important trade-off between enhancement performance and spatial
fidelity. Channel-specific masks generally improve speech enhancement,
particularly in the beamformer domain. In contrast, a shared mask can
better preserve inter-channel relationships and, in some cases, the
reverberant characteristics of the target signal. Notably, in the
first-order Ambisonics domain, the shared-mask strategy achieves speech
enhancement performance close to that of channel-specific masking while
using only a single network. This makes shared masking in the Ambisonics
domain an attractive compromise between computational complexity, speech
enhancement, and spatial preservation.

The per-channel analysis further shows that the suitability of a shared
mask depends strongly on the signal representation. Beamformer channels
exhibit substantially different directional characteristics and
therefore benefit from channel-specific masks. Microphone channels are
more similar, resulting in only modest differences between the two
masking strategies. Ambisonics channels can have substantially different
spatial characteristics while still allowing a mask optimized for the
omnidirectional zeroth-order channel to generalize effectively across
channels. These results emphasize that the choice of signal
representation and masking strategy should be considered jointly when
designing spatial speech enhancement systems.

Overall, the findings demonstrate that there is no single representation
that simultaneously optimizes all performance criteria. Instead, the
appropriate masking strategy depends on the desired balance between
speech enhancement, spatial fidelity, and computational complexity. In
particular, first-order Ambisonics with a shared mask provides a
practical and efficient solution that maintains strong speech
enhancement performance while preserving spatial information, whereas
channel-specific beamformer masking is preferable when maximizing speech
quality and intelligibility is the primary objective.
