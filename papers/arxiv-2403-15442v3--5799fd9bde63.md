###### Abstract

Automatic speech recognition (ASR) plays a pivotal role in our daily
lives, offering utility not only for interacting with machines but also
for facilitating communication for individuals with partial or profound
hearing impairments. The process involves receiving the speech signal in
analog form, followed by various signal processing algorithms to make it
compatible with devices of limited capacities, such as cochlear implants
(CIs). Unfortunately, these implants, equipped with a finite number of
electrodes, often result in speech distortion during synthesis. Despite
efforts by researchers to enhance received speech quality using various
[SOTA](#id48) ([SOTA](#id48)) signal processing techniques, challenges
persist, especially in scenarios involving multiple sources of speech,
environmental noise, and other adverse conditions. The advent of new
artificial intelligence (AI) methods has ushered in cutting-edge
strategies to address the limitations and difficulties associated with
traditional signal processing techniques dedicated to CIs. This review
aims to comprehensively cover advancements in CI-based ASR and speech
enhancement, among other related aspects. The primary objective is to
provide a thorough overview of metrics and datasets, exploring the
capabilities of AI algorithms in this biomedical field, and summarizing
and commenting on the best results obtained. Additionally, the review
will delve into potential applications and suggest future directions to
bridge existing research gaps in this domain.

## I Introduction

In the symphony of modern technology, [ASR](#id53) ([ASR](#id53))
emerges as a master, orchestrating a seamless interaction between humans
and machines. This transformative technology has quietly become an
integral part of our daily lives, influencing how we communicate, access
information, and even navigate the intricacies of healthcare. The
significance of [ASR](#id53) extends beyond its role in facilitating
human-computer interaction; it permeates diverse applications such as
voice assistants and virtual agents, speech-to-text conversion, identity
verification, and holds particular promise in the realm of biomedical
research \[[1](#bib.bib1), [2](#bib.bib2)\]. [ASR](#id53) bridges the
gap between spoken language and digital communication, enabling the
conversion of spoken words into written text with remarkable accuracy.
The pervasiveness of [ASR](#id53) technology is evident in the devices
we use daily—smartphones, smart speakers, and voice-activated virtual
assistants—all seamlessly responding to our spoken commands and queries.
The convenience it brings to our lives is undeniable, offering a
hands-free and efficient mode of interaction that has become second
nature.

[ASR](#id53) is pivotal in authentication systems, safeguarding the
security and privacy of sensitive information. The integrity of audio
speech can be verified through techniques-based [ASR](#id53) such as
adversarial attack detection \[[3](#bib.bib3)\], steganalysis
\[[4](#bib.bib4), [5](#bib.bib5), [6](#bib.bib6)\], speech biometrics
\[[7](#bib.bib7)\], and more. Beyond the realm of communication,
[ASR](#id53) finds itself at the heart of various applications, each
playing a unique role in different domains. Speaker recognition, a facet
of [ASR](#id53), is not merely confined to enhancing security measures.
It has evolved into a versatile tool employed in healthcare, where the
identification of individuals through their unique vocal signatures
holds promise for personalized patient care. This is particularly
relevant in scenarios where quick and secure authentication is crucial,
such as accessing medical records or authorizing medical procedures.
Event recognition, another dimension of [ASR](#id53), is a game-changer
in sectors ranging from security to healthcare. In the former,
[ASR](#id53) algorithms analyze audio data to automatically detect and
categorize specific events, reinforcing surveillance capabilities. In
healthcare, event recognition becomes a powerful tool for monitoring and
early detection of health-related events, recognizing speech from noisy
environment \[[8](#bib.bib8)\], understanding person with dysarthria
severity \[[9](#bib.bib9)\], and more. In the context of cardiac health,
[ASR](#id53) can aid in identifying anomalies in heart sounds,
potentially enabling early intervention and preventive measures
\[[10](#bib.bib10)\]. Source separation, the ability to discern and
isolate individual sound sources from complex audio signals, is a boon
in fields like entertainment and music production. However, its
significance extends into the realm of biomedical research, where
[ASR](#id53) plays a pivotal role in decoding the intricate language of
physiological signals. In the context of [CI](#id4), source separation
becomes a critical component in enhancing the auditory experience for
individuals with hearing impairments.

The [CI](#id4), designed to restore hearing in individuals with severe
hearing loss or deafness, rely on [ASR](#id53) for optimizing their
functionality. [ASR](#id53) contributes significantly to the improvement
of speech perception in [CI](#id4) users by enhancing the processing and
interpretation of auditory signals. [CI](#id4) work by converting sound
waves into electrical signals that stimulate the auditory nerve,
bypassing damaged parts of the inner ear. [ASR](#id53) complements this
process by aiding in the recognition and translation of spoken language.
The technology plays a crucial role in optimizing speech understanding
for [CI](#id4) users by refining the interpretation of varied speech
patterns, tones, and nuances. Moreover, [ASR](#id53) in the context of
[CI](#id4) extends beyond basic speech recognition. It contributes to
the recognition of environmental sounds, facilitating a more immersive
auditory experience for individuals with hearing impairments. This is
particularly significant in enhancing the quality of life for [CI](#id4)
recipients, allowing them to navigate and engage with their surroundings
more effectively.

## II Background in speech processing for [CI](#id4)

### II-A Speech processing for [CI](#id4)

[CI](#id4) are electronic devices that can be implanted in one or both
ears to restore some level of hearing for individuals with partial or
severe deafness. [CI](#id4) comprise an external part with a microphone
and speech processor and an internal part with a receiver-stimulator and
electrode array, as shown in Figure
[3](#S2.F3 "Fig. 3 ‣ II-A Speech processing for ‣ II Background in speech processing for ‣ Artificial Intelligence for Cochlear Implants: Review of Strategies, Challenges, and Perspectives").
They convert sounds into electrical signals, stimulating the auditory
nerve to enable sound perception in individuals with profound hearing
loss \[[16](#bib.bib16)\].

![Refer to caption](2403.15442v3/img_coch.png)

Fig. 3: Illustration of a [CI](#id4) depicting the components situated
externally and internally within the device \[[16](#bib.bib16)\]

The incoming sound is divided into multiple frequency channels using
bandpass filters and then processed by envelope detectors. Non-linear
compressors adjust the dynamic range of the envelope for each patient.
The compressed envelope amplitudes are then utilized to modulate a
fixed-rate biphasic carrier signal. A current source converts voltage
into pulse trains of current, which are delivered to electrodes placed
along the cochlea in a non-overlapping manner. This stimulation method
is called [CIS](#id126) ([CIS](#id126)). Another coding strategy, known
as [ACE](#id32) ([ACE](#id32)), uses a greater number of channels and
dynamically selects the ”n-of-m” bands with the largest envelope
amplitudes (prior to compression). Only the corresponding ”n” electrodes
are stimulated. A popular device widely used for [CI](#id4), such as the
cochlear nucleus, typically has 22 channels.

The sound processor, which usually contains a microphone, battery, and
other components, can be worn either behind-the-ear (BTE) or off-the-ear
(OTE). A headpiece holds a transmitter coil, positioned externally above
the ear, while internally, a receiver coil, stimulator, and electrode
array are implanted. The SP includes a digital signal processor (DSP)
with memory units (maps) that store patient-specific information. An
audiologist configures these maps during the fitting process, adjusting
thresholds for each electrode, including T-Levels (the softest current
levels audible to the [CI](#id4) user) and C/M-Levels (current levels
perceived as comfortably loud), as well as the stimulation rate or
programming strategy. Data (pulse amplitude, pulse duration, pulse gap,
etc.) and power are sent through the skull via a radio frequency signal
from the transmitter coil to the receiver coil. The stimulator decodes
the received bitstream and converts it into electric currents to be
delivered to the cochlear electrodes. High-frequency signals stimulate
electrodes near the base of the cochlea, while low-frequency signals
stimulate electrodes near the apex.

The [CI](#id4) stimulates the auditory nerve afferents, which connect to
the central auditory pathways. However, compared to individuals with
normal hearing (NH), [CI](#id4) users face more difficulties in speech
perception, particularly in noisy environments. Hearing loss can be
caused by various factors, including natural aging, genetic
predisposition, exposure to loud sounds, and medical treatments. Damage
to the hair cells in the inner ear often leads to a reduced dynamic
range of hearing, as well as decreased frequency selectivity and
discriminative ability in speech processing. To evaluate the
effectiveness of [CI](#id4) in speech perception amid noise, listening
tests involving both normal hearing individuals and [CI](#id4) users are
commonly conducted. These tests typically employ a combination of speech
utterances from a recognized speech corpus and background noises like
speech-weighted noise and babble. Alternatively, vocoder simulations can
be utilized alongside speech intelligibility metrics. While
sentence-based tests are frequently employed, other stimuli such as
vowels, consonants, and phonemes are also used. As a result, noise
reduction techniques are increasingly employed to enhance the
performance of [CI](#id4) in challenging environments
\[[15](#bib.bib15)\].

### III-A [CI](#id4)-based AI Implementation

[CI](#id4) programming involves adjusting device settings to optimize
sound perception for individual users. This includes setting stimulation
levels, electrode configurations, and signal processing parameters to
enhance speech understanding and auditory experiences based on patient
feedback and objective measures. In 2010, Govaerts et al.
\[[41](#bib.bib41)\] described the development of an intelligent agent,
called [FOX](#id113) ([FOX](#id113)), for optimizing [CI](#id4)
programming, as illustrated in Figure
[5](#S3.F5 "Fig. 5 ‣ III-A -based AI Implementation ‣ III Taxonomy of -based AI techniques ‣ Artificial Intelligence for Cochlear Implants: Review of Strategies, Challenges, and Perspectives").
The agent analyzes map settings and psychoacoustic test results to
recommend and execute modifications to improve outcomes. The tool
focuses on an outcome-driven approach, reducing fitting time and
improving the quality of fitting. It introduces principles of [AI](#id2)
into the [CI](#id4) fitting process. The study proposed objective
measures and group electrode settings as strategies to reduce fitting
time.

Fig. 5: The fundamental operating concept of the [FOX](#id113) involves
inputting an initial program and multiple psychoacoustic test outcomes.
[FOX](#id113) processes this information and generates fitting
suggestions as its output. When integrated with proprietary outcome and
[CI](#id4) fitting software, the shaded boxes represent its
functionality, while the unfilled boxes represent its standalone
capability \[[41](#bib.bib41)\]. Audiqueen is a dataset with A and E
(A&E) phoneme discrimination.

Similarly, in \[[42](#bib.bib42), [43](#bib.bib43), [44](#bib.bib44),
[45](#bib.bib45), [46](#bib.bib46)\], all have employed [FOX](#id113)
for programming [CI](#id4). Vaerenberg et al. \[[42](#bib.bib42)\]
discusses the use of [FOX](#id113) for programming [CI](#id4) sound
processors in new users. [FOX](#id113) modifies maps based on specific
outcome measures using heuristic logic and deterministic rules. The
study showed positive results and optimized performance after three
months of programming, with good speech audiometry and loudness scaling
outcomes. The paper highlights the importance of individualized
programming parameters and the need for outcome-based adjustments rather
than relying solely on comfort. In \[[43](#bib.bib43)\],
computer-assisted [CI](#id4) fitting using [FOX](#id113) assessed its
impact on speech understanding. Results from 25 recipients showed that
84% benefited from suggested map changes, significantly improving speech
understanding thanks to the learning capacity of [FOX](#id113). This
approach offers standardized, systematic [CI](#id4) fitting, enhancing
auditory performance.

The COCH gene, referred to as the Cochlin gene, is responsible for
encoding the cochlin protein situated on chromosome 14 in humans,
primarily expressed in the inner ear. Cochlin predominantly functions
within the cochlea, a spiral-shaped structure involved in the process of
hearing, contributing to its structural integrity and proper operation.
Wathour et al. in \[[44](#bib.bib44)\] discuss the use of [AI](#id2) in
[CI](#id4) fitting through two case studies. The first case involves a
75-year-old lady who received a left ear implant due to gradual and
severe hearing loss in both ears without a clear cause. In the second
case, a 72-year-old man with a COCH gene mutation causing profound
hearing loss in both ears underwent a right ear implant to assess
whether [CI](#id4) programming using the [AI](#id2) software
[FOX](#id113) application could improve [CI](#id4) performance. The
results showed that [AI](#id2)-assisted fitting led to improvements in
auditory outcomes for adult [CI](#id4) recipients who had previously
undergone manual fitting. The [AI](#id2) suggestions helped improve word
recognition scores and loudness scaling curves. Similarly, Waltzman et
al. \[[45](#bib.bib45)\] incorporate [AI](#id2) in programming
[CI](#id4), aiming to assess the performance and standardization of
[AI](#id2)-based programming on fifty-five adult [CI](#id4) recipients.
The results showed that the [AI](#id2)-based [FOX](#id113) system
performed better for some patients, while others had similar results;
however, the majority preferred the [FOX](#id113) system.

### III-B [ML](#id5)-based methods

[ML](#id5) is a subfield of [AI](#id2) that focuses on developing
algorithms and statistical models that enable computer systems to
improve their performance in a specific task by learning features from
input data. The research in \[[47](#bib.bib47)\] focuses on
algorithm-based hearing and speech therapy rehabilitation after cochlear
implantation, particularly for older individuals. They propose the
development of an [ML](#id5)-based application that offers personalized
hearing therapy tailored to the patient’s needs, such as select
exercises, adjust difficulty levels, and analyze patient difficulties.
It operates independently, reducing the reliance on local speech
therapists, cost-effective, and accessible alternative to traditional
therapy, improving outcomes and quality of life for [CI](#id4)
recipients. In addition, Torresen et al. \[[48](#bib.bib48)\] discusses
the use of [ML](#id5) techniques to streamline the adjustment process
for [CI](#id4). The goal is to predict optimal adjustment values for new
patients based on data from previous patients. By analyzing data from
158 former patients, the study shows that while fully automatic
adjustments are not possible, [ML](#id5) can provide a good starting
point for manual adjustment. The research also identifies the most
important electrodes to measure for predicting levels of other
electrodes. This approach has the potential to reduce programming time,
benefit patients, and improve speech recognition scores, particularly
for young children and patients with post-lingual deafness. Henry et al.
in their \[[49](#bib.bib49)\] investigates the importance of acoustic
features in optimizing intelligibility for [CI](#id4) in noisy
environments. The study employs [ML](#id5) algorithms and extracts
acoustic features from speech and noise mixtures to train a [DNN](#id54)
([DNN](#id54)). The results, using various metrics, reveal that
frequency domain features, particularly Gammatone features, perform best
for normal hearing, while Mel spectrogram features exhibit the best
overall performance for hearing impairment. The study suggests a
stronger correlation between [STOI](#id124) and [NCM](#id37) in
predicting intelligibility for hearing-impaired listeners. The findings
can aid in designing adaptive intelligibility enhancement systems for
[CI](#id4) based on noise characteristics.

Moreover, the research in \[[50](#bib.bib50)\] focuses on imputing
missing values in [CI](#id4) candidate audiometric data. This study
assessed the performance of various imputation algorithms using a
dataset of 7,451 audiograms from [CI](#id4) patients. The results showed
that the quantity of missing data affected the imputation performance,
with greater amounts leading to poorer results. The distribution of
sparsity in the audiometric data was found to be non-uniform, with
inter-octave frequencies being less commonly tested. The [MICE](#id100)
([MICE](#id100)) method, safely imputed up to six missing data points in
an 11-frequency audiogram, consistently outperformed other models. This
study highlights the importance of imputation techniques in maximizing
datasets in hearing healthcare research. Xu et al. in
\[[51](#bib.bib51)\] explores the objective discrimination of bimodal
speech using [FFR](#id103). The study investigates the neural encoding
of [\$f\_{0}\$](#id104) ([\$f\_{0}\$](#id104)), called also pitch
\[[52](#bib.bib52)\], and [TFSC](#id105) ([TFSC](#id105)) in simulated
bimodal speech conditions. The results show that increasing acoustic
bandwidth enhances the neural representation of [\$f\_{0}\$](#id104) and
[TFSC](#id105) components in the non-implanted ear. Moreover, [ML](#id5)
algorithms successfully classify and discriminate [FFR](#id103) based on
spectral differences between vowels. The findings suggest that the
enhancement of [\$f\_{0}\$](#id104) and [TFSC](#id105) neural encoding
with increasing bandwidth is predictive of perceptual bimodal benefit in
speech-in-noise tasks. [FFR](#id103) may serve as a useful tool for
objectively assessing individual variability in bimodal hearing. The
research conducted by Crowson et al. \[[53](#bib.bib53)\] aimed to
predict postoperative [CI](#id4) performance using supervised
[ML](#id5). The authors used neural networks and [DT](#id128)
([DT](#id128))-based ensemble algorithms on a dataset of 1,604 adults
who received [CI](#id4). They included 282 text and numerical variables
related to demographics, audiometric data, and patient-reported
outcomes. The results showed that the neural network model achieved a
1-year postoperative performance prediction [RMSE](#id106)
([RMSE](#id106)) of 0.57 and classification accuracy of 95.4%. When both
text and numerical variables were used, the [RMSE](#id106) was 25.0% and
classification accuracy was 73.3%. The study identified influential
variables such as preoperative sentence-test performance, age at
surgery, and specific questionnaire responses. The findings suggest that
supervised [ML](#id5) can predict [CI](#id4) performance and provide
insights into factors affecting outcomes. In the same context of
prediction, Mikulskis et al. \[[54](#bib.bib54)\] focuse on predicting
the attachment of broad-spectrum pathogens to coating materials for
biomedical devices as illustrated in Figure
[6](#S3.F6 "Fig. 6 ‣ III-B -based methods ‣ III Taxonomy of -based AI techniques ‣ Artificial Intelligence for Cochlear Implants: Review of Strategies, Challenges, and Perspectives").
The authors employ [ML](#id5) methods to generate quantitative
predictions for pathogen attachment to a large library of polymers. This
approach aims to accelerate the discovery of materials that resist
bacterial biofilm formation, reducing the rate of infections associated
with medical devices. The study highlights the need for new materials
that prevent bacterial colonization and biofilm development,
particularly in the context of antibiotic resistance. The results
demonstrate the potential of [ML](#id5) in designing polymers with low
pathogen attachment, offering promising candidate materials for
implantable and indwelling medical devices. Similarly, Alohali et al.
\[[55](#bib.bib55)\] focuses on using [ML](#id5) algorithms to predict
the post-operative electrode impedances in [CI](#id4) patients. The
study used a dataset of 80 pediatric patients and considered factors
such as patient age and intraoperative electrode impedance. The results
showed that the best algorithm varied by channel, with Bayesian linear
regression and neural networks providing the best results for 75% of the
channels. The accuracy level ranged between 83% and 100% in half of the
channels one year after surgery. Additionally, the patient’s age alone
showed good prediction results for 50% of the channels at six months or
one year after surgery, suggesting it could be a predictor of electrode
impedance.

### III-C [CNN](#id1)-based methods

\Acp

CNN are a class of [DL](#id3) algorithms widely used in computer vision
tasks. Their architecture includes convolutional layers that
automatically learn hierarchical features from input data. The core
convolution for 2D data operation is defined by the equation:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       S(i,j)=(I*K)(i,j)=\sum_{m}\sum_{n}I(m,n)\cdot K(i-m,j-n)
       ```                                                       |     | (1) |

Here, $`I`$ represents the input 2D data, $`K`$ is the convolutional
kernel, and $`S`$ is the output feature map. \AcpCNN excel at
recognizing spatial patterns, making them essential in image
recognition, object detection, and other visual tasks. Additionally,
there exist 1D [CNN](#id1), which are effective for sequential data
analysis, such as in natural language processing or time series
applications. [CNN](#id1) is widely used for the interdisciplinary
nature of [CI](#id4), which involves aspects of neurobiology, signal
processing, and medical technology. For example, the proposed work
\[[57](#bib.bib57)\] introduces a novel pathological voice
identification system using signal processing and [DL](#id3). It employs
[CI](#id4) models with bandpass and optimized gammatone filters to mimic
human cochlear vibration patterns. The system processes speech samples
and utilizes a [CNN](#id1) for final pathological voice identification.
Results show discrimination of pathological voices with [F1](#id29)
scores of 77.6% (bandpass) and 78.7% (gammatone). The paper addresses
voice pathology causes, compares filter models, and proposes a
non-invasive, objective assessment system. It contributes to the field
with a comprehensive performance analysis, achieving high accuracy and
demonstrating effectiveness compared to related works. Addtionally, in
the scheme proposed by Wang \[[17](#bib.bib17)\], the [FCN](#id6)
([FCN](#id6)) model is evaluated for enhancing speech intelligibility in
mismatched training and testing conditions. Using 2,560 Mandarin
utterances and 100 noise types, the study compares [FCN](#id6) with
traditional [MMSE](#id8) and [DDAE](#id9) ([DDAE](#id9)) models. Two
sets of experiments are conducted for normal and vocoded speech. The
[FCN](#id6) model demonstrates superior performance, maintaining clearer
speech structures, especially in mid-low frequency regions crucial for
intelligibility. Objective evaluations using [STOI](#id124) scores and a
listening test confirm [FCN](#id6)’s effectiveness under challenging
[SNR](#id10) conditions, outperforming [MMSE](#id8) and [DDAE](#id9).
The study suggests [FCN](#id6) as a promising choice for [EAS](#id11)
([EAS](#id11)) speech processors.

Moving on, the research paper in \[[58](#bib.bib58)\] presents a novel
approach to optimize stimulus energy for [CI](#id4). A [CNN](#id1) was
developed as a surrogate model for a biophysical auditory nerve fiber
model, significantly reducing simulation time while maintaining high
accuracy. The [CNN](#id1) was then used in conjunction with an
evolutionary algorithm \[[59](#bib.bib59)\] to optimize the shape of the
stimulus waveform, resulting in energy-efficient waveforms and potential
improvements in [CI](#id4) technology. Traditional computational models
of the cochlea, which represent it as a transmission line, are
computationally expensive due to their cascaded architecture and the
inclusion of non-linearities. As a result, they are not suitable for
real-time applications such as hearing aids, robotics, and [ASR](#id53).
For the aforementioned conditions, the study in \[[60](#bib.bib60)\]
presents a hybrid approach, called CoNNear¹⁵¹⁵ 15
[https://github.com/HearingTechnology/CoNNear_periphery](https://github.com/HearingTechnology/CoNNear_periphery),
which combines [CNN](#id1), capable of performing end-to-end waveform
predictions in real-time, with computational neuroscience to create a
real-time model of human cochlear mechanics and filter tuning. The
[CNN](#id1) filter weights were trained using simulated [BM](#id107)
([BM](#id107)) displacements from cochlear channels, and the model’s
performance was evaluated using basic acoustic stimuli. The CoNNear
model is designed to capture the tuning, level-dependence, and
longitudinal coupling characteristics of human cochlear processing. It
converts acoustic speech stimuli into [BM](#id107) displacement
waveforms across 201 cochlear filters. Its computational efficiency and
ability to capture human cochlear characteristics make it suitable for
developing human-like machine-hearing applications.

The research paper in \[[26](#bib.bib26)\] explores the utilization of a
[CNN](#id1) in simulating speech processing with [CI](#id4). The study
investigates the effect of channel interaction, a phenomenon that
degrades spectral resolution in [CI](#id4) delivered speech, on learning
in neural networks. By modifying speech spectrograms to approximate
[CI](#id4) delivered signals, the [CNN](#id1) is trained to classify
them. The findings suggest that early in training, the presence of
channel interaction negatively impacts performance. This indicates that
the spectral degradation caused by channel interaction conflicts with
perceptual expectations acquired from high-resolution speech. The study
highlights the potential for reducing channel interaction to enhance

### III-D [GAN](#id57)-based methods

A [GAN](#id57) is a type of [AI](#id2) model consisting of two neural
networks, a generator, and a discriminator, engaged in a competitive
learning process as presented in Figure
[8](#S3.F8 "Fig. 8 ‣ III-D -based methods ‣ III Taxonomy of -based AI techniques ‣ Artificial Intelligence for Cochlear Implants: Review of Strategies, Challenges, and Perspectives").

![Refer to caption](2403.15442v3/img_GAN.png)

Fig. 8: Illustrative depiction of a standard [GAN](#id57).

The generator aims to create realistic data, such as images, while the
discriminator tries to differentiate between real and generated samples.
This adversarial training dynamic leads to the refinement of the
generator’s output, generating increasingly authentic data. The
objective is for the generator to produce data that is indistinguishable
from real samples. The training process is represented by the minimax
game framework, with the [GAN](#id57) objective function given by:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\begin{split}\min_{G}\max_{D}V(D,G)=&\,\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{\text{data}}(x)}[\log D(x)]\\
&+\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p_{z}(z)}[\log(1-D(G(z)))]\end{split}
``` |  | (2) |

In the [GAN](#id57) objective function,
$`\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{\text{data}}(x)}`$ and
$`\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p_{z}(z)}`$ indicate the expected values over real
data samples $`x`$ and noise samples $`z`$, respectively. $`G`$
generates samples, $`D`$ discriminates between real and generated
samples, $`p_{\text{data}}`$ and $`p_{z}`$ are the distributions of real
data and noise, respectively. Using [GAN](#id57), the research in
\[[78](#bib.bib78)\] proposes a [DL](#id3)-based method for reducing
metal artifacts in post-operative [CT](#id20) imaging. The method
utilizes a 3D-[GAN](#id57) trained on a large number of pre-operative
images with simulated metal artifacts. The [GAN](#id57) generates
artifact-free images by reducing the metal artifacts. The effectiveness
of the method is evaluated quantitatively and qualitatively, showing
promising results compared to classical artifact reduction algorithms.
The approach overcomes the challenges of post-operative assessment of
cochlear implantation caused by metal artifacts, and it does not require
registration of pre and post-operative images. The 3D-[GAN](#id57)
improves spatial consistency and is applicable to various types of
artifacts. In addition, Wang et al. in theirs paper
\[[70](#bib.bib70)\], proposes a 3D metal artifact reduction algorithm
for post-operative high-resolution [CT](#id20) imaging. The algorithm is
based on a [GAN](#id57) that uses simulated physically realistic
[CT](#id20) metal artifacts created by [CI](#id4) electrodes. The
generated images are used to train the network for artifact reduction.
The metal artifact reduction-[GAN](#id57) based method as described in
\[[70](#bib.bib70)\], utilizes a three-step process for reducing metal
artifacts. Firstly, a simulation is performed to replicate [CI](#id4)
positioning. Secondly, a physical simulation of CI metal artifacts is
conducted. Lastly, a 3D GAN is trained using both simulated and
preoperative datasets. The generator component of the GAN generates an
image that has reduced metal artifacts, while the discriminator network
is responsible for determining whether the input image contains metal
artifacts or not. The method was evaluated on clinical [CT](#id20)
images of [CI](#id4) postoperative cases and outperformed other general
metal artifact reduction approaches. The paper introduces a novel
approach that combines the physical simulation of metal artifacts with
3D-[GAN](#id57), providing a promising solution for improving the visual
assessment of post-operative imaging in [CT](#id20).

Similarly, for [CI](#id4) metal artifacts reduction also, a
[cGAN](#id74) ([cGAN](#id74)) were proposed by Wang et al.
\[[71](#bib.bib71)\]. The approach involves training a [cGAN](#id74) to
learn mapping from artifact-affected [CT](#id20) to artifact-free
[CT](#id20). During inference, the [cGAN](#id74) generated [CT](#id20)
images with removed artifacts. Additionally, a band-wise normalization
method was proposed as a preprocessing step to improve the performance
of [cGAN](#id74). The method was evaluated on post-implantation
[CT](#id20) recipients, and the quality of the artifact-corrected images
was quantitatively assessed using [P2PE](#id76). The results demonstrate
promising artifact reduction, outperforming the previously proposed
techniques. The authors evaluates the quality of artifact-corrected
images using a quantitative metric based on segmentations of
intracochlear anatomical structures. Specifically, the segmentation
results obtained from a previously published method were compared
between real preimplantation [CT](#id20) and artifact-corrected
[CT](#id20) generated by the proposed method. The [ASE](#id123) was used
as a metric to assess the accuracy of the segmentation. The paper
reports that the proposed method achieves an [ASE](#id123) of 0.18 mm,
which is approximately half of the error obtained with a previously
proposed technique. Gogate et al. \[[79](#bib.bib79)\] propose a robust
real-time audio-visual speech enhancement framework for [CI](#id4). By
leveraging a [GAN](#id57) and [DNN](#id54), the framework effectively
addresses visual and acoustic speech noise in real-world environments.
Experimental results demonstrate significant improvements in speech
quality and intelligibility, offering potential benefits for [CI](#id4)
users in noisy social settings.

RNN are a class of artificial neural networks designed for sequential
data processing. They maintain hidden state information that is updated
at each time step, allowing them to capture temporal dependencies. The
hidden state at time $`t`$, denoted as $`h_{t}`$, is computed based on
the input $`x_{t}`$, the previous hidden state $`h_{t-1}`$, and model
parameters $`W`$ and $`U`$, with $`b`$ representing the bias term. The
equations governing the hidden state update are given by:

|     |                                 |     |     |
|-----|---------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       h_{t}=\sigma(Wx_{t}+Uh_{t-1}+b)
       ```                              |     | (3) |

where, $`\sigma`$ is an activation function, typically the hyperbolic
tangent or [ReLU](#id98) ([ReLU](#id98)). Several speech processing
techniques that utilize [CI](#id4) are based on [RNN](#id99).

[CI](#id4) users struggle with music perception, and many studies have

### III-F [AE](#id47)-based methods

An [AE](#id47) ([AE](#id47)) is a type of neural network designed for
unsupervised learning, tasked with encoding input data into a compressed
representation and decoding it back to the original form. Examples
include [VAE](#id45), which balance data compression with generative
modeling, [CAE](#id46), which employ convolutional layers for efficient
feature learning and reconstruction, and sparse [AE](#id47), which
induce sparsity, promoting selectivity in feature representation, among
others. The encoding equation typically involves a mapping function,
such as $`h=f(x)`$, where $`h`$ is the encoded representation and $`x`$
is the input. The decoding equation is the reconstruction of the input,
often expressed as $`r=g(h)`$, where $`r`$ is the reconstructed output
and $`g`$ is the decoding function. [AE](#id47) find applications in
data compression, denoising, feature learning, and more. Recently, many
research papers for [CI](#id4) have been proposed that are based on
[AE](#id47).

As a point of the case, the scientific paper \[[18](#bib.bib18)\] delves
into the pivotal objective of enhancing speech perception for [CI](#id4)
users in noisy conditions, recognizing the critical role of [NR](#id67)
([NR](#id67)) in this pursuit. The proposed method, named
[DDAE](#id9)-[NR](#id67), has been proven effective in restoring clean
speech. The study focuses on evaluating the [DDAE](#id9)-based
[NR](#id67) using envelope-based vocoded speech, mimicking [CI](#id4)
devices. The procedure of [DDAE](#id9)-based [NR](#id67) can be split
into two main stages: training and testing. During the training phase, a
collection of pairs of noisy and clean speech signals is prepared. These
signals are initially transformed into the frequency domain using an
[FFT](#id31) ([FFT](#id31)). The logarithmic amplitudes of the noisy and
clean speech spectra are then used as inputs and outputs, respectively,
for the [DDAE](#id9) model.

Key findings underscore the superior intelligibility of
[DDAE](#id9)-based [NR](#id67) in vocoded speech compared to
[SOTA](#id48) conventional methods, indicating its potential
implementation in [CI](#id4) speech processors. However, the study
acknowledges the use of noise-vocoded speech simulation for evaluation
and emphasizes the need for further validation with real [CI](#id4)
recipients in clinical settings, addressing potential inconsistencies in
the transition to actual [CI](#id4) devices.

A zero-delay [DAE](#id61) ([DAE](#id61)) is proposed in
\[[23](#bib.bib23)\] for compressing and transmitting electrical
stimulation patterns generated by [CI](#id4). The goal is to conserve
battery power in wireless transmission while maintaining low latency,
which is crucial for speech perception in [CI](#id4) users. The
[DAE](#id61) architecture is optimized using Bayesian optimization and
the [STOI](#id124). The results show that the proposed [DAE](#id61)
achieves equal or superior speech understanding compared to audio
codecs, with reference vocoder [STOI](#id124) scores at 13.5 kbit/s.
This approach offers a promising solution for efficient and real-time
compression of [CI](#id4) stimulation patterns, addressing the
constraints of low latency and battery power consumption. Moreover, The
research in \[[68](#bib.bib68)\] focuses on achieving accurate
segmentation of the vestibule in [CT](#id20) images, a crucial step for
clinical diagnosis of congenital ear malformations and [CI](#id4). The
challenges addressed include the small size and irregular shape of the
vestibule, making segmentation difficult, and the limited availability
of labelled samples due to high labour costs. To overcome these
challenges, the proposed method introduces a vestibule segmentation
network within a basic encoder-decoder framework. Key innovations
include the incorporation of a [Res-CA](#id68) ([Res-CA](#id68)) block

global context information, an , [ACE-Loss](#id70) ([ACE-Loss](#id70))
function for detailed boundary learning, and a [DS](#id71) ([DS](#id71))
mechanism to enhance network robustness. The network architecture

feature fusion. Results showcases a high performance, and are supported
by comprehensive comparisons, ablation studies, and visualized
segmentation outcomes. The study also acknowledges limitations, such as
reliance on professional annotations.

In addition, the study presented in \[[85](#bib.bib85)\] aims to enhance
the accuracy and robustness of [ICA](#id62) ([ICA](#id62)) segmentation,
a vital component in preoperative decisions, insertion planning, and

includes structures such as [ST](#id63) ([ST](#id63)), [SV](#id64)
([SV](#id64)), and the [AR](#id65) ([AR](#id65)). The researchers

[DL](#id3) based on 3D U-Net [AE](#id47), and combined them to achieve
improved accuracy and robustness. A two-level training strategy involved
pretraining on clinical [CT](#id20) using [ASM](#id66) and fine-tuning
on specimens’ [CT](#id20) with ground truth. Results demonstrated that
[DL](#id3) methods outperformed [ASM](#id66) in accuracy. While a
trade-off between accuracy and robustness was observed, the combined
[DL](#id3) and [ASM](#id66) approach showed improvements in both
aspects. The study concludes that the proposed [DL](#id3) and
[ASM](#id66) method effectively balances accuracy and robustness for
[ICA](#id62) segmentation, highlighting the potential of
[DL](#id3)-based methods, especially when integrated with [ASM](#id66),
to enhance [CI](#id4) procedures.

The proposed [MMS](#id34) ([MMS](#id34)) methodology in
\[[69](#bib.bib69)\] represents a groundbreaking approach to
semi-supervised segmentation networks, particularly in the context of
medical applications such as endoscopy surgical tool segmentation and
[CI](#id4) surgery. [MMS](#id34) is introduced through dual-view
training with contrastive learning, utilizing classifiers and projectors
to create negative, positive, and negative pairs. The inclusion of
pixel-wise contrastive loss ensures the consistency of unlabeled
predictions. In the evaluation phase, [MMS](#id34) was tested on four
public endoscopy surgical tool segmentation datasets and a manually
annotated [CI](#id4) surgery dataset. The results demonstrate its
superiority over [SOTA](#id48) semi-supervised and fully supervised
segmentation algorithms, both quantitatively and qualitatively. Notably,
[MMS](#id34) exhibited successful recognition of unknown surgical tools,
providing reliable predictions, and achieved real-time video
segmentation with an impressive inference speed of about 40 frames per
second. This signifies the potential of [MMS](#id34) as a highly
effective and efficient tool in medical image segmentation, showcasing
its applicability in real-world surgical scenarios.

Similarly, The primary aim of the study in \[[86](#bib.bib86)\] is to
devise an automated method for the segmentation and measurement of the
human cochlea in [UHR](#id72) ([UHR](#id72)) [CT](#id20)-images. The
objective is to explore variations in cochlear size to enhance outcomes
in cochlear surgery through personalized implant planning. Initially,
the input scans undergo a two-step process using a detection module and
a pixel-wise classification module for cochlea localization and
segmentation, respectively using an [AE](#id47) as illustrated in Figure
[10](#S3.F10 "Fig. 10 ‣ III-F -based methods ‣ III Taxonomy of -based AI techniques ‣ Artificial Intelligence for Cochlear Implants: Review of Strategies, Challenges, and Perspectives").
The detection module reduces the search area for the classification
module, improving algorithm speed and reducing false positives. Both
modules are trained on image patches, allowing for a larger training set
size by generating multiple examples from each scan. The segmented
cochlear structure then proceeds to a final module that combines
[DL](#id3) and thinning algorithms to extract patient-specific
anatomical measurements. [DL](#id3) is employed in each step to leverage
its ability to learn directly from input data, providing automatic
results without the need for user-adjustable parameters during testing.

### III-G RL-based methods

[DRL](#id109) ([DRL](#id109)) is a subfield of [ML](#id5) that enable
agents to learn and make decisions in complex environments. It involves
training an agent to interact with an environment, learn from the
outcomes of its actions, and optimize its behavior over time
\[[87](#bib.bib87)\]. In traditional [RL](#id110), agents learn by trial
and error, receiving feedback in the form of rewards or penalties for
their actions. However, [DRL](#id109) incorporates [DNN](#id54), make it
capable of learning complex patterns and representations from raw data.
This allows [DRL](#id109) agents to handle high-dimensional input
spaces, such as images or sensor data, and make more sophisticated
decisions. Figure

The paper presented by radutoiu et al. \[[34](#bib.bib34)\] presents a
novel method for accurately localizing [ROI](#id108) in the inner ear
using [DRL](#id109). The proposed method addresses the challenges of
robust [ROI](#id108) extraction in full head [CT](#id20) scans, which is
crucial for [CI](#id4) surgery. The approach utilizes communicative
multi-agent [RL](#id110) and landmarks specifically designed to extract

of 1.07 mm for landmark localization. The extracted [ROI](#id108)
demonstrate an [IoU](#id42) of 0.84 and a dice similarity coefficient of

for automatic [ROI](#id108) extraction in medical imaging. In addition,

characterizing facial and cochlear nerves in [CT](#id20) scans using

automated measurement of the cochlear nerve canal diameter, extraction
and segmentation of the cochlear nerve cross-section, and path selection
for the facial nerve characterization. The pipeline was developed and
evaluated using 119 clinical [CT](#id20) images. The results show
accurate characterizations of the nerves in the cochlear region,
providing reliable measurements for computer-aided diagnosis and surgery
planning. The proposed approach demonstrates the potential of
[DRL](#id109) for landmark detection in challenging medical imaging
tasks.

### IV-A Speech denoising and enhancement

The integration of [ML](#id5), and [DL](#id3) has proven invaluable in
the field of [CI](#id4). Researchers have harnessed these technologies
to tackle numerous challenges and enhance speech perception for
individuals with hearing impairments. The work \[[19](#bib.bib19),
[89](#bib.bib89)\] employed [DDAE](#id9) approach to reduce unwanted
background noise in speech signals. However, Lai et al. in
\[[89](#bib.bib89)\] devised a [NR](#id67) system that employed a noise
classifier and [DDAE](#id9), specifically tailored for Mandarin-speaking
[CI](#id4) recipients. The proposed schemes \[[24](#bib.bib24),
[28](#bib.bib28), [90](#bib.bib90)\] aim to perform end-to-end speech
denoising, with the goal of enhancing speech intelligibility in noisy
environments. Gajecki et al. \[[24](#bib.bib24), [28](#bib.bib28)\]
employed [DNN](#id54) to develop the Deep ACE method, while Healy et al.
in \[[90](#bib.bib90)\] utilized [DNN](#id54) to separate speech from
background noise. These examples underscore the broad spectrum of
applications of [AI](#id2), particularly [DL](#id3) techniques, in
addressing challenges related to noise reduction and enhancing speech
intelligibility in [CI](#id4) applications.

Moreover, Kang et al. \[[30](#bib.bib30)\] used [DL](#id3)-based speech
enhancement algorithms to optimize speech perception for [CI](#id4)
recipients. Their approach achieved a balance between noise suppression
and speech distortion by experimenting with different loss functions. Hu
et al. \[[91](#bib.bib91)\] developed environment-specific noise
suppression algorithms for [CI](#id4) using [ML](#id5) techniques. They
improved the processed sound by classifying and selecting envelope
amplitudes based on the [SNR](#id10) in each channel. Banerjee et al.
\[[92](#bib.bib92)\] employed online unsupervised algorithms to learn
features from the speech of individuals with severe-to-profound hearing
loss, aiming to enhance the audibility of speech through modified signal
processing. Li et al. \[[93](#bib.bib93)\] developed an improved
[NR](#id67) system for [CI](#id4) using [DL](#id3), specifically
[DDAE](#id9), and knowledge transfer technology. Their goal was to
enhance speech intelligibility in noisy conditions. Fischer et al.
\[[31](#bib.bib31)\] utilized [DL](#id3)-based virtual sensing of
head-mounted microphones to improve speech signals in cocktail party
scenarios for individuals with hearing loss, resulting in enhanced
speech quality and intelligibility, particularly in noisy environments.
These studies exemplify the versatility of [AI](#id2) and [DL](#id3) in
addressing various challenges associated with [CI](#id4), including
[NR](#id67), speech enhancement, and improved speech perception.
Furthermore, the paper by Chu et al. \[[25](#bib.bib25)\] explores the
application of [ML](#id5) algorithms to mitigate the effects of
reverberation and noise in [CI](#id4), to improve speech intelligibility
for individuals with severe hearing loss.

### IV-B Imaging

[DL](#id3) methods have revolutionized [CI](#id4) applications by
leveraging imaging data for enhanced analysis and optimization. Hussain
et al. \[[98](#bib.bib98)\] employed image analysis tools, such as the
oticon medical nautilus software, to automatically detect landmarks and
extract clinically relevant parameters from cochlear [CT](#id20) images.
This approach provides valuable insights into cochlear morphology,
facilitating the development of less traumatic electrode arrays for
cochlear implantation. Zhang et al. \[[61](#bib.bib61)\] focused on
automatically detecting the presence and location of inner ears in head
[CT](#id20) images, aiming to assist in image-guided [CI](#id4)
programming for patients with profound hearing loss. Regodic et al.
\[[99](#bib.bib99)\] introduced an algorithm that utilizes a [CNN](#id1)
for automatic fiducial marker detection and localization in [CT](#id20)
images, enhancing registration accuracy, reducing human errors, and
shortening intervention time in computer-assisted surgeries. Margeta et
al. \[[94](#bib.bib94)\] presented Nautilus, a web-based research
platform that employs [AI](#id2) and image processing techniques for
automated cochlear image analysis. This platform enables accurate
delineation of cochlear structures, detection of electrode locations,
and personalized pre- and post-operative metrics, facilitating clinical
exploration in cochlear implantation studies. Li et al.
\[[100](#bib.bib100)\] proposed the integration of [DL](#id3) techniques
into a clinical $`\mu`$[CT](#id20) system to optimize imaging
performance, improve reconstruction accuracy, and enhance diagnostic
capabilities in temporal bone imaging and other clinical applications.
Wang et al. \[[78](#bib.bib78)\] addressed the reduction of metal
artifacts in post-operative [CI](#id4) [CT](#id20) imaging using a 3D
[GAN](#id57), enabling better analysis of electrode positions and
assessment of [CI](#id4) insertion. These advancements highlight the
significant role of [DL](#id3), [ML](#id5), and [AI](#id2) in leveraging
imaging data for improved [CI](#id4) analysis, design, and surgical
procedures.

In addition to the previous advancements, [DL](#id3) and [AI](#id2) have
been applied to various aspects of [CI](#id4) applications using imaging
data. Chen et al. \[[68](#bib.bib68)\] utilize [AI](#id2) for accurate
vestibule segmentation in [CT](#id20) images, which plays a crucial role
in the clinical diagnosis of congenital ear malformations and [CI](#id4)
procedures. Kugler et al. \[[101](#bib.bib101)\] employ [AI](#id2)
techniques to accurately estimate instrument pose from X-ray images in
temporal bone surgery, enabling high-precision navigation and
facilitating minimally invasive procedures. Waldeck et al.
\[[102](#bib.bib102)\] develop an ultra-fast algorithm that utilizes
automated cochlear image registration to detect misalignment in
[CI](#id4), significantly reducing the time required for diagnosis
compared to traditional multiplanar reconstruction analysis. Finally,
Chen et al. \[[103](#bib.bib103)\] focus on creating a three-dimensional
finite element model of the brain based on [MRI](#id119) ([MRI](#id119))
data to analyze and optimize the current flow path induced by
[CI](#id4). This application of [AI](#id2) contributes to the
improvement of implant design in the future. These innovative approaches
demonstrate the diverse applications of [DL](#id3), [ML](#id5), and
[AI](#id2) in [CI](#id4) research, ranging from scene understanding to
precise segmentation, instrument pose estimation, misalignment
detection, and implant design optimization.

### IV-C Segmentation

[DL](#id3), [ML](#id5), and [AI](#id2) have revolutionized [CI](#id4)
segmentation, enabling precise identification and analysis of cochlear
structures in various imaging modalities. Li et al. \[[96](#bib.bib96)\]
applied a UNETR model to automatically segment cochlear structures in
temporal bone [CT](#id20) images, enhancing surgical planning and
cochlear implantation outcomes. Reda et al. \[[104](#bib.bib104)\]
developed an automatic segmentation method for intra-cochlear anatomy in
post-implantation [CT](#id20) scans, facilitating the customization of
sound processing strategies for individual [CI](#id4) recipients.
Moudgalya et al. \[[105](#bib.bib105)\] employed a modified V-Net
[CNN](#id1) to segment cochlear compartments in $`\mu`$[CT](#id20)
images, enabling precise quantification of local drug delivery for
potential treatment of sensorineural hearing loss. Wang et al.
\[[106](#bib.bib106)\] focused on metal artifact reduction and
intra-cochlear anatomy segmentation in [CT](#id20) images using a
multi-resolution multi-task deep network, benefiting [CI](#id4)
recipients. Heutink et al. \[[86](#bib.bib86)\] developed a [DL](#id3)
framework for the automatic segmentation and analysis of cochlear
structures in ultra-high-resolution [CT](#id20) images, providing
accurate measurements for personalized implant planning in cochlear
surgery. Zhang et al. \[[97](#bib.bib97)\] utilized a 3D U-Net
[DL](#id3) method to achieve accurate segmentation of intra-cochlear
anatomy in head [CT](#id20) images, facilitating optimal programming of
[CI](#id4) and improving hearing outcomes. These studies highlight the
significant impact of [DL](#id3), [ML](#id5), and [AI](#id2) in
advancing [CI](#id4) segmentation, ultimately leading to improved
patient care and treatment outcomes. Recently, Zhu et al.
\[[107](#bib.bib107)\] proposes an uncertainty-aware dual-stream
network, called UADSN, for facial nerve segmentation in [CT](#id20)
scans for cochlear implantation surgery. UADSN combines 2D and 3D
segmentation streams and uses consistency loss to improve accuracy in
uncertain regions. The network achieves superior performance compared to
other methods on a facial nerve dataset, with an emphasis on topology
preservation.

### IV-D Thresholding

[DL](#id3), [ML](#id5), and [AI](#id2) have been instrumental in the
field of [CI](#id4), particularly in thresholding applications.
Kuczapski et al. \[[108](#bib.bib108)\] developed a software tool that
utilizes [AI](#id2) to estimate and monitor the [EST](#id117)
([EST](#id117)) levels in [CI](#id4) recipients. By leveraging patient
data, audiograms, and fitting settings, this tool aids in the fitting
process and predicts changes in hearing levels, enhancing personalized
care. Botros et al. \[[109](#bib.bib109)\] introduced AutoNRT, an
automated system that combines [ML](#id5) and pattern recognition to
measure [ECAP](#id118) ([ECAP](#id118)) thresholds with the Nucleus
Freedom [CI](#id4). This objective fitting system streamlines clinical
procedures and ensures precise and efficient threshold measurements.
Furthermore, Schuerch et al. \[[36](#bib.bib36)\] utilized a
[DL](#id3)-based algorithm to objectively evaluate and analyze
[ECochG](#id101) signals. This algorithm enables the assessment of
[ECochG](#id101) measurement repeatability, comparison with audiometric
thresholds, and identification of signal patterns and tonotopic behavior
in [CI](#id4) recipients. Through the integration of [DL](#id3), machine
[ML](#id5), and [AI](#id2), these studies have significantly advanced
thresholding techniques in [CI](#id4) applications, leading to improved
fitting accuracy, streamlined procedures, and objective evaluation of
signal responses.

### IV-E Localization of [CI](#id4)

[DL](#id3) methods have been instrumental in [CI](#id4) localization
applications, providing accurate and automated solutions. Chi et al.
\[[95](#bib.bib95)\] proposed a [DL](#id3)-based method for precise
localization of electrode contacts in [CT](#id20) images. Their approach
utilized cGANs to generate likelihood maps, which were then processed to
estimate the exact location of each contact. Radutoiu et al.
\[[34](#bib.bib34)\] focused on the automatic extraction of
[ROI](#id108) in full head [CT](#id20) scans of the inner ear. By
leveraging [AI](#id2), they achieved high precision in [ROI](#id108)
localization, facilitating accurate surgical planning for insertion.
Noble et al. \[[110](#bib.bib110)\] and Zhao et al.
\[[111](#bib.bib111), [112](#bib.bib112)\], developed AI-based systems
to automatically identify and position electrode arrays in [CT](#id20)
images. These technologies enable large-scale analyses of the
relationship between electrode placement and hearing outcomes, leading
to potential advancements in implant design and surgical techniques.
Heutink et al. \[[86](#bib.bib86)\] employed [DL](#id3) for the
automatic segmentation and localization of the cochlea in
ultra-high-resolution [CT](#id20) images. This approach allows for
precise measurements that can be used in personalized planning, reducing
the risk of intra-cochlear trauma and optimizing surgical outcomes.
These studies showcase the significant contributions of [DL](#id3) and
[AI](#id2) in localization applications, enabling accurate and efficient
identification, positioning, and analysis of electrode arrays and
facilitating improved surgical planning and outcomes. Burkart et al.
\[[113](#bib.bib113)\] investigates the influence of sound source
position and electrode placement on the stimulation patterns of
[CI](#id4) under noise conditions. The study utilizes a measurement
setup to simulate realistic listening scenarios. The results reveal that
the effectiveness of [CI](#id4) noise reduction systems is influenced by
these factors, and artificial intelligence fitting algorithms should be
considered to optimize [CI](#id4) performance.

### IV-F Other

[DL](#id3) techniques have been employed in various [CI](#id4)
applications, showcasing their potential to enhance hearing outcomes and
improve device performance. Bermejo et al. \[[114](#bib.bib114)\]
introduced a decision support system using a novel probabilistic
graphical model to optimize [CI](#id4) parameters based on audiological
tests and the current device status, aiming to optimize the user’s
hearing ability. Castaneda et al. \[[115](#bib.bib115)\] focused on the
use of [BSS](#id120) ([BSS](#id120)) and independent component analysis
(ICA) to identify [AEP](#id121) and isolate artifacts in children with
[CI](#id4), enabling improved assessment of auditory function. Incerti

cross-over frequency settings for [EAS](#id11) on binaural speech
perception, localization, and functional performance in adults with
[CI](#id4) and residual hearing, providing valuable insights for
personalized device programming. Katthi et al. \[[117](#bib.bib117)\]
developed a [DL](#id3) framework based on [CCA](#id122) ([CCA](#id122))
to decode the auditory brain, establishing a strong correlation between
audio input and brain activity measured through [EEG](#id80) recordings.
This research has implications for decoding human auditory attention and
improving [CI](#id4) by leveraging the power of [DL](#id3).

## V Open issues and future directions

While significant strides have been achieved in integrating [AI](#id2)
into [CI](#id4), numerous research lacunae persist, offering avenues for
further advancements in the field. Here are several potential realms
warranting exploration in future studies:

Real-time signal processing and personalized design: Investigating
real-time adaptive signal processing methods employing [AI](#id2)
algorithms has the potential to enhance sound processing for [CI](#id4)
recipients, yielding enhanced speech intelligibility outcomes.
Enhancements in adaptability to dynamic acoustic environments and
real-time optimization of stimulation parameters have the capacity to
substantially enhance [CI](#id4) performance. The authors have observed
a gap in the study and implementation of [AI](#id2) models tailored for
[CI](#id4) on real-time platforms like field programmable gate arrays
(FPGAs). Further research in this burgeoning area holds promise for
adapting a variety of existing AI models to enhance real-time
capabilities.

Besides, tailoring [CI](#id4) to meet the unique needs of individual
users poses a significant challenge. Investigating [AI](#id2)-driven
methodologies leveraging personal data to personalize device
configurations based on factors like physiological, auditory, and neural
feedback during mobility can enhance both individual outcomes and
overall satisfaction.

Predicting the long-term effects: Gaining insight into the enduring
[CI](#id4) is vital for enhancing patient selection, counseling, and
device advancement. Utilizing [AI](#id2) methods to shift through
extensive datasets can pinpoint the predictive elements influencing
sustained success. These factors may encompass pre-implantation
attributes, surgical approaches, and auditory rehabilitation.
Constructing predictive models using AI algorithms can furnish valuable
perspectives on long-term consequences, thereby informing clinical
judgments.

Incorporating multiple sensory and modalities: [CI](#id4) traditionally
prioritize the reinstatement of auditory experiences. Yet, enriching the
perception and comprehension of sound can be achieved by integrating
additional sensory dimensions like vision and touch, resulting in a
multi-modal approach. Investigating [AI](#id2)-driven techniques that
amalgamate inputs from various senses to enhance speech recognition,
spatial sound perception, and overall auditory understanding presents a
promising direction for future exploration. Besides, [CI](#id4) in both
ears, when paired with [AI](#id2) algorithms, can enhance speech
comprehension. By analyzing sound patterns from both implants, AI
adjusts settings to optimize signal processing, improving overall
accuracy and clarity of speech perception for users with bilateral
implants, and enhancing their auditory experience and communication
abilities, as investigated in \[[118](#bib.bib118)\]. However, extensive
research possibilities are required to tailor solutions with [CI](#id4)
hardware capabilities, by taking into account computation cost, and
[AI](#id2) model complexity.

Empowering AI-based CI using DTL: [DTL](#id127) ([DTL](#id127)) is a
highly efficient [DL](#id3) technique enabling the transfer of knowledge
from pre-trained models, trained on millions of speech corpora and/or
images, to train smaller models with limited data availability
\[[119](#bib.bib119), [120](#bib.bib120)\]. This approach offers
significant advantages in producing lightweight [AI](#id2) models
suitable for devices with limited computational resources, such as
[CI](#id4). Only a limited number of studies have explored the impact of
[DTL](#id127) on [CI](#id4), as demonstrated in \[[93](#bib.bib93)\],
which has received relatively little attention from researchers. We
anticipate further exploration of this promising technique, particularly
through the utilization of various [DTL](#id127) sub-techniques, such as
domain adaptation, transductive methods like cross-lingual transfer,
cross-corpus transfer, zero-shot learning, fine-tuning, among others
\[[2](#bib.bib2)\].

Ensuring data privacy through FL: [FL](#id129) ([FL](#id129))
facilitates collaborative model training across decentralized devices by
aggregating local updates rather than centralizing data. This preserves
user privacy and enhances model performance, particularly beneficial in
healthcare applications \[[121](#bib.bib121)\]. Gathering comprehensive
datasets is challenging due to rare anomaly cases and privacy concerns.
[FL](#id129) addresses this by training models on distributed, encrypted
data from multiple sources, ensuring privacy while maintaining efficacy.
Researchers have yet to fully explore FL-based model building for
[CI](#id4), neglecting the potential to construct efficient [AI](#id2)
models capable of accommodating diverse classes. Further investigation
into this promising technique is warranted, with potential for
significant advancements in model robustness and versatility. Moreover,
this approach could lead to the development of a pretrained model
utilizing [FL](#id129), which could be seamlessly integrated with
[DTL](#id127).

Transformers-based CI techniques: AI researchers have adopted the
CNN-LSTM model that excels in capturing spatial and temporal features,
enhancing performance in sequential data tasks \[[8](#bib.bib8),
[122](#bib.bib122), [123](#bib.bib123)\]. However, Transformer-based ASR
techniques, such as [CTC](#id130) ([CTC](#id130)), [BERT](#id131)
([BERT](#id131)), and others, proved in the literature that have the
potential to greatly enhance the functioning of [ASR](#id53)
\[[124](#bib.bib124), [125](#bib.bib125), [126](#bib.bib126)\]. By
leveraging the self-attention mechanism, transformers can improve speech
intelligibility by effectively suppressing background noise and modeling
long-range dependencies. They can also aid in acoustic scene analysis,
separating and prioritizing important auditory information in complex
environments. Transformers can build language models that enhance
[ASR](#id53) systems, improving speech comprehension for users.
Additionally, transformers enable personalized sound processing by
adapting stimulation patterns and processing parameters based on
user-specific preferences. They facilitate multi-modal integration,
combining audio and visual inputs to enhance speech recognition and
sound localization. Furthermore, transformers support long-term learning
and adaptation, continually optimizing [CI](#id4) performance over time.
These advancements offer promising prospects for improving auditory
experiences and overall quality of life for [CI](#id4) users.

Exploring chat-bots-based CI capabilities: Chat-bot techniques offer
several opportunities to enhance the functioning of [CI](#id4). They can
provide real-time support, troubleshooting, and personalized
rehabilitation programs for users, empowering them to address common
issues and improve their auditory skills. Chat-bots enable remote
monitoring, allowing users to share data and receive adjustments to
their device settings without in-person appointments. They also offer
emotional and psychological support, fostering a sense of community and
well-being. Chat-bots contribute to data collection for research and
development, aiding in the improvement of [CI](#id4) technology and
rehabilitation protocols. Additionally, chat-bots employ[ML](#id5) to
continuously learn from user interactions, improving their responses and
understanding over time. These techniques have the potential to enhance
the overall user experience, outcomes, and accessibility of [CI](#id4)
services. For example, in \[[127](#bib.bib127)\], the effectiveness of
ChatGPT-4 in providing postoperative care information to [CI](#id4)
patients was evaluated. Five common questions were posed to ChatGPT-4,
and its responses were analyzed for accuracy, response time, clarity,
and relevance. The results showed that ChatGPT-4 provided accurate and
timely responses, making it a reliable supplementary resource for
patients in need of information.

## VI Conclusion

This review has provided a comprehensive overview of the advancements in
[AI](#id2) algorithms for [CI](#id4) applications and their impact on
[ASR](#id53) and speech enhancement. The integration of [AI](#id2)
methods has brought cutting-edge strategies to address the limitations
and challenges faced by traditional signal processing techniques in the
context of [CI](#id4). Moreover, the application of [AI](#id2) in
[CI](#id4) has led to the emergence of new datasets and evaluation
metrics, offering alternative methods for validating proposed schemes
without the need for human surgical intervention and traditional tests.
The review highlighted the role of [ASR](#id53) in optimizing speech
perception and understanding for [CI](#id4) users, contributing to the
improvement of their quality of life. [ASR](#id53) not only enhances
basic speech recognition but also aids in the recognition of
environmental sounds, enabling a more immersive auditory experience.
Furthermore, [ASR](#id53) finds applications in authentication systems,
event recognition, source separation, and speaker recognition, extending
its reach beyond communication. Various [AI](#id2) algorithms, belong to
[ML](#id5) and [DL](#id3), have been explored in the context of
[CI](#id4), demonstrating promising results in speech synthesis and
noise reduction. These algorithms have shown the potential to overcome
challenges associated with multiple sources of speech, environmental
noise, and other complex scenarios. The review has summarized and
commented on the best results obtained, providing valuable insights into
the capabilities of [AI](#id2) algorithms in this biomedical field.
Moving forward, the review suggests future directions to bridge existing
research gaps in the domain of [AI](#id2) algorithms for [CI](#id4). It
emphasizes the need for high-quality data inputs, algorithm
transparency, and collaboration between researchers, clinicians, and
industry experts. Addressing these aspects will facilitate the
development of more accurate and efficient [AI](#id2) algorithms for
[CI](#id4), ultimately benefiting individuals with hearing impairments.
The integration of advanced [AI](#id2) algorithms has the potential to
revolutionize the field of [CI](#id4), providing individuals with
hearing impairments to better communicate and engage with the world
around them. Continued research and development in this area hold great
promise for the future of [CI](#id4) technology.
