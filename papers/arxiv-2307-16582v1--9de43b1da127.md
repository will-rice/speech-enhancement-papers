# SAMbA: Speech enhancement with Asynchronous ad-hoc Microphone Arrays

Nicolas Furnon    Romain Serizel    Slim Essid    Irina Illina

###### Abstract

Speech enhancement in ad-hoc microphone arrays is often hindered by the
asynchronization of the devices composing the microphone array.
Asynchronization comes from sampling time offset and sampling rate
offset which inevitably occur when the microphones are embedded in
different hardware components. In this paper, we propose a deep neural
network (DNN)-based speech enhancement solution that is suited for
applications in ad-hoc microphone arrays because it is distributed and
copes with asynchronization. We show that asynchronization has a limited
impact on the spatial filtering and mostly affects the performance of
the DNNs. Instead of resynchronising the signals, which requires costly
processing steps, we use an attention mechanism which makes the DNNs,
thus our whole pipeline, robust to asynchronization. We also show that
the attention mechanism leads to an estimation of the sampling time
offset in an unsupervised manner.

## 1 Introduction

Due to their increased number of microphones, their spatial coverage and
their flexibility of use, ad-hoc microphone arrays offer great potential
to speech enhancement. This potential however may be limited by a series
of challenges. One of the main challenges is the need for a distributed
strategy which does not rely on a fusion center as most of classic
beamformers do. Distributed algorithms have been proposed for speech
enhancement in ad-hoc microphone arrays \[[1](#bib.bib1),
[2](#bib.bib2), [3](#bib.bib3), [4](#bib.bib4)\] and recently, a
[dnn](#id21) ([dnn](#id21))-based distributed solution has also been
introduced to combine the increased modelling capacity of [dnn](#id21)
with the flexibility of use of ad-hoc microphone
arrays \[[5](#bib.bib5)\]. Besides, because the microphones embedded in
different devices do not share the same hardware, they are acquired at
different [sr](#id42) ([sr](#id42)) (even if the nominal [sr](#id42)
([sr](#id42)) is the same), causing a [sro](#id38) ([sro](#id38)), and
triggered at different starting times, causing a [sto](#id39)
([sto](#id39)). These phenomena cause asynchronization \[[6](#bib.bib6),
[7](#bib.bib7)\]. Asynchronization can have a negative impact on speech
enhancement \[[8](#bib.bib8)\], especially on solutions relying on an
accurate estimation of the direction-of-arrival, like the minimum
variance distortionless response beamformer \[[9](#bib.bib9),
[10](#bib.bib10), [11](#bib.bib11)\]. It is yet interesting to note that
some works showed that asynchronization could be tolerated in speech
enhancement tasks without any attempt to resample the signals
\[[20](#bib.bib20), [21](#bib.bib21)\].

Solutions to asynchronization can be broadly classified into two
categories. In the first category, specific signals are sent among the
nodes. These signals can be either calibration signals \[[8](#bib.bib8),
[12](#bib.bib12)\] or time stamps \[[13](#bib.bib13), [6](#bib.bib6),
[7](#bib.bib7)\]. The second category gathers so-called blind
approaches, because no signals are exchanged but the ones captured by
the microphones of the nodes. Out of these observations, the
[sro](#id38) and [sto](#id39) can be estimated and compensated for based
on the coherence \[[14](#bib.bib14), [15](#bib.bib15)\], the correlation
\[[16](#bib.bib16)\] or the cross-correlation \[[17](#bib.bib17),
[18](#bib.bib18), [15](#bib.bib15), [19](#bib.bib19)\] between signals
of different nodes. These solutions proved to be efficient, but they
suffer from two limitations. The first one is that they require extra
computing steps, which might overload the small devices of ad-hoc
microphone arrays and add some latency to the processing. The second
limitation is that none of these works studies the impact of
asynchronization on [dnn](#id21) although [dnn](#id21) take a more and
more important place in speech enhancement.

In this paper, we propose to study the impact of asynchronization on a
speech enhancement solution for ad-hoc microphone arrays based on
[dnn](#id21). We show that the impact of [sro](#id38) and [sto](#id39)
on spatial filtering is limited, but that their impact on the
[dnn](#id21) performance is not negligible. To cope with this, instead
of resampling the signals, we decide to use an attention mechanism which
implicitly realigns the input signals of the [dnn](#id21). This avoids
an explicit search of the asynchronization parameters.

This paper is organized as follows. In
Section [2](#S2 "2 Problem formulation ‣ SAMbA: Speech enhancement with Asynchronous ad-hoc Microphone Arrays")
we describe the problem, the notations used throughout the paper and we
introduce our speech enhancement system. The experimental setup is
described in
Section [3](#S3 "3 Experimental setup ‣ SAMbA: Speech enhancement with Asynchronous ad-hoc Microphone Arrays").
In
Section [4](#S4 "4 Impact of asynchronization on -based speech enhancement ‣ SAMbA: Speech enhancement with Asynchronous ad-hoc Microphone Arrays")
we analyse the impact of [sto](#id39) and [sro](#id38) on our speech
enhancement system. In
Section [5](#S5 "5 Solution to asynchronization of the input signals of ‣ SAMbA: Speech enhancement with Asynchronous ad-hoc Microphone Arrays"),
we introduce a solution to cope with the asynchronization effects on the
[dnn](#id21) performance in our system. Lastly,
Section [6](#S6 "6 Conclusions ‣ SAMbA: Speech enhancement with Asynchronous ad-hoc Microphone Arrays")
concludes this paper.

## 2 Problem formulation

### 2.1 Notations

In the following, signals are considered in the [stft](#id11)
([stft](#id11)) domain, where time and frequency indices are dropped for
the sake of conciseness. Bold lowercase letters represent vectors. Bold
uppercase letters represent matrices. Regular lowercase represent
scalars. We consider an ad-hoc microphone array of $`K`$ nodes of
$`M_{k}`$ microphones each. The $`m`$-th microphone of the $`k`$-th node
records a noisy mixture $`y_{k,m}~=~s_{k,m}+n_{k,m}`$ according to an
additive noise model, where $`s_{k,m}`$ and $`n_{k,m}`$ are respectively
the target speech and the noise components recorded by the microphone.
The signals recorded by node $`k`$ are stacked in a vector
$`\mathbf{y}_{k}~=~[y_{k,1},\cdots,y_{k,M_{k}}]^{T}`$ .

The [sro](#id38) of a node $`k`$ relatively to a reference node will be
denoted by $`\epsilon_{k}`$. The [sto](#id39) of a node $`k`$ relatively
to a reference node will be denoted by $`\tau_{k}`$.

### 2.2 Distributed speech enhancement in ad-hoc microphone arrays

\includegraphics

\[width=trim=0cm 0cm 1cm 0cm, clip\]tango

Figure 1: Graphical representation of our distributed speech enhancement
solution. Bold arrows represent multichannel signals, simple arrows
represent single-channel signals.

In a previous work, we introduced a distributed speech enhancement
system for ad-hoc microphone arrays, called Tango \[[5](#bib.bib5)\]. It
processes in two steps, highlighted in
Figure [1](#S2.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 2.2 Distributed speech enhancement in ad-hoc microphone arrays ‣ 2 Problem formulation ‣ SAMbA: Speech enhancement with Asynchronous ad-hoc Microphone Arrays").
In the first step, at each node $`k`$, a [mwf](#id6) ([mwf](#id6))
$`\mathbf{w}_{kk}`$ is applied on the local signals $`\mathbf{y}_{k}`$.
To do this, a [sndnn](#id40) ([sndnn](#id40)) is used to predict a
[tf](#id12) ([tf](#id12)) mask $`m_{k}`$ out of the reference signal
$`y_{k,1}`$. The [tf](#id12) mask is used to compute the spatial
covariance matrices of the speech and noise required by the spatial
filter.

Filtering the mixture with this beamformer yields a so-called compressed
signal $`z_{k}=\mathbf{w}_{kk}^{H}\mathbf{y}_{k}\,.`$ The compressed
signals are exchanged among nodes, so node $`k`$ receives $`K-1`$
compressed signals $`\mathbf{z}_{-k}`$:
$`\mathbf{z}_{-k}~=~[z_{1},...,z_{k-1},z_{k+1},...,z_{K}]^{T}`$. In the
second step, a global [mwf](#id6) $`\mathbf{w}_{k}`$ is applied on
$`\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{k}=\left[\mathbf{y}_{k}^{T},~\mathbf{z}_{-k}^{T}\right]^{T}`$.
The compressed signals $`\mathbf{z}_{-k}`$ are used for the spatial
filtering operation, but they are also fed to a [mndnn](#id41)
([mndnn](#id41)) to predict the [tf](#id12) mask $`\tilde{m}_{k}`$
required by the spatial filter. The spatial filters at both filtering
steps are computed following the rank-1 [gevd](#id9) ([gevd](#id9)) of
the covariance matrices of the mixture and of the noise proposed by
Serizel et al. \[[22](#bib.bib22)\].

We showed that this algorithm could efficiently process the spatial
information conveyed by the compressed signals and outperforms an oracle
[vad](#id17) ([vad](#id17))-based [mwf](#id6) \[[23](#bib.bib23)\]. We
also showed that it performs comparatively well to FaSNet
\[[24](#bib.bib24)\], while allowing for a trade-off between noise
reduction and speech distortion, and relying on a much simpler
[dnn](#id21) architecture \[[5](#bib.bib5)\]. For these reasons, we
continue using this system for the current work.

## 3 Experimental setup

### 3.1 Signal setup and [dnn](#id21) settings

All the signals are sampled at 16 kHz and last between 5 s and 10 s. The
[stft](#id11) is computed with a Hann window of 32 ms with an overlap of
16 ms. The [crnn](#id26) ([crnn](#id26)) architecture is composed of
three convolutional layers followed by a recurrent layer and a
fully-connected layer. The convolutional layers have 32, 64 and 64
filters, with kernel size $`3\times 3`$ and stride $`1\times 1`$. Each
convolutional layer is followed by a batch normalisation and a
maximum-pooling layer of kernel size $`4\times 1`$ so that no pooling is
applied over the time axis. The recurrent layer is a 256-unit GRU. The
fully-connected layer has 257 units with a sigmoid activation function.
The input of the model are the magnitudes of the [stft](#id11) windows
of 21 consecutive frames and the ground truth labels are the
corresponding frames of the ideal ratio mask. At test time, only the
middle frame of the predicted window is considered to estimate the mask,
so sliding windows of the input are fed to the [dnn](#id21). The mask of
the whole signal is predicted before being used to enhance the speech in
a batch mode.

### 3.2 Training and evaluation data

The data used to train and evaluate our systems is extracted from the
Disco dataset.¹¹ 1
https://github.com/nfurnon/disco/tree/master/dataset_generation Room
impulse responses in shoebox-shaped rooms are simulated. The rooms have
a length, width and height randomly picked in the ranges
$`\llbracket 3;8\rrbracket`$ m, $`\llbracket 3;5\rrbracket`$ m and
$`\llbracket 2;3\rrbracket`$ m respectively. 2 sources, one target
source and one noise source, are randomly laid in the room. 4 nodes of 4
microphones each are randomly laid in the room and record the scene. The
only constraint is that the sources and the microphones should not be
closer than 50 cm from each other and from the walls.

In our experiments, the effects of [sro](#id38) and [sto](#id39) are
considered separately. Their joint impact is left for future work. To
simulate asynchronization, in each simulated configuration, one node
$`k`$ among the four is chosen as the reference node. Its [sr](#id42)
(resp. sampling start) is left unchanged, this is why we have
$`\epsilon_{k}=0`$ (resp. $`\tau_{k}=0`$) for this node. The
[sro](#id38) is simulated by resampling the signals of nodes $`j\neq k`$
at various sampling frequencies. The [sto](#id39) is simulated by
padding zeros at the beginning of the signals of nodes $`j\neq k`$.
Because of the symmetry of the [sro](#id38) and [sto](#id39) effects,
only positive [sro](#id38) and [sto](#id39) will be considered. For each
node $`j\neq k`$, the [sro](#id38) $`\epsilon_{j}`$ is randomly taken
between 0 parts per million (ppm) and a maximum value
$`\text{SRO}_{\text{max}}`$. 6 different values of
$`\text{SRO}_{\text{max}}`$ are considered, leading to 6 evaluation
conditions. The [sto](#id39) is randomly taken between 0 ms and a
maximum value $`\text{STO}_{\text{max}}`$. 5 different values of
$`\text{SRO}_{\text{max}}`$ are considered, leading to 5 evaluation
conditions. Because the signals of one node share the same hardware and
software implementation, we assume that they are synchronized. As a
consequence, asynchronization can only affect the second filtering step
of Tango.

## 4 Impact of asynchronization on [dnn](#id21)-based speech enhancement

In this section, the system described in section
[2.2](#S2.SS2 "2.2 Distributed speech enhancement in ad-hoc microphone arrays ‣ 2 Problem formulation ‣ SAMbA: Speech enhancement with Asynchronous ad-hoc Microphone Arrays")
is evaluated on the data described in section
[3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Training and evaluation data ‣ 3 Experimental setup ‣ SAMbA: Speech enhancement with Asynchronous ad-hoc Microphone Arrays").
However, the [mndnn](#id41) of the second filtering step are trained
with synchronized data: at train time, the compressed signals received
by a given node are perfectly synchronized with the mixtures recorded by
the receiving node.

is reported in

in terms of [sir](#id35), [sar](#id34) \[[25](#bib.bib25)\] and
[stoi](#id36) \[[26](#bib.bib26)\], where the bars represent the 95 %
confidence interval.

that the [sro](#id38) has a limited impact on the performance of our
system, even for high values of [sro](#id38). This is probably explained
by the fact that the signals are rather short, so that the effect of the
[sro](#id38) on the signals alignment is limited. Thus, given a rough
estimation of the [sro](#id38), resampling the signals with this
estimation at large intervals is enough to cope with [sro](#id38).

The impact of [sto](#id39) on our system is stronger. The [sir](#id35)
([sir](#id35)) seems robust to [sto](#id39), probably because of the
rank-1 decomposition of the [mwf](#id6). However, the other metrics,
especially the [stoi](#id36) ([stoi](#id36)), are sensitive to this kind
of asynchronization, in particular when the [sto](#id39) exceeds 16 ms,
corresponding to the duration of one frame.

As a conclusion of this section, asynchronization does have a negative
impact on our system, especially because of [sto](#id39). In the sequel,
we will therefore focus on the impact of [sto](#id39) on our distributed
speech enhancement system and consider that no [sro](#id38) affects the
recordings. A solution to compensate for [sto](#id39) without resampling
the signals is proposed in the next section.

## 5 Solution to asynchronization of the input signals of [dnn](#id21)

We propose to use an attention mechanism to compensate for the negative
impact of [sto](#id39) on our speech enhancement system. Since the
consequence of asynchronization is that the signals recorded on
different devices are not aligned in time, we propose to use an
alignment mechanism to implicitly shift the asynchronized signals
\[[27](#bib.bib27), [28](#bib.bib28)\]. To this effect, a temporal
alignment attention mechanism is used, which is inspired by the one
introduced by Schulze-Forster et al. in a different application
field \[[29](#bib.bib29)\]. It is described in the next section.

### 5.1 Temporal alignment attention mechanism

Let $`\mathbf{C}_{k}`$ be a reference channel and $`\mathbf{C}_{j}`$ an
input channel, both of size $`T\times F`$; let $`\mathbf{c}_{k}(m)`$ and
$`\mathbf{c}_{j}(n)`$ be their $`m`$-th and $`n`$-th column
respectively. A score between these two columns is computed as:

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\tilde{s}_{k,j}(m,n)=\mathbf{c}_{k}(m)\mathbf{W}{\mathbf{c}_{j}(n)}^{T}\,,
``` |  |

where $`\cdot^{T}`$ denotes the transpose operator and $`\mathbf{W}`$ is
a learnable matrix. In the sequel, we will always consider the first
channel of the [mndnn](#id41) as the reference channel, so we will drop
the index $`k`$. We have: $`\tilde{s}_{j}(m,n)=\tilde{s}_{k,j}(m,n)`$.
All the elements $`\tilde{s}_{j}(m,n)`$ are gathered in the matrix
$`\tilde{\mathbf{S}}_{j}`$ of size $`T\times T`$. A softmax operation is
applied on the rows of $`\tilde{\mathbf{S}}_{j}`$ to obtain the
so-called similarity matrix $`\mathbf{S}_{j}`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbf{S}_{j}=\text{softmax}\big(\tilde{\mathbf{S}}_{j}\big)\,.
``` |  | (1) |

The idea of this mechanism is that $`\mathbf{S}_{j}`$ should contain the
probability of the frames of $`\mathbf{C}_{j}`$ being aligned with the
frames of the reference channel $`\mathbf{C}_{1}`$. These probabilities
are multiplied with $`\{\mathbf{c}_{1}(i)\}_{i=1..T}`$, the columns of
$`\mathbf{C}_{1}`$ following:

|     |                                                                |     |
|-----|----------------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \mathbf{p}_{j}(m)=\sum_{i=1}^{T}s_{j}(m,i)\mathbf{c}_{1}(i)\,.
       ```                                                             |     |

The output matrix $`\mathbf{P}_{j}`$ of columns
$`\{\mathbf{p}_{j}(m)\}_{m=1..T}`$ is concatenated with the input matrix
$`\mathbf{C}_{j}`$ over the frequency axis.

### 5.2 Integration of the temporal alignment mechanism in Tango

The previously described attention mechanism is used at the input of the
[crnn](#id26). Since only the second filtering step is affected by
asynchronization, they are integrated into the [mndnn](#id41) only. The
new architecture is represented in
Figure [3](#S5.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 5.2 Integration of the temporal alignment mechanism in Tango ‣ 5 Solution to asynchronization of the input signals of ‣ SAMbA: Speech enhancement with Asynchronous ad-hoc Microphone Arrays").
Since the input data has twice more features on the frequency axis
compared to the initial [mndnn](#id41), the last maximum-pooling layer
has a kernel size 8$`\times`$1 to keep the size of the GRU layer the
same. On each node, the first channel, corresponding to the local
mixture, is taken as the reference channel.

### 5.3 Quantitative evaluation and analysis

Three systems are compared to evaluate our solution. The first system is
the same as in
section [4](#S4 "4 Impact of asynchronization on -based speech enhancement ‣ SAMbA: Speech enhancement with Asynchronous ad-hoc Microphone Arrays"),
where the [mndnn](#id41) are trained on synchronized data only. The
second system has [mndnn](#id41) trained on asynchronized data. The
third system has [mndnn](#id41) with the attention mechanism, trained on
asynchronized data. In the asynchronous training set, the [sro](#id38)
is set to 0 ppm and the [sto](#id39) is randomly taken between 0 ms and
32 ms. During evaluation, the [sto](#id39) is randomly taken between
0 ms and a maximum value $`\text{STO}_{\text{max}}`$. The same values of
$`\text{SRO}_{\text{max}}`$ as in Section
[4](#S4 "4 Impact of asynchronization on -based speech enhancement ‣ SAMbA: Speech enhancement with Asynchronous ad-hoc Microphone Arrays")
are considered. The results obtained with these three systems are
represented in
Figure [4](#S5.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 5.3 Quantitative evaluation and analysis ‣ 5 Solution to asynchronization of the input signals of ‣ SAMbA: Speech enhancement with Asynchronous ad-hoc Microphone Arrays").

\includegraphics

\[width=\]rp_0kOr-hK3W-P6sG_sto

Figure 4: Speech enhancement performance of three different systems.

The first conclusion from this experiment is that training the
[mndnn](#id41) in matching conditions brings robustness to the system in
terms of [sar](#id34) ([sar](#id34)) and [stoi](#id36). However, it does
not have any significant impact on the [sir](#id35). With the attention
mechanism, even if the differences are not significant, there is a
noticeable improvement in terms of [sir](#id35) over the system where
the [mndnn](#id41) are trained in matched conditions but without
attention mechanism. This experiment confirms that this attention
mechanism is adapted to the misalignment problem, and that it makes our
speech enhancement system robust to asynchronization. Another advantage
of using such a mechanism is introduced in the next section.

## 6 Conclusions

We addressed the issue of asynchronization in a distributed speech
enhancement system based on [dnn](#id21). We showed that [sro](#id38)
had a limited impact on our experiments, but that the influence of
[sto](#id39) was detrimental to the speech enhancement performance of
our system. To cope with it, we introduced a temporal alignment
attention mechanism that makes the [dnn](#id21) of our system robust to
[sto](#id39). In addition, we show that the hidden values of the
attention mechanism can be interpreted and that they lead to a coarse
estimation of the [sto](#id39) at all nodes. We believe that our work
introduces a novel and interesting use of attention mechanisms for
speech enhancement in ad-hoc microphone arrays. It would be interesting
to apply this kind of attention mechanisms on signals in the time domain
rather than in the time-frequency domain, where they would probably lead
to more precise results and higher performance.
````
