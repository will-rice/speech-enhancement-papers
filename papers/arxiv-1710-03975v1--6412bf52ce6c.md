###### Abstract

The goal in speech enhancement is to obtain an estimate of clean speech
starting from the noisy signal by minimizing a chosen distortion
measure, which results in an estimate that depends on the unknown clean
signal or its statistics. Since access to such prior knowledge is
limited or not possible in practice, one has to estimate the clean
signal statistics. In this paper, we develop a new risk minimization
framework for speech enhancement, in which, one optimizes an unbiased
estimate of the distortion/risk instead of the actual risk. The
estimated risk is expressed solely as a function of the noisy
observations. We consider several perceptually relevant distortion
measures and develop corresponding unbiased estimates under realistic
assumptions on the noise distribution and a priori signal-to-noise ratio
(SNR). Minimizing the risk estimates gives rise to the corresponding
denoisers, which are nonlinear functions of the a posteriori SNR.
Perceptual evaluation of speech quality (PESQ), average segmental SNR
(SSNR) computations, and listening tests show that the proposed risk
optimization approach employing Itakura-Saito and weighted hyperbolic
cosine distortions gives better performance than the other distortion
measures. For SNRs greater than $`5`$ dB, the proposed approach gives
superior denoising performance over the benchmark techniques based on
the Wiener filter, log-MMSE minimization, and Bayesian nonnegative
matrix factorization.  
_Index Terms_ — Speech enhancement, perceptual distortion measure,
unbiased risk estimation, Stein’s lemma, objective and subjective
assessment.

## I Introduction

The goal in speech enhancement is to suppress noise and enhance signal
intelligibility and quality. Over the past few decades, several
techniques have been developed for noise suppression. The challenges are
nonstationarity of the speech signal, distribution of noise, type of
noise distortion, noise being signal-dependent or independent, etc. The
problem continues to be of significant interest to the speech community
particularly considering the enormous increase in the number of
smartphone users. An early review of various noise reduction techniques
was given by Lim and Oppenheim \[[1](#bib.bib1)\]. Loizou’s book on
speech enhancement \[[2](#bib.bib2)\] is a recent and comprehensive
reference on the topic. We shall briefly review related literature
before proceeding with the development of the new risk optimization
framework for speech enhancement.

### I-A Related Literature

Speech enhancement techniques can be classified as follows.

#### I-A1 Spectral subtraction algorithms

Boll \[[3](#bib.bib3)\] and Weiss et al. \[[4](#bib.bib4)\] proposed to
subtract an estimate of the noise power spectrum from the noisy signal
spectrum, in order to estimate the clean signal spectrum. The assumption
is that the noise is additive and stationary. The noisy signal phase is
used in reconstructing the time-domain signal. Weiss et al.
\[[4](#bib.bib4)\] also proposed subtraction techniques in
autocorrelation and cepstral domains. Lockwood and Boudy
\[[5](#bib.bib5)\], Kamath and Loizou \[[6](#bib.bib6)\] proposed
improved versions of spectral subtraction algorithms.

#### I-A2 Wiener filtering techniques

These are based on the minimum mean-squared error (MMSE) criterion
\[[2](#bib.bib2)\], in which one constructs the Wiener filter using an
estimate of the clean and noisy speech power spectra. Lim and Oppenheim
\[[1](#bib.bib1)\] proposed a parametric Wiener filter, which allows for
controlling the trade-off between the signal distortion and residual
noise. Hu and Loizou incorporated psychoacoustic constraints into this
framework \[[7](#bib.bib7), [8](#bib.bib8)\]. In \[[7](#bib.bib7)\],
they use a perceptual weighting filter to shape the residual noise to
make it inaudible. In \[[8](#bib.bib8)\], they constrain the noise
spectrum to lie below a preset threshold at each frequency. Chen et al.
\[[9](#bib.bib9)\] quantified the amount of noise reduction and analyzed
its relation to speech distortion. The Wiener filter requires an
estimate of the a priori signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). Scalart and Filho
\[[10](#bib.bib10)\] used a recursive a priori SNR estimator whereas Lim
and Oppenheim \[[11](#bib.bib11)\] iteratively estimated the Wiener
filter based on autoregressive modeling of the speech signal. Hansen and
Clements \[[12](#bib.bib12)\] imposed inter- and intra-frame constraints
to ensure speech-like characteristics within each iteration. Sreenivas
and Kirnapure \[[13](#bib.bib13)\] proposed a codebook constrained,
iterative Wiener filter with superior convergence behavior. Srinivasan
et al. \[[14](#bib.bib14), [15](#bib.bib15)\] proposed
maximum-likelihood and Bayesian methods for estimating the speech and
noise power spectra. Rosenkranz and Puder \[[16](#bib.bib16)\] proposed
adaptation techniques to improve the performance of the codebook
approaches reported in \[[14](#bib.bib14), [15](#bib.bib15)\] against
model mismatches and unknown noise types.

#### I-A3 Subspace techniques

Originally proposed by Ephraim and Van Trees, subspace techniques rely
on eigenvalue decomposition of the data covariance matrix
\[[17](#bib.bib17), [18](#bib.bib18), [19](#bib.bib19),
[20](#bib.bib20)\] or singular-value decomposition of the data matrix
\[[21](#bib.bib21), [22](#bib.bib22), [23](#bib.bib23)\]. The noise
eigenvalues/singular values are smaller than those of the noisy signal
and denoising happens when the signal is reconstructed from the
eigen/singular vectors corresponding to the signal subspace alone.
Jabloun and Champagne \[[24](#bib.bib24)\] incorporated properties of
human audition into the signal subspace approaches.

#### I-A4 Statistical model based methods

McAulay and Malpass \[[25](#bib.bib25)\] proposed a maximum-likelihood
(ML) estimator of the clean speech short-time Fourier transform (STFT)
magnitude. The clean speech spectra are assumed to be deterministic and
the noise is modeled as zero-mean complex Gaussian. The ML estimate of
the magnitude spectrum is combined with the noisy phase spectrum in
order to reconstruct the speech signal. Ephraim and Malah
\[[26](#bib.bib26)\] proposed a Bayesian MMSE estimator of the
short-time spectral amplitude (STSA) by assuming speech and noise to be
statistically independent, zero-mean, complex Gaussian random variables.
McCallum and Guillemin \[[27](#bib.bib27)\] proposed an MMSE-STSA
estimator assuming a nonzero-mean speech signal. Ephraim
\[[28](#bib.bib28)\] used hidden Markov model (HMMs) to model the
dynamics of speech and noise processes. Erkelens et al.
\[[29](#bib.bib29)\] proposed MMSE estimators of clean speech discrete
Fourier transform (DFT) coefficients and DFT magnitudes assuming
generalized Gamma distributions on speech. Kundu et al.
\[[30](#bib.bib30)\] developed an MMSE estimator by using a Gaussian
mixture model (GMM) for the clean speech signal. Lotter and Vary
\[[31](#bib.bib31)\] proposed a maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimator
assuming super-Gaussian statistics. Ephraim and Malah
\[[32](#bib.bib32)\] proposed an estimator that minimizes the MSE of
log-magnitude spectra as it is perceptually more correlated. Loizou
\[[33](#bib.bib33)\] computed Bayesian estimators for magnitude spectrum
using perceptual distortion metrics such as the Itakura-Saito
distortion, hyperbolic-cosine distortion, etc. Mohammadiha et al.
\[[34](#bib.bib34)\] use a Bayesian non-negative matrix factorization
(NMF) approach to obtain an MMSE estimate of the the clean speech DFT
magnitude.

### I-B Our Contributions

We introduce the notion of risk estimation for speech denoising. The
clean speech signal is considered to be deterministic and the random
noise to be additive (Section
[II](#S2 "II Problem Formulation ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement")).
Direct minimization of the risk results in estimates that are a function
of the deterministic clean speech signal. Considering a transform-domain
Gaussian observation model, one can develop an unbiased estimate of the
MSE based on Stein’s lemma \[[35](#bib.bib35)\], which is referred to as
Stein’s unbiased risk estimator (SURE) in the literature
\[[36](#bib.bib36), [37](#bib.bib37)\]. The main advantage is that,
unlike MSE, SURE does not require knowledge of the unknown deterministic
clean signal. The state-of-the-art image denoising techniques are based
on risk minimization \[[36](#bib.bib36), [37](#bib.bib37)\] considering
the MSE. In this paper, we solve the speech denoising problem within the
framework of unbiased risk estimation, where we derive unbiased
estimates of speech-specific perceptual distortion measures and minimize
them to obtain the corresponding shrinkage functions. Distortion
measures such as Itakura-Saito, hyperbolic-cosine (cosh), weighted cosh,
etc. are considered as they are more perceptually relevant than MSE
\[[38](#bib.bib38)\].  
Further, in practice, real-world disturbances generate bounded noise
amplitudes and quantization limits the dynamic range. Therefore, we
consider the more realistic case of a truncated Gaussian distribution
for the samples. The details will be described in Section
[II](#S2 "II Problem Formulation ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement").
In order to develop a risk estimator, we make use of Stein’s lemma and
its higher-order generalization, originally proposed for Gaussian noise
(Section
[III](#S3 "III Risk Estimation Results ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement")).
The higher-order generalization becomes important in the context of
perceptual distortion measures. Correspondingly, we develop the notion
of perceptual risk optimization for speech enhancement (PROSE) (Section
[IV](#S4 "IV Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement (PROSE) ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement")).
The key advantage of the PROSE framework is that it allows one to
replace an ensemble-averaged distortion measure by a practically viable
surrogate. We employ a transform-domain point-wise shrinkage, which is
nonlinear in the observations. We also consider parametric versions,
which give additional flexibility to trade-off between residual noise
level and speech distortion. It turns out that perceptually optimized
denoising functions result in more noise attenuation than MSE. We also
carry out objective assessment in terms of average segmental
signal-to-noise ratio (SSNR), global SNR, perceptual evaluation of
speech quality (PESQ) \[[39](#bib.bib39)\], short-time objective
intelligibility (STOI) \[[40](#bib.bib40)\], and subjective assessment
by means of listening tests and scoring as per ITU-T recommendations
\[[41](#bib.bib41)\] (Section
[VI](#S6 "VI Experimental Results ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement")).

## II Problem Formulation

Consider a short-time frame of noisy speech in which samples of clean
speech $`s_{n}`$ are distorted additively by noise $`w_{n}`$ resulting
in the observations:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       x_{n}=s_{n}+w_{n}\,,n=1,2,\cdots,N,
       ```                                  |     | (1) |

where $`N`$ is the frame length. The signal samples $`\{s_{n}\}`$ are
assumed to be deterministic and noise samples $`\{w_{n}\}`$ to be
zero-mean, bounded, i.i.d. random variables. Most real-world noise
processes are bounded and the presence of a quantizer in a practical
data acquisition scenario further justifies the assumption.
Consequently, $`\{x_{n}\}`$ are bounded, and
$`\mathcal{E}\{x_{n}\}=s_{n}`$, which implies that $`\{x_{n}\}`$ are
independent, but not identically distributed. The time-domain noise
distribution is not restricted to be a Gaussian. Typical speech
enhancement approaches work on the DFT magnitudes \[[2](#bib.bib2)\],
and the phase is left unaltered. We prefer the discrete cosine transform
(DCT) as it is real-valued and known to give rise to a more parsimonious
representation than the DFT \[[42](#bib.bib42)\]. Further, Soon et al.
established that, for shrinkage estimators, DCT-domain denoising is
superior to DFT \[[42](#bib.bib42)\]. Since our point-wise
multiplicative shrinkage estimator belongs to this class, we prefer the
DCT to DFT. The DCT representation of
([1](#S2.E1 "In II Problem Formulation ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement"))
is

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       X_{k}=S_{k}+W_{k},\quad k=1,2,\cdot\cdot\cdot,N,
       ```                                               |     | (2) |

where the transform-domain noise $`\{W_{k}\}`$ being a linear
combination of i.i.d. random variables $`\{w_{n}\}`$ has a distribution
that approaches a Gaussian by virtue of the central limit theorem.
However, since $`\{w_{n}\}`$ are bounded, $`\{W_{k}\}`$ will also be
bounded. These two properties taken together make a truncated Gaussian
distribution model more appropriate and realistic for the transform
coefficients than the standard Gaussian. The noisy samples are
concentrated about the mean and the deviations from the mean are
bounded. The suitability of the truncated Gaussian for modeling
real-world processes has been advocated by Burkardt
\[[43](#bib.bib43)\].  
The goal is to estimate $`S_{k}`$ given $`X_{k}`$ and noise statistics.
Let $`d(S_{k},\widehat{S}_{k})`$ denote a distortion measure that
quantifies the deviation of the estimate $`\widehat{S}_{k}`$ from
$`S_{k}`$. The corresponding ensemble averaged distortion or risk, as
referred to in the statistics literature, is defined as
$`\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{E}\left\{d(S_{k},\widehat{S}_{k})\right\},`$
where $`{\cal E}`$ denotes the expectation operator. The estimate is
expressed as $`{\hat{S}_{k}}=f(X_{k})`$, where $`f`$ is the denoising
function, which may not always be linear. We consider point-wise
shrinkage, $`f(X_{k})=a_{k}X_{k},a_{k}\in\left[0,1\right]`$, and
optimize an estimate of $`\mathcal{R}`$ with respect to $`a_{k}`$.  
A block diagram representation of the proposed method is shown in Figure
[1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ I-B Our Contributions ‣ I Introduction ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement").
To take into account the quasi-stationarity of speech, denoising is
performed on a frame-by-frame basis and the enhanced speech is
reconstructed using the standard overlap-add synthesis methodology.

## III Risk Estimation Results

We recall a key result from \[[35](#bib.bib35)\], which is central to
the subsequent developments

###### Lemma 1

(Stein, 1981) Let $`W`$ be a $`{\cal N}(0,\sigma^{2})`$ real random
variable and let $`f:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}`$ be an indefinite
integral of the Lebesgue measurable function $`f^{(1)}`$, essentially
the derivative of $`f`$. Suppose also that
$`\mathcal{E}\{|f^{(1)}(W)|\}<\infty`$. Then,
$`\mathcal{E}\{Wf(W)\}=\sigma^{2}\mathcal{E}\{f^{(1)}(W)\}.`$

Stein’s lemma facilitates estimation of the mean of $`Wf(W)`$ in terms
of $`f^{(1)}(W)`$. Effectively, $`\sigma^{2}\,f^{(1)}(W)`$ could be used
as an unbiased estimate of $`\mathcal{E}\{Wf(W)\}`$. The implications of
this apparently simple result can be appreciated when we are required to
compute an unbiased estimator of the MSE. Next, we develop a
higher-order generalization of Stein’s lemma.

###### Lemma 2

(Generalized Stein’s lemma) Let $`W`$ be a $`{\cal N}(0,\sigma^{2})`$
real random variable and let $`f:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}`$ be an
$`n`$-fold indefinite integral of the Lebesgue measurable function
$`f^{(n)}`$, which is the $`n^{th}`$ derivative of $`f`$. Suppose also
that
$`\mathcal{E}\left\{|W^{(n+1-k)}f^{(k)}(W)|\right\}<\infty,k=1,2,\cdots,n`$.
Then

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\mathcal{E}\{W^{n+1}f(W)\}=\sigma^{2}\mathcal{E}\{f^{(1)}(W)W^{n}\}+\sigma^{2}\,n\mathcal{E}\{f(W)W^{n-1}\}.
``` |  |

###### Proof:

Let us express $`\mathcal{E}\{W^{n+1}f(W)\}`$ as
$`\mathcal{E}\{Wg(W)\},`$ where $`g(W)=W^{n}f(W)`$. Applying Stein’s
lemma to $`\mathcal{E}\{Wg(W)\}`$, we get that

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{E}\{W^{n+1}f(W)\}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\mathcal{E}\{Wg(W)\}=\sigma^{2}\mathcal{E}\{g^{(1)}(W)\},`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}\mathcal{E}\{f^{(1)}(W)W^{n}\}+\sigma^{2}\,n\mathcal{E}\{f(W)W^{n-1}\}.`$ |  |

∎Stein’s lemma could be applied recursively to each of the terms on the
right-hand side, up to a stage where the terms comprise only the
derivatives of all orders of $`f`$ up to $`n`$.
Our next set of results is in the context of developing Stein-type
lemmas for the practical case of a truncated Gaussian distribution.

###### Lemma 3

Let $`W`$ be a real random variable with probability density function
(p.d.f.)

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
p\left(w;c,\sigma\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma K}\exp\left(-\frac{w^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)\mathds{1}_{\{w<|c\sigma|\}},
``` |  | (3) |

where $`K`$ ensures that $`p`$ integrates to unity,
$`c\in\mathbb{R}^{+}`$, and $`\mathds{1}`$ denotes the indicator
function. Let $`f:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}`$ be an indefinite
integral of the Lebesgue measurable function $`f^{(1)}`$. Suppose also
that $`\mathcal{E}\{|f^{(1)}\left(W\right)|\}<\infty`$ and $`f`$ does
not grow faster than an exponential, then
$`\mathcal{E}\{Wf\left(W\right)\}=\sigma^{2}\mathcal{E}\{f^{(1)}\left(W\right)\}+\mathcal{O}\{\exp(-c^{2})\}`$.

###### Proof:

Using the property:
$`\displaystyle-\sigma^{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}\exp\left(-\frac{w^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)}{\mathrm{d}w}=w\exp\left(-\frac{w^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)`$,
we write

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{E}\{Wf\left(W\right)\}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}wf(w)p\left(w;c,\sigma\right)\mathrm{d}w,`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=-\int_{-c\sigma}^{+c\sigma}\sigma^{2}f(w)\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma K}\frac{\mathrm{d}\exp\left(-\frac{w^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)}{\mathrm{d}w}\mathrm{d}w,`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=-\sigma^{2}f(w)p\left(w;c,\sigma\right)\Big|_{-c\sigma}^{+c\sigma}`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt+\int_{-c\sigma}^{+c\sigma}\sigma^{2}\,f^{(1)}(w)p\left(w;c,\sigma\right)\mathrm{d}w`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\{\exp(-c^{2})\}+\sigma^{2}\mathcal{E}\left\{f^{(1)}(W)\right\}.`$ |  |

∎For even $`f`$, the approximation error is zero. Next, we state the
counterpart of Lemma
[2](#S3.Ex1 "Lemma 2 ‣ III Risk Estimation Results ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement")
for truncated Gaussian distribution, which has a similar proof
mechanism.

###### Lemma 4

Let $`W`$ be a real random variable with p.d.f
$`p\left(w;c,\sigma\right)`$ and let
$`f:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}`$ be an $`n`$-fold indefinite
integral of the Lebesgue measurable function $`f^{(n)}`$, which is the
$`n^{th}`$ derivative of $`f`$. Suppose also that
$`\mathcal{E}\left\{|W^{(n+1-k)}f^{(k)}(W)|\right\}<\infty,k=1,2,\cdots,n`$
and $`f^{(k)}`$ does not grow faster than an exponential, then

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{E}\{W^{n+1}f\left(W\right)\}=\sigma^{2}\mathcal{E}\{f^{(1)}(W)W^{n}\}`$ | $`\displaystyle+\sigma^{2}\,n\mathcal{E}\{f\left(W\right)W^{n-1}\}`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle+\mathcal{O}\{\exp(-c^{2})\}.`$ |  |

The approximation error is negligible for large values of $`c`$. These
results will be handy in computing risk estimators for various
perceptual distortion measures.

## IV Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement (PROSE)

### IV-A Mean-Square Error (MSE)

The squared error is the most commonly employed distortion measure
largely because of ease of optimization. The distortion function for
squared error in the transform domain is

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
d(S_{k},\widehat{S}_{k})=\left(\widehat{S}_{k}-S_{k}\right)^{2},\,\,\,\mbox{where}\,\,\,\widehat{S}_{k}=f(X_{k}).
``` |  |

The MSE is $`\mathcal{R}={\cal E}\{d(S_{k},\widehat{S}_{k})\}`$, which
may be expanded as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{E}\left\{f^{2}(X_{k})+S_{k}^{2}-2f(X_{k})X_{k}+2f(X_{k})W_{k}\right\}.
``` |  | (4) |

Applying Lemma
[3](#Thmthm3 "Lemma 3 ‣ Proof: ‣ III Risk Estimation Results ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement")
gives
$`\mathcal{E}\{f(X_{k})W_{k}\}\approx\sigma^{2}\mathcal{E}\{f^{(1)}(X_{k})\}`$,
and from
([4](#S4.E4 "In IV-A Mean-Square Error (MSE) ‣ IV Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement (PROSE) ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement")),
we get that

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{R}\approx\mathcal{E}\{f^{2}(X_{k})-2f(X_{k})X_{k}+2\sigma^{2}f^{(1)}(X_{k})\}+S_{k}^{2},
``` |  |

from which it can be concluded that

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
{\widehat{\cal R}}=f^{2}(X_{k})-2f(X_{k})X_{k}+2\sigma^{2}f^{(1)}(X_{k})+S_{k}^{2},
``` |  | (5) |

is a nearly unbiased estimator of the MSE. Although
$`{\widehat{\cal R}}`$ contains the signal term $`S_{k}^{2}`$, it does
not affect the minimization with respect to $`f`$. Consider the
point-wise shrinkage estimator $`f(X_{k})=a_{k}X_{k}`$, where
$`a_{k}\in\left[0,1\right]`$. The optimum value of $`a_{k}`$ is obtained
by minimizing $`\widehat{{\cal R}}`$ subject to the constraint
$`a_{k}\in\left[0,1\right]`$. The Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions
\[[44](#bib.bib44), pp. 211\] for solving this problem are given in
Appendix
[A](#A1 "Appendix A Karush-Kuhn-Tucker Conditions ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement")
(cf.
([26a](#Ax1.E26.1 "In 26 ‣ F-A The Optimization Problem and KKT Conditions ‣ Karush-Kuhn-Tucker Conditions and Optimization of Perceptual Distortion Measures Supporting Document ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement"))
–
([26e](#Ax1.E26.5 "In 26 ‣ F-A The Optimization Problem and KKT Conditions ‣ Karush-Kuhn-Tucker Conditions and Optimization of Perceptual Distortion Measures Supporting Document ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement"))).
The optimum $`a_{k}`$ that satisfies the KKT conditions¹¹ 1 The
calculations related to the constrained optimization of all the
perceptual risk estimates considered in this paper are provided in the
supporting document. is given by

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
a_{k}=\text{max}\left\{1-\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{2}}{X_{k}^{2}},0\right\}.
``` |  |

The optimum shrinkage estimator becomes:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\widehat{S}_{k}=\text{max}\left\{1-\displaystyle\frac{1}{\xi_{k}},0\right\}X_{k}=a_{\text{MSE}}(\xi_{k})X_{k},
``` |  |

where $`\xi_{k}=\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{X_{k}^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}`$
denotes the a posteriori SNR determined based on the noisy signal, and
$`a_{\text{MSE}}(\xi_{k})`$ denotes the MSE-related shrinkage function.
To impart additional flexibility, we consider parametric refinements,
which allow us to trade-off between residual noise level and speech
distortion. It is also useful when estimates of the noise variance may
not be sufficiently accurate. The parametrically refined version is
given by

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\widehat{S}_{k}=\text{max}\left\{1-\displaystyle\frac{\alpha}{\xi_{k}},0\right\}X_{k},
``` |  | (6) |

where $`\alpha`$ is the parameter, akin to the over-subtraction factor
in spectral subtraction algorithms.

### IV-B Weighted Euclidean (WE) Distortion

The MSE is perceptually less relevant for speech signals since a large
MSE does not always imply poor signal quality. Auditory masking effects
render humans more robust to errors at spectral peaks than at the
valleys and are taken advantage of in speech/audio compression. One way
to introduce differential weighting to spectral peaks and valleys is to
consider the weighted Euclidean (WE) distortion:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
d(S_{k},\widehat{S}_{k})=\frac{\left(\widehat{S}_{k}-S_{k}\right)^{2}}{S_{k}},\mbox{where}\,\widehat{S}_{k}=f(X_{k}).
``` |  |

The measure $`d<0`$ when $`S_{k}<0`$, but this is not a problem, because
in that case, the cost function must be maximized and not minimized. We
shall see that the proposed methodology implicitly takes care of this
aspect. Developing $`d`$, we get

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
d(S_{k},\widehat{S}_{k})=\frac{\widehat{S}^{2}_{k}}{S_{k}}+S_{k}-2\widehat{S}_{k}.
``` |  |

Let us consider a high-SNR scenario, that is,
$`\left|\frac{W_{k}}{X_{k}}\right|<1`$. This event occurs with
probability 1 if $`|S_{k}|>2c\sigma`$ (cf. Appendix
[B](#A2 "Appendix B The High-SNR Scenario ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement")).
This condition also implies that $`X_{k}`$ and $`S_{k}`$ have the same
sign. In this scenario, the first term is expanded as

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\frac{\widehat{S}^{2}_{k}}{S_{k}}=\frac{\widehat{S}^{2}_{k}}{X_{k}}\left(1-\frac{W_{k}}{X_{k}}\right)^{-1}=\frac{\widehat{S}^{2}_{k}}{X_{k}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{W_{k}}{X_{k}}\right)^{n},
``` |  |

and the distortion function is rewritten as

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
d(S_{k},\widehat{S}_{k})=\frac{\widehat{S}^{2}_{k}}{X_{k}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{W_{k}}{X_{k}}\right)^{n}+S_{k}-2\widehat{S}_{k}.
``` |  |

We truncate the infinite sum beyond the fourth-order, in order to
maintain high accuracy in the calculations:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
d(S_{k},\widehat{S}_{k})\approx\frac{\widehat{S}^{2}_{k}}{X_{k}}\sum_{n=0}^{4}\left(\frac{W_{k}}{X_{k}}\right)^{n}+S_{k}-2\widehat{S}_{k}.
``` |  |

Considering $`f(X_{k})=a_{k}X_{k}`$, we seek to minimize the risk
$`\mathcal{R}`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{E}\left\{a_{k}^{2}X_{k}\sum_{n=0}^{4}\left(\frac{W_{k}}{X_{k}}\right)^{n}\right\}+S_{k}-2\mathcal{E}\{a_{k}X_{k}\}.
``` |  | (7) |

To proceed further, we are required to compute expectations of
reciprocals of truncated Gaussian random variables, which may not always
be finite. A necessary and sufficient condition for the expectations to
be finite is that $`|S_{k}|>c\sigma`$, which is satisfied in the
high-SNR regime. This is an added benefit of working with more realistic
distributions such as the truncated Gaussian.
A simplification for the expectation of the sum appearing in the first
term of
([7](#S4.E7 "In IV-B Weighted Euclidean (WE) Distortion ‣ IV Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement (PROSE) ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement"))
could be made by invoking *Lemma*
[4](#S3.EGx3 "Lemma 4 ‣ Proof: ‣ Proof: ‣ III Risk Estimation Results ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement")
(cf. Appendix
[C](#A3 "Appendix C Weighted Euclidean Distance ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement")).
Consequently, we get

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{R}`$ | $`\displaystyle=a_{k}^{2}\mathcal{E}\left\{X_{k}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{X_{k}}-\frac{\sigma^{4}}{X_{k}^{3}}+48\frac{\sigma^{6}}{X_{k}^{5}}+360\frac{\sigma^{8}}{X_{k}^{7}}-2\frac{X_{k}}{a_{k}}\right\}+S_{k}.`$ |  |

The corresponding unbiased risk estimator is obtained as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
{\widehat{\cal R}}=a_{k}^{2}\Big(X_{k}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{X_{k}}-\frac{\sigma^{4}}{X_{k}^{3}}+48\frac{\sigma^{6}}{X_{k}^{5}}+360\frac{\sigma^{8}}{X_{k}^{7}}\Big)-2a_{k}X_{k}+S_{k}.
``` |  | (8) |

The goal is to determine $`a_{k}\in[0,1]`$ such that
$`{\widehat{\cal R}}`$ is minimized if $`S_{k}>0`$ and maximized if
$`S_{k}<0`$. Solving the KKT conditions
([26a](#Ax1.E26.1 "In 26 ‣ F-A The Optimization Problem and KKT Conditions ‣ Karush-Kuhn-Tucker Conditions and Optimization of Perceptual Distortion Measures Supporting Document ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement"))$`-`$([26e](#Ax1.E26.5 "In 26 ‣ F-A The Optimization Problem and KKT Conditions ‣ Karush-Kuhn-Tucker Conditions and Optimization of Perceptual Distortion Measures Supporting Document ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement"))
corresponding to these two scenarios results in the same optimum
$`a_{k}`$. The corresponding estimator is given by

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\widehat{S}_{k}=\underbrace{\left(1+\displaystyle\frac{1}{\xi_{k}}-\displaystyle\frac{1}{\xi_{k}^{2}}+\displaystyle\frac{48}{\xi_{k}^{3}}+\displaystyle\frac{360}{\xi_{k}^{4}}\right)^{-1}}_{a_{\text{WE}}(\xi_{k})}X_{k},
``` |  |

where $`a_{\text{WE}}(\xi_{k})`$ is the shrinkage factor. Akin to
([6](#S4.E6 "In IV-A Mean-Square Error (MSE) ‣ IV Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement (PROSE) ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement")),
one can define parametric counterparts of $`a_{\text{WE}}`$ by replacing
$`\xi_{k}`$ with $`\xi_{k}/\alpha`$. Figure
[2](#S4.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ IV-C Logarithmic Mean-Square Error (log MSE) ‣ IV Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement (PROSE) ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement")(a)
compares $`a_{\text{MSE}}`$ and $`a_{\text{WE}}`$ for different values
of $`\alpha`$. We observe that the attenuation provided by
$`a_{\text{MSE}}`$ is considerably smaller than $`a_{\text{WE}}`$ when
the a posteriori SNR is greater than zero dB. This indicates that, in
the high-SNR regime, minimizing the weighted Euclidean distortion
results in higher noise attenuation than the MSE. As $`\alpha`$
increases, the shrinkage curves shift to the right, implying more
attenuation for a given a posteriori SNR.

### IV-C Logarithmic Mean-Square Error (log MSE)

Since loudness perception of the peripheral auditory system is
logarithmic, one could compare speech spectra by computing the MSE on a
logarithmic scale. This property has been used to advantage in vector
quantization and speech coding applications \[[38](#bib.bib38)\]. The
log MSE between $`S_{k}`$ and $`{\widehat{S}_{k}}`$ is given by

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle d(S_{k},\widehat{S}_{k})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\Big(\log\frac{\widehat{S}_{k}}{S_{k}}\Big)^{2},`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=(\log\widehat{S}_{k})^{2}+(\log S_{k})^{2}-2\log S_{k}\log\widehat{S}_{k}.`$ |  | (9) |

Recall that we consider only non-negative shrinkage functions for
denoising, and that under the high SNR assumption both
$`\widehat{S}_{k}`$ and $`S_{k}`$ have the same sign. Consequently, the
argument of the logarithm is always positive. The last term in
([9](#S4.E9 "In IV-C Logarithmic Mean-Square Error (log MSE) ‣ IV Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement (PROSE) ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement"))
is rewritten as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\log\widehat{S}_{k}\log S_{k}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\log\widehat{S}_{k}\log(X_{k}-W_{k}),(\text{from \eqref{xk}}),`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\log\widehat{S}_{k}\log X_{k}+\log\widehat{S}_{k}\log\left(1-\displaystyle\frac{W_{k}}{X_{k}}\right),`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\log\widehat{S}_{k}\log X_{k}-\log\widehat{S}_{k}\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\left(\displaystyle\frac{W_{k}}{X_{k}}\right)^{n}.`$ |  |

Substituting in
([9](#S4.E9 "In IV-C Logarithmic Mean-Square Error (log MSE) ‣ IV Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement (PROSE) ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement")),
truncating the series beyond $`n=4`$, and considering the expectation
results in

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{E}\left\{(\log\widehat{S}_{k})^{2}+(\log S_{k})^{2}-2\log\widehat{S}_{k}\log X_{k}\right\}`$ |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle+\mathcal{E}\left\{2\log\widehat{S}_{k}\displaystyle{\sum_{n=1}^{4}}\displaystyle{\frac{1}{n}}\left(\displaystyle{\frac{W_{k}}{X_{k}}}\right)^{n}\right\}.`$ |  |

For the shrinkage estimate $`\widehat{S}_{k}=a_{k}X_{k}`$, the risk
becomes

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{E}`$ | $`\displaystyle\left\{(\log a_{k}X_{k})^{2}+(\log S_{k})^{2}\right\}-2\mathcal{E}\left\{\log a_{k}X_{k}\log X_{k}\right\}`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle+2\mathcal{E}\left\{\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{4}\displaystyle\frac{\log a_{k}X_{k}}{n}\left(\displaystyle\frac{W_{k}}{X_{k}}\right)^{n}\right\}.`$ |  |

The last term may be simplified as shown in Appendix
[D](#A4 "Appendix D Log Mean-Square Error ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement"),
using *Lemma*
[4](#S3.EGx3 "Lemma 4 ‣ Proof: ‣ Proof: ‣ III Risk Estimation Results ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement").
Consequently, the unbiased risk estimator is

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle{\widehat{\cal R}}=`$ | $`\displaystyle(\log S_{k})^{2}+(\log a_{k}X_{k})^{2}-2\log a_{k}X_{k}\log X_{k}`$ |  |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle+2`$ | $`\displaystyle\left(\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{2}}{X_{k}^{2}}-1.5\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{4}}{X_{k}^{4}}+2.17\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{6}}{X_{k}^{6}}-159.5\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{8}}{X_{k}^{8}}\right)`$ |  |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle-2`$ | $`\displaystyle\log a_{k}X_{k}\left(0.5\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{2}}{X_{k}^{2}}-0.75\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{4}}{X_{k}^{4}}-10\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{6}}{X_{k}^{6}}-210\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{8}}{X_{k}^{8}}\right).`$ |  | (10) |

The optimum value of $`a_{k}\in[0,1]`$ obtained by solving

–
([26e](#Ax1.E26.5 "In 26 ‣ F-A The Optimization Problem and KKT Conditions ‣ Karush-Kuhn-Tucker Conditions and Optimization of Perceptual Distortion Measures Supporting Document ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement"))
in this case is:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle a_{k}=\min\left\{\exp\left(0.5\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{2}}{X_{k}^{2}}-0.75\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{4}}{X_{k}^{4}}-10\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{6}}{X_{k}^{6}}-210\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{8}}{X_{k}^{8}}\right),1\right\}.`$ |  |

The corresponding estimate of $`S_{k}`$ is given by

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\widehat{S}_{k}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\underbrace{\text{min}\left\{\exp\left(\displaystyle\frac{0.5}{\xi_{k}}-\displaystyle\frac{0.75}{\xi_{k}^{2}}-\displaystyle\frac{10}{\xi_{k}^{3}}-\displaystyle\frac{210}{\xi_{k}^{4}}\right),1\right\}}_{a_{\text{log MSE}}(\xi_{k})}X_{k}.`$ |  |

### IV-D Itakura-Saito (IS) Distortion

The IS distortion, although not symmetric, is a popular quality measure
used in speech coding due to its perceptual relevance in matching two
power spectra. Here, we compute the IS distortion between the DCT
coefficients of the noise-free speech and its estimate considering both
of them to have the same sign:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
d(S_{k},\widehat{S}_{k})=\displaystyle\frac{\widehat{S}_{k}}{S_{k}}-\log\displaystyle\frac{\widehat{S}_{k}}{S_{k}}-1.
``` |  |

In the high-SNR scenario, the distortion measure is expanded as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle d(S_{k},\widehat{S}_{k})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\displaystyle\frac{\widehat{S}_{k}}{X_{k}}\left(1-\displaystyle\frac{W_{k}}{X_{k}}\right)^{-1}-\log\widehat{S}_{k}+\log S_{k}-1,`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\displaystyle\frac{\widehat{S}_{k}}{X_{k}}\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\displaystyle\frac{W_{k}}{X_{k}}\right)^{n}-\log\widehat{S}_{k}+\log S_{k}-1.`$ |  |

Expressing $`\widehat{S}_{k}=a_{k}X_{k}`$, and truncating the series
beyond $`n=4`$, the risk turns out to be

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{R}=\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{4}{\cal E}\left\{\frac{a_{k}W_{k}^{n}}{X_{k}^{n}}\right\}-{\cal E}\left\{\log a_{k}X_{k}\right\}+\log S_{k}-1.
``` |  | (11) |

The first term is evaluated using *Lemma*
[4](#S3.EGx3 "Lemma 4 ‣ Proof: ‣ Proof: ‣ III Risk Estimation Results ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement")
(cf. Appendix
[E](#A5 "Appendix E Itakura-Saito Distortion ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement"))
resulting in the risk

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{E}\left\{a_{k}\left(1+60\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{6}}{X_{k}^{6}}+840\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{8}}{X_{k}^{8}}\right)-\log a_{k}X_{k}\right\}+\log S_{k}-1.
``` |  |

The corresponding unbiased estimator of $`{\cal R}`$ is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
{\widehat{\cal R}}=a_{k}\left(1+60\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{6}}{X_{k}^{6}}+840\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{8}}{X_{k}^{8}}\right)-\log a_{k}X_{k}+\log\,S_{k}-1,
``` |  | (12) |

which when optimized with respect to $`a_{k}\in\left[0,1\right]`$
(solution of
([26a](#Ax1.E26.1 "In 26 ‣ F-A The Optimization Problem and KKT Conditions ‣ Karush-Kuhn-Tucker Conditions and Optimization of Perceptual Distortion Measures Supporting Document ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement"))
to
([26e](#Ax1.E26.5 "In 26 ‣ F-A The Optimization Problem and KKT Conditions ‣ Karush-Kuhn-Tucker Conditions and Optimization of Perceptual Distortion Measures Supporting Document ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement")))
gives

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
a_{k}=\left(1+\displaystyle\frac{60}{\xi_{k}^{3}}+\displaystyle\frac{840}{\xi_{k}^{4}}\right)^{-1}\Rightarrow\widehat{S}_{k}=\underbrace{\left(1+\displaystyle\frac{60}{\xi_{k}^{3}}+\displaystyle\frac{840}{\xi_{k}^{4}}\right)^{-1}}_{a_{\text{IS}}(\xi_{k})}X_{k}.
``` |  |

Figure
[3](#S4.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ IV-C Logarithmic Mean-Square Error (log MSE) ‣ IV Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement (PROSE) ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement")(a)
shows a comparison of $`a_{\text{IS}}`$ and $`a_{\text{MSE}}`$. For a
posteriori SNR greater than $`0`$ dB, the attenuation is higher in the
case of Itakura-Saito distortion.

### IV-E Itakura-Saito Distortion Between DCT Power Spectra (IS-II)

We next consider the IS distortion between $`S_{k}^{2}`$ and
$`\widehat{S}_{k}^{2}`$:

|  |  |  |  |

Considering $`{\widehat{S}}_{k}=a_{k}X_{k}`$, and truncating the series
beyond $`n=4`$, results in the risk

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{E}\left\{a_{k}^{2}\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{4}(n+1)\left(\displaystyle\frac{W_{k}}{X_{k}}\right)^{n}\right\}-\mathcal{E}\left\{\log a_{k}^{2}X_{k}^{2}\right\}+\log S_{k}^{2}-1.`$ |  |

|  |  | $`\displaystyle\text{\hskip 28.45274pt}-\mathcal{E}\left\{\log a_{k}^{2}X_{k}^{2}\right\}+\log S_{k}^{2}-1.`$ |  |

An unbiased estimator of $`{\cal R}`$ is

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle{\widehat{\cal R}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=a_{k}^{2}\left(1+\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{2}}{X_{k}^{2}}-3\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{4}}{X_{k}^{4}}+360\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{6}}{X_{k}^{6}}+4200\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{8}}{X_{k}^{8}}\right)`$ |  |  |

Optimizing $`{\widehat{\cal R}}`$ with respect to

we get

### IV-F Hyperbolic Cosine Distortion Measure (COSH)

A symmetrized version of the IS distortion results in the cosh measure:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
d(S_{k},\widehat{S}_{k})=\cosh\left(\log\displaystyle\frac{S_{k}}{\widehat{S}_{k}}\right)-1=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left[\displaystyle\frac{S_{k}}{\widehat{S}_{k}}+\displaystyle\frac{\widehat{S}_{k}}{S_{k}}\right]-1.
``` |  |

The corresponding risk is
$`\mathcal{R}=\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{E}\left\{\displaystyle\frac{S_{k}}{\widehat{S}_{k}}\right\}+\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{E}\left\{\displaystyle\frac{\widehat{S}_{k}}{S_{k}}\right\}-1.`$
Substituting $`\widehat{S}_{k}=a_{k}X_{k}`$, and invoking *Lemma*
[4](#S3.EGx3 "Lemma 4 ‣ Proof: ‣ Proof: ‣ III Risk Estimation Results ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement"),
the expectations turn out to be

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{E}\left\{\displaystyle\frac{S_{k}}{a_{k}X_{k}}\right\}=\mathcal{E}\left\{\displaystyle\frac{1}{a_{k}}+\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{2}}{a_{k}X_{k}^{2}}\right\},\,\,\,\mbox{and}`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{E}\left\{\displaystyle\frac{a_{k}X_{k}}{S_{k}}\right\}=\mathcal{E}\left\{a_{k}\left(1+60\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{6}}{X_{k}^{6}}+840\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{8}}{X_{k}^{8}}\right)\right\}.`$ |  |

Correspondingly, the unbiased risk estimator is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
{\widehat{\cal R}}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\displaystyle\frac{1}{a_{k}}+\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{2}}{a_{k}X_{k}^{2}}+a_{k}\left(1+60\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{6}}{X_{k}^{6}}+840\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{8}}{X_{k}^{8}}\right)\right)-1.
``` |  | (14) |

Optimizing $`{\widehat{\cal R}}`$ with respect to
$`a_{k}\in\left[0,1\right]`$ following
([26a](#Ax1.E26.1 "In 26 ‣ F-A The Optimization Problem and KKT Conditions ‣ Karush-Kuhn-Tucker Conditions and Optimization of Perceptual Distortion Measures Supporting Document ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement"))–([26e](#Ax1.E26.5 "In 26 ‣ F-A The Optimization Problem and KKT Conditions ‣ Karush-Kuhn-Tucker Conditions and Optimization of Perceptual Distortion Measures Supporting Document ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement"))
gives

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\widehat{S}_{k}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\underbrace{\min\left\{1,\sqrt{\displaystyle\frac{1+\displaystyle\frac{1}{\xi_{k}}}{1+60\displaystyle\frac{1}{\xi_{k}^{3}}+840\displaystyle\frac{1}{\xi_{k}^{4}}}}\right\}}_{a_{\text{COSH}}(\xi_{k})}X_{k}.`$ |  |

Figure
[3](#S4.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ IV-C Logarithmic Mean-Square Error (log MSE) ‣ IV Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement (PROSE) ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement")(c)
shows a comparison of $`a_{\text{MSE}}`$ and $`a_{\text{COSH}}`$ versus
$`\xi_{k}`$ – it is clear that $`a_{\text{COSH}}`$ results in a higher
attenuation than $`a_{\text{MSE}}`$.

### IV-G Weighted COSH Distortion (WCOSH)

Similar to the weighted Euclidean measure, we consider the weighted cosh
distortion:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
d(S_{k},\widehat{S}_{k})=\left(\frac{1}{2}\left[\displaystyle\frac{S_{k}}{\widehat{S}_{k}}+\displaystyle\frac{\widehat{S}_{k}}{S_{k}}\right]-1\right)\displaystyle\frac{1}{S_{k}}.
``` |  | (15) |

The risk estimator can be computed as in the case of the cosh measure
(cf. Appendix
[F](#A6 "Appendix F Weighted COSH Distance ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement")).
The optimal estimate is given by

## V Implementation Details

For experimental validation, we use the clean speech and nonstationary
noise data from the NOIZEUS database \[[45](#bib.bib45)\] ($`8`$ kHz
sampling frequency and $`16`$-bit quantization). Noisy speech is
generated by adding noise at a desired global SNR. The noisy speech
signal is processed in the DCT domain on a frame-by-frame basis. The
frame size is $`40`$ ms, the window function is Hamming, and the overlap
between consecutive frames is $`75\%`$. We experiment with both
stationary and nonstationary noise types. The developments in the PROSE
framework assumed knowledge of the noise variance in each bin of a given
frame. In practice, the noise variance has to be estimated. For this
purpose, we use the likelihood-ratio-test-based voice activity detector
(VAD) of Sohn et al. \[[46](#bib.bib46)\], which was also employed in
the extensive comparisons reported by Hu and Loizou
\[[45](#bib.bib45)\]. The inverse a posteriori SNR is then estimated
using the recursive formula

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\frac{1}{\widehat{\xi}_{k}\left(i\right)}=\beta\frac{\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{k}\left(i\right)}{X^{2}_{k}\left(i\right)}+\left(1-\beta\right)\text{max}\left(1-\frac{\widehat{S}^{2}_{k}\left(i-1\right)}{X^{2}_{k}\left(i-1\right)},0\right),
``` |  | (16) |

where $`k`$ denotes the index of the DCT coefficient, $`i`$ denotes the
frame index, and $`\beta`$ is a smoothing parameter (set to $`0.98`$ in
our experiments), $`\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{k}(i)`$ is the estimate of the
noise variance at the $`k^{th}`$ DCT coefficient in the $`i^{th}`$
frame, $`{\widehat{S}_{k}\left(i-1\right)}`$ is the denoised $`k^{th}`$
DCT coefficient in the $`(i-1)^{th}`$ frame. The first few frames are
assumed to contain only noise. For the first frame, $`\beta`$ is set to
unity, and the $`k^{th}`$ noise variance is estimated by averaging the
noise variances of the $`k^{th}`$ coefficient in the first ten frames.
The noise variance is updated in the noise-only frames as classified by
the VAD following the recursion:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{k}\left(i\right)=\begin{cases}&\eta\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{k}\left(i-1\right)+\left(1-\eta\right)X^{2}_{k}\left(i\right),\text{under}\quad\mathcal{H}_{0},\\
&\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{k}(i-1),\text{under}\quad\mathcal{H}_{1},\\
\end{cases}
``` |  |

where $`\mathcal{H}_{0}`$ and $`\mathcal{H}_{1}`$ are the null
hypothesis (noise-only) and the alternative hypothesis (signal + noise),
respectively. The value of $`\eta=0.98`$ following \[[45](#bib.bib45)\].
The noisy speech signals are denoised using shrinkage functions
corresponding to various distortion measures considered in this paper.
We set $`\alpha=1.75`$ uniformly across all measures in the PROSE
framework as it was found to give better results than $`\alpha=1`$. The
enhanced speech frames are combined using overlap-and-add synthesis to
result in the denoised speech signal.

## VI Experimental Results

The denoising performance of PROSE estimators is evaluated using both
objective measures and subjective listening tests. For benchmarking, we
use three algorithms: (i) Wiener filter method, where a priori SNR is
estimated using the decision-directed approach (WFIL)
\[[10](#bib.bib10)\]; (ii) a Bayesian estimator for short-time
log-spectral amplitude (LSA) \[[32](#bib.bib32)\]; and (iii) Bayesian
non-negative matrix factorization algorithm (BNMF)
\[[34](#bib.bib34)\]²² 2 Matlab implementation available online:
[https://www.uni-oldenburg.de/en/mediphysics-acoustics/sigproc/staff/nasser-mohammadiha/matlab-codes/](https://www.uni-oldenburg.de/en/mediphysics-acoustics/sigproc/staff/nasser-mohammadiha/matlab-codes/),
which gives an MMSE estimate of the clean speech DFT magnitude. In the
BNMF approach, the speech bases are learned offline and the noise bases
are learned online. The WFIL and LSA algorithms were shown to perform
better than the other techniques in an extensive evaluation carried out
by Hu and Loizou \[[45](#bib.bib45)\]³³ 3 Matlab implementations of
algorithms used in \[[45](#bib.bib45)\] are available with
\[[2](#bib.bib2)\]. For performance comparisons, we have used Matlab
codes given with \[[2](#bib.bib2)\]. . In terms of intelligibility also,
these techniques were shown to be superior (Ch. 11, pp. 567 of
\[[2](#bib.bib2)\]). BNMF has been shown to be the best among the NMF
approaches for speech enhancement.

### VI-A Objective Evaluation

The denoising performance is quantified in terms of: (i) global SNR;
(ii) average segmental SNR (SSNR), a local metric, which is the average
of the SNRs computed over short segments; (iii) perceptual evaluation of
speech quality (PESQ) \[[39](#bib.bib39)\], which is an objective score
for assessing end-to-end speech quality in narrowband telephone networks
and speech codecs, described in ITU-T Recommendation P.862
\[[39](#bib.bib39)\]; and (iv) short-time objective intelligibility
measure (STOI) \[[40](#bib.bib40)\]⁴⁴ 4 Matlab implementation available
at [http://siplab.tudelft.nl/](http://siplab.tudelft.nl/), which ranges
from $`0`$ to $`1`$ and reflects the correlation between short-time
temporal envelope of clean speech and denoised speech. It has been shown
to correlate highly with the intelligibility scores obtained through
listening tests \[[40](#bib.bib40)\]. Measures (i), (ii), and (iii)
assess the speech quality, whereas (iv) measures the intelligibility.
For SNR, SSNR, and PESQ, we report the gains achieved by denoising. The
SNR gain is the difference between the output and input SNR values. The
PESQ gain and the SSNR gain are also computed similarly.
We consider all $`30`$ speech files from the NOIZEUS database
\[[45](#bib.bib45)\], and three noise types – white Gaussian noise,
train noise, and street noise, the last two being real-world
nonstationary noise types. The results presented here are obtained after
averaging over the entire database for $`50`$ noise realizations.

### VI-B Subjective Evaluation

We consider four speech files (two male and two female speakers) at SNRs
0, 10, and 20 dB. Fifteen listeners in the age group of $`20-35`$ years
endowed with normal hearing were selected for the listening test. The
subjects were given a Sennheiser HD650 headphone for listening, and were
asked to rate the enhanced speech signal based on the ITU-T recommended
P.835 scale \[[41](#bib.bib41)\]:
(i) Speech signal distortion (SIG); $`1`$: very distorted, $`2`$: fairly
distorted, $`3`$: somewhat distorted, $`4`$: little distorted, $`5`$:
not distorted;
(ii) Background intrusiveness (BAK); $`1`$: very intrusive, $`2`$:
somewhat intrusive, $`3`$: noticeable but not intrusive, $`4`$: somewhat
noticeable, $`5`$: not noticeable; and
(iii) Overall quality (OVRL); $`1`$: bad, $`2`$: poor, $`3`$: fair,
$`4`$: good, $`5`$: excellent.
The listening tests were conducted in three sessions, one for each noise
type (white/street/train) at all SNRs. Following the ITU-T
recommendation, each session is further divided into two subsessions. In
the first one, two files (one male and one female speaker) are used with
the rating order as SIG$`-`$BAK$`-`$OVRL and in the second one, the
other two files (again one male and one female speaker) are presented
and the order of rating is BAK$`-`$SIG$`-`$OVRL. The two-session test is
done to suppress listener’s bias. In each subsession, the listeners rate
the denoising performance of all the algorithms. Thus, in one
subsession, a listener had to grade a total of 2 (speakers) $`\times 3`$
(SNRs) $`\times 11`$ (algorithms) = 66 files. The subjects were given
sufficient time to relax within and across subsessions, and across SNRs.
The denoised speech files were presented in a random order, and the
scores were derandomized accordingly before calculating the average
scores. The listeners were given a token reward at the end of the
listening test. Figure
[7](#S6.F7 "Figure 7 ‣ VI-B Subjective Evaluation ‣ VI Experimental Results ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement")
shows the mean scores of SIG, BAK, and OVRL for white noise, street
noise, and train noise.
We observe that, among the PROSE algorithms, WCOSH exhibits a
consistently high denoising performance in terms of SIG, BAK, and OVRL
scores compared with the other algorithms. Among the techniques
considered for benchmarking performance, LSA shows a consistently higher
performance. The performances of LSA and PROSE with WCOSH are
comparable. Also, PROSE consistently improves the BAK and OVRL scores
compared with the noisy signal. Listening results reveal that, for all
the algorithms considered, the extent of improvement in SIG scores is
not significant compared with the improvement in BAK and OVRL scores.
Within the PROSE family, WCOSH, IS-II, and IS show a high BAK and OVRL
scores, whereas MSE results in a lower score, compared with the other
algorithms.

## VII Conclusions

We introduced the notion of unbiased risk estimation within a perceptual
framework (abbreviated PROSE) for performing single-channel speech
enhancement. The analytical developments are based on Stein’s lemma and
its generalized version introduced in this paper, which proved to be
efficient for obtaining unbiased estimates of the distortion measures.
We have also established the optimality of the shrinkage parameters
considering the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker conditions. Validation on several
speech signals in both synthesized noise and real-world nonstationary
noise scenarios, and comparisons with benchmark techniques showed that,
for input SNR greater than $`5`$ dB, the proposed PROSE method results
in better denoising performance. Within the PROSE family, estimators
based on Itakura-Saito distortion and weighted cosh distortion resulted
in superior denoising performance. It must be emphasized that the PROSE
methodology is relatively simpler from an implementation perspective
(recall Fig.
[1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ I-B Our Contributions ‣ I Introduction ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement"))
and does not require any training, making it ideal for deployment in
practical applications involving hearing aids, mobile devices, etc.
Further, we employed a voice-activity detector and a recursive algorithm
for estimating the time-varying a posteriori SNR. Improving on the
accuracy of noise variance estimation for handling rapidly changing
noise would further enhance the performance of the PROSE method.

## Appendix A Karush-Kuhn-Tucker Conditions

The goal is to solve the optimization problem

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\underset{a_{k}}{\operatorname{min}}\,\widehat{\mathcal{R}}\,\text{subject to}\,a_{k}\in\left[0,1\right].
``` |  |

The corresponding Lagrangian is
$`\mathbf{L}\left(a_{k},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\right)=\widehat{\mathcal{R}}+\lambda_{1}\left(a_{k}-1\right)-\lambda_{2}a_{k}`$,
where $`\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\cup\{0\}`$. The KKT
conditions are as follows:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  |  |  |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle{\tt C1}:\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{L}\left(a_{k},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\right)}{\mathrm{d}a_{k}}=0,`$ |  | (17a) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle{\tt C2}:\,\lambda_{1}(a_{k}-1)=0,-\lambda_{2}a_{k}=0,`$ |  | (17b) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle{\tt C3}:\,a_{k}\in\left[0,1\right],`$ |  | (17c) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle{\tt C4}:\,\lambda_{1}\geq 0,\lambda_{2}\geq 0,\,\text{and}\,`$ |  | (17d) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle{\tt C5}:\,\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{L}\left(a_{k},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\right)}{\mathrm{d}a_{k}^{2}}>0.`$ |  | (17e) |

Solving
([26a](#Ax1.E26.1 "In 26 ‣ F-A The Optimization Problem and KKT Conditions ‣ Karush-Kuhn-Tucker Conditions and Optimization of Perceptual Distortion Measures Supporting Document ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement"))$`-`$([26e](#Ax1.E26.5 "In 26 ‣ F-A The Optimization Problem and KKT Conditions ‣ Karush-Kuhn-Tucker Conditions and Optimization of Perceptual Distortion Measures Supporting Document ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement"))
gives the optimum shrinkage parameter $`a_{k}`$. The derivations for all
the distortion measures are provided in the supporting document.

## Appendix B The High-SNR Scenario

The a priori SNR is defined as
$`\text{SNR}:=\displaystyle\frac{{S_{k}}^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}`$. By high
SNR, we mean that $`\text{SNR}>4c^{2}`$. Consider the probability:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\text{Prob}\left\{\left|W_{k}\right|<\left|X_{k}\right|\right\}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\text{Prob}\left\{{X_{k}}^{2}-{W_{k}}^{2}>0\right\},`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\text{Prob}\left\{\right({X_{k}}+{W_{k}}\left)\right({X_{k}}-{W_{k}}\left)>0\right\},`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\text{Prob}\left\{S_{k}\right({X_{k}}+{W_{k}}\left)>0\right\},`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\text{Prob}\left\{{W_{k}}<-\frac{S_{k}}{2}\right\},\text{if}\quad S_{k}<0`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\text{Prob}\left\{{W_{k}}>-\frac{S_{k}}{2}\right\},\text{if}\quad S_{k}>0.`$ |  | (18) |

Therefore,
$`\text{Prob}\left\{\left|W_{k}\right|<\left|X_{k}\right|\right\}=\text{Prob}\left\{W_{k}<\frac{\left|S_{k}\right|}{2}\right\}=1`$
if $`|S_{k}|>2c\sigma\Rightarrow\text{a priori SNR}>4c^{2}.`$

## Karush-Kuhn-Tucker Conditions and Optimization of Perceptual Distortion Measures Supporting Document

In this supporting document, we provide the detailed derivations for
obtaining the optimum shrinkage factors for various distortion measures
considered in the main document. The section and equation references
correspond to those in the main document.

### F-A The Optimization Problem and KKT Conditions

The goal is to solve

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\underset{a_{k}}{\operatorname{min}}\,\widehat{\mathcal{R}}\,\text{subject to}\,a_{k}\in\left[0,1\right],
``` |  | (25) |

where $`\widehat{\mathcal{R}}`$ is a chosen risk/distortion measure. The
unconstrained form using Lagrangian is given by
$`\mathbf{L}\left(a_{k},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\right)=\widehat{\mathcal{R}}+\lambda_{1}\left(a_{k}-1\right)-\lambda_{2}a_{k},`$
where $`\lambda_{1}\in\mathbf{R}`$ and $`\lambda_{2}\in\mathbf{R}`$ . In
order to obtain the optimum $`a_{k}`$, we have to solve the
Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions given by

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  |  |  |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle{\tt C1}:\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{L}\left(a_{k},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\right)}{\mathrm{d}a_{k}}=0,`$ |  | (26a) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle{\tt C2}:\,\lambda_{1}(a_{k}-1)=0,-\lambda_{2}a_{k}=0,`$ |  | (26b) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle{\tt C3}:\,a_{k}\in\left[0,1\right],`$ |  | (26c) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle{\tt C4}:\,\lambda_{1}\geq 0,\lambda_{2}\geq 0,\,\text{and}\,`$ |  | (26d) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle{\tt C5}:\,\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{L}\left(a_{k},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\right)}{\mathrm{d}a_{k}^{2}}>0.`$ |  | (26e) |

### F-B Mean-squared error (MSE)

In this case, recall from (cf. Eq. (5) in Section IV-A) that

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
{\widehat{\cal R}}=a_{k}^{2}X_{k}^{2}-2a_{k}X_{k}^{2}+2\sigma^{2}a_{k}+S_{k}^{2}.
``` |  |

The corresponding Lagrangian is

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbf{L}\left(a_{k},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\right)=a_{k}^{2}X_{k}^{2}-2a_{k}X_{k}^{2}+2\sigma^{2}a_{k}+S_{k}^{2}+\lambda_{1}\left(a_{k}-1\right)-\lambda_{2}a_{k}.
``` |  |

Our goal is to determine $`a_{k}`$ that satisfies the KKT conditions
([26a](#Ax1.E26.1 "In 26 ‣ F-A The Optimization Problem and KKT Conditions ‣ Karush-Kuhn-Tucker Conditions and Optimization of Perceptual Distortion Measures Supporting Document ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement"))
to
([26e](#Ax1.E26.5 "In 26 ‣ F-A The Optimization Problem and KKT Conditions ‣ Karush-Kuhn-Tucker Conditions and Optimization of Perceptual Distortion Measures Supporting Document ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement")).
The first- and second-order derivatives of the Lagrangian are

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{L}}{\mathrm{d}a_{k}}=2a_{k}X_{k}^{2}-2X_{k}^{2}+2\sigma^{2}+\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2},
``` |  |

and

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{L}}{\mathrm{d}a_{k}^{2}}=2X_{k}^{2},
``` |  |

respectively. In order to solve
([26a](#Ax1.E26.1 "In 26 ‣ F-A The Optimization Problem and KKT Conditions ‣ Karush-Kuhn-Tucker Conditions and Optimization of Perceptual Distortion Measures Supporting Document ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement"))
–
([26e](#Ax1.E26.5 "In 26 ‣ F-A The Optimization Problem and KKT Conditions ‣ Karush-Kuhn-Tucker Conditions and Optimization of Perceptual Distortion Measures Supporting Document ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement"))
for $`a_{k}`$, we consider different cases of $`\lambda_{1}`$ and
$`\lambda_{2}`$.

- •
  Case 1: $`\lambda_{1}=0`$, and $`\lambda_{2}>0`$
  ([26b](#Ax1.E26.2 "In 26 ‣ F-A The Optimization Problem and KKT Conditions ‣ Karush-Kuhn-Tucker Conditions and Optimization of Perceptual Distortion Measures Supporting Document ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement"))
  and
  ([26c](#Ax1.E26.3 "In 26 ‣ F-A The Optimization Problem and KKT Conditions ‣ Karush-Kuhn-Tucker Conditions and Optimization of Perceptual Distortion Measures Supporting Document ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement"))
  $`\Rightarrow\,a_{k}=0`$, and from
  ([26a](#Ax1.E26.1 "In 26 ‣ F-A The Optimization Problem and KKT Conditions ‣ Karush-Kuhn-Tucker Conditions and Optimization of Perceptual Distortion Measures Supporting Document ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement"))
  we obtain

  |  |  |  |  |  |
  |----|----|----|----|----|
  |  | $`\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{L}}{\mathrm{d}a_{k}}\Bigg|_{a_{k}=0}`$ | $`\displaystyle=-2X_{k}^{2}+2\sigma^{2}-\lambda_{2}=0,`$ |  |  |
  |  |  | $`\displaystyle\Rightarrow\,\sigma^{2}-X_{k}^{2}=\frac{\lambda_{2}}{2}\overset{\eqref{dual_feasibility}}{>}0.`$ |  |  |
  |  |  | $`\displaystyle\Rightarrow\,\sigma^{2}>X_{k}^{2}\Rightarrow\,a_{k}=0.`$ |  | (27) |
- •
  Case 2: $`\lambda_{1}>0`$, and $`\lambda_{2}=0`$
  ([26b](#Ax1.E26.2 "In 26 ‣ F-A The Optimization Problem and KKT Conditions ‣ Karush-Kuhn-Tucker Conditions and Optimization of Perceptual Distortion Measures Supporting Document ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement"))

  ([26c](#Ax1.E26.3 "In 26 ‣ F-A The Optimization Problem and KKT Conditions ‣ Karush-Kuhn-Tucker Conditions and Optimization of Perceptual Distortion Measures Supporting Document ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement"))
  $`\Rightarrow\,a_{k}=1`$, and from
  ([26a](#Ax1.E26.1 "In 26 ‣ F-A The Optimization Problem and KKT Conditions ‣ Karush-Kuhn-Tucker Conditions and Optimization of Perceptual Distortion Measures Supporting Document ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement"))
  we obtain

  |  |  |  |  |  |
  |----|----|----|----|----|
  |  | $`\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{L}}{\mathrm{d}a_{k}}\Bigg|_{a_{k}=1}`$ | $`\displaystyle=2\sigma^{2}+\lambda_{1}=0,`$ |  |  |

  ([28](#Ax1.Ex8 "In 2nd item ‣ F-B Mean-squared error (MSE) ‣ Karush-Kuhn-Tucker Conditions and Optimization of Perceptual Distortion Measures Supporting Document ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement"))

  Case 3: $`\lambda_{1}>0`$, and $`\lambda_{2}>0`$
  ([26b](#Ax1.E26.2 "In 26 ‣ F-A The Optimization Problem and KKT Conditions ‣ Karush-Kuhn-Tucker Conditions and Optimization of Perceptual Distortion Measures Supporting Document ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement"))

  ([26c](#Ax1.E26.3 "In 26 ‣ F-A The Optimization Problem and KKT Conditions ‣ Karush-Kuhn-Tucker Conditions and Optimization of Perceptual Distortion Measures Supporting Document ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement"))
  imply that no solution exists.

  Case 4: $`\lambda_{1}=0`$, and $`\lambda_{2}=0`$

  ([26b](#Ax1.E26.2 "In 26 ‣ F-A The Optimization Problem and KKT Conditions ‣ Karush-Kuhn-Tucker Conditions and Optimization of Perceptual Distortion Measures Supporting Document ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement"))

  |----|----|----|----|----|
  |  | $`\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{L}}{\mathrm{d}a_{k}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=2a_{k}X_{k}^{2}-2X_{k}^{2}+2\sigma^{2}=0,`$ |  |  |
  |  |  | $`\displaystyle\Rightarrow\,a_{k}=1-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{X_{k}^{2}}.`$ |  | (29) |

Consolidating Case 1 to Case 4, we obtain that

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math

``` |  |

Also observe that
$`\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{L}}{\mathrm{d}a_{k}^{2}}=2X_{k}^{2}>0`$,

$`\widehat{\mathcal{R}}`$ over $`\left[0,1\right]`$.

  ([26a](#Ax1.E26.1 "In 26 ‣ F-A The Optimization Problem and KKT Conditions ‣ Karush-Kuhn-Tucker Conditions and Optimization of Perceptual Distortion Measures Supporting Document ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement")),
  one can obtain $`a_{k}`$ as follows:

  |  |  |  |  |  |
  |----|----|----|----|----|
  |  | $`\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{L}}{\mathrm{d}a_{k}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=2a_{k}\left(1+\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{2}}{X_{k}^{2}}-3\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{4}}{X_{k}^{4}}+360\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{6}}{X_{k}^{6}}+4200\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{8}}{X_{k}^{8}}\right)-\displaystyle\frac{2}{a_{k}}=0,`$ |  |  |

### F-G Hyperbolic cosine distortion measure (COSH)

The COSH risk estimate is (cf. Eq. (14) in Section IV-F)

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle{\widehat{\cal R}}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\displaystyle\frac{1}{a_{k}}+\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{2}}{a_{k}X_{k}^{2}}+a_{k}\left(1+60\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{6}}{X_{k}^{6}}+840\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{8}}{X_{k}^{8}}\right)\right)-1.`$ |  |

The corresponding Lagrangian, its first- and second-order derivatives
are

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathbf{L}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\displaystyle\frac{1}{a_{k}}+\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{2}}{a_{k}X_{k}^{2}}+a_{k}\left(1+60\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{6}}{X_{k}^{6}}+840\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{8}}{X_{k}^{8}}\right)\right)-1+\lambda_{1}\left(a_{k}-1\right)-\lambda_{2}a_{k},`$ |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{L}}{\mathrm{d}a_{k}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\displaystyle-\frac{1}{a_{k}^{2}}-\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{2}}{a_{k}^{2}X_{k}^{2}}+\left(1+60\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{6}}{X_{k}^{6}}+840\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{8}}{X_{k}^{8}}\right)\right)+\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2},\text{\,and}`$ |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d^{2}}\mathbf{L}}{\mathrm{d}a_{k}^{2}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\displaystyle\left(\displaystyle\frac{1}{a_{k}^{3}}+\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{2}}{a_{k}^{3}X_{k}^{2}}\right).`$ |  |

Consider four cases of $`\lambda_{1}`$ and $`\lambda_{2}`$.

- •
  Case 1: $`\lambda_{1}=0`$, and $`\lambda_{2}>0`$
  ([26b](#Ax1.E26.2 "In 26 ‣ F-A The Optimization Problem and KKT Conditions ‣ Karush-Kuhn-Tucker Conditions and Optimization of Perceptual Distortion Measures Supporting Document ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement"))

  $`\Rightarrow\,a_{k}=0`$, and from
  ([26a](#Ax1.E26.1 "In 26 ‣ F-A The Optimization Problem and KKT Conditions ‣ Karush-Kuhn-Tucker Conditions and Optimization of Perceptual Distortion Measures Supporting Document ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement"))
  we obtain

  |  |  |  |  |  |
  |----|----|----|----|----|
  |  | $`\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{L}}{\mathrm{d}a_{k}}\Bigg|_{a_{k}=0}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\displaystyle-\frac{1}{a_{k}^{2}}-\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{2}}{a_{k}^{2}X_{k}^{2}}+\left(1+60\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{6}}{X_{k}^{6}}+840\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{8}}{X_{k}^{8}}\right)\right)-\lambda_{2}\Bigg|_{a_{k}=0}=0,`$ |  |  |

  which contradicts
  ([26d](#Ax1.E26.4 "In 26 ‣ F-A The Optimization Problem and KKT Conditions ‣ Karush-Kuhn-Tucker Conditions and Optimization of Perceptual Distortion Measures Supporting Document ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement")),
  and hence $`a_{k}=0`$ is not a solution.
- •
  Case 2: $`\lambda_{1}>0`$, and $`\lambda_{2}=0`$
  ([26b](#Ax1.E26.2 "In 26 ‣ F-A The Optimization Problem and KKT Conditions ‣ Karush-Kuhn-Tucker Conditions and Optimization of Perceptual Distortion Measures Supporting Document ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement"))
  and
  ([26c](#Ax1.E26.3 "In 26 ‣ F-A The Optimization Problem and KKT Conditions ‣ Karush-Kuhn-Tucker Conditions and Optimization of Perceptual Distortion Measures Supporting Document ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement"))
  $`\Rightarrow\,a_{k}=1`$, and from
  ([26a](#Ax1.E26.1 "In 26 ‣ F-A The Optimization Problem and KKT Conditions ‣ Karush-Kuhn-Tucker Conditions and Optimization of Perceptual Distortion Measures Supporting Document ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement")),
  we obtain

  |  |  |  |  |  |
  |----|----|----|----|----|
  |  | $`\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{L}}{\mathrm{d}a_{k}}\Bigg|_{a_{k}=1}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\displaystyle-\left(1+\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{2}}{X_{k}^{2}}\right)+\left(1+60\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{6}}{X_{k}^{6}}+840\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{8}}{X_{k}^{8}}\right)\right)+\lambda_{1}=0,`$ |  |  |
  |  |  | $`\displaystyle\Rightarrow\,\left(1+\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{2}}{X_{k}^{2}}\right)-\left(1+60\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{6}}{X_{k}^{6}}+840\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{8}}{X_{k}^{8}}\right)=2\lambda_{1}\overset{\eqref{dual_feasibility}}{>}0,`$ |  | (44) |

  $`\Rightarrow\,`$ if
  $`\left(1+60\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{6}}{X_{k}^{6}}+840\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{8}}{X_{k}^{8}}\right)<\left(1+\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{2}}{X_{k}^{2}}\right)`$,
  then $`a_{k}=1`$ is a solution.
- •
  Case 3: $`\lambda_{1}>0`$, and $`\lambda_{2}>0`$
  ([26b](#Ax1.E26.2 "In 26 ‣ F-A The Optimization Problem and KKT Conditions ‣ Karush-Kuhn-Tucker Conditions and Optimization of Perceptual Distortion Measures Supporting Document ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement"))
  and
  ([26c](#Ax1.E26.3 "In 26 ‣ F-A The Optimization Problem and KKT Conditions ‣ Karush-Kuhn-Tucker Conditions and Optimization of Perceptual Distortion Measures Supporting Document ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement"))
  imply that no solution exists.
- •
  Case 4: $`\lambda_{1}=0`$, and $`\lambda_{2}=0`$
  In this case, for $`a_{k}\in\left[0,1\right]`$,
  ([26b](#Ax1.E26.2 "In 26 ‣ F-A The Optimization Problem and KKT Conditions ‣ Karush-Kuhn-Tucker Conditions and Optimization of Perceptual Distortion Measures Supporting Document ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement"))
  is satisfied. Using
  ([26a](#Ax1.E26.1 "In 26 ‣ F-A The Optimization Problem and KKT Conditions ‣ Karush-Kuhn-Tucker Conditions and Optimization of Perceptual Distortion Measures Supporting Document ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement"))
  one can obtain $`a_{k}`$ as follows:

  |  |  |  |  |  |
  |----|----|----|----|----|
  |  | $`\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{L}}{\mathrm{d}a_{k}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\displaystyle-\frac{1}{a_{k}^{2}}\left(1+\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{2}}{X_{k}^{2}}\right)+\left(1+60\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{6}}{X_{k}^{6}}+840\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{8}}{X_{k}^{8}}\right)\right)=0,`$ |  |  |
  |  |  | $`\displaystyle\Rightarrow\,a_{k}=\left(\frac{1+\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{2}}{X_{k}^{2}}}{1+60\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{6}}{X_{k}^{6}}+840\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{8}}{X_{k}^{8}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.`$ |  | (45) |

From Case 1 to Case 4, we obtain

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
a_{k}=\min\left\{1,\left(\frac{1+\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{2}}{X_{k}^{2}}}{1+60\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{6}}{X_{k}^{6}}+840\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{8}}{X_{k}^{8}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\}.
``` |  |

Since
$`\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d^{2}}\mathbf{L}\left(a_{k},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\right)}{\mathrm{d}a_{k}^{2}}>0`$,
$`a_{k}`$ turns out to be the unique minimizer of $`{\widehat{\cal R}}`$
over $`\left[0,1\right]`$.

### F-H Weighted cosine distortion measure (WCOSH)

Here, we consider $`{\widehat{\cal R}}`$ (cf. Eq. (23) in Appendix F)
defined as follows:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle{\widehat{\cal R}}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{a_{k}}{X_{k}}\left(1-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{X_{k}^{2}}+3\frac{\sigma^{4}}{X_{k}^{4}}+420\frac{\sigma^{6}}{X_{k}^{6}}+8400\frac{\sigma^{8}}{X_{k}^{8}}\right)+\frac{1}{2a_{k}X_{k}}-\frac{1}{S_{k}}.`$ |  |

The goal is to find $`a_{k}`$ that minimizes $`{\widehat{\cal R}}`$ when
$`S_{k}>0`$, and maximizes it when $`S_{k}<0`$. Under the high SNR
assumption, we assume that $`S_{k}`$ and $`X_{k}`$ have the same sign.
First, we consider the case where $`S_{k}>0`$. To obtain the optimum
$`a_{k}`$, we solve
([26a](#Ax1.E26.1 "In 26 ‣ F-A The Optimization Problem and KKT Conditions ‣ Karush-Kuhn-Tucker Conditions and Optimization of Perceptual Distortion Measures Supporting Document ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement"))$`-`$([26e](#Ax1.E26.5 "In 26 ‣ F-A The Optimization Problem and KKT Conditions ‣ Karush-Kuhn-Tucker Conditions and Optimization of Perceptual Distortion Measures Supporting Document ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement"))
where

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\mathbf{L}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{a_{k}}{X_{k}}\left(1-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{X_{k}^{2}}+3\frac{\sigma^{4}}{X_{k}^{4}}+420\frac{\sigma^{6}}{X_{k}^{6}}+8400\frac{\sigma^{8}}{X_{k}^{8}}\right)+\frac{1}{2a_{k}X_{k}}-\frac{1}{S_{k}}+\lambda_{1}\left(a_{k}-1\right)-\lambda_{2}a_{k},`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{L}}{\mathrm{d}a_{k}}=\frac{1}{2X_{k}}\left(1-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{X_{k}^{2}}+3\frac{\sigma^{4}}{X_{k}^{4}}+420\frac{\sigma^{6}}{X_{k}^{6}}+8400\frac{\sigma^{8}}{X_{k}^{8}}\right)-\frac{1}{2a_{k}^{2}X_{k}}+\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2},\text{\,and}`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d^{2}}\mathbf{L}}{\mathrm{d}a_{k}^{2}}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{a_{k}^{3}X_{k}}.`$ |  |

- •
  Case 1: $`\lambda_{1}=0`$, and $`\lambda_{2}>0`$
  ([26b](#Ax1.E26.2 "In 26 ‣ F-A The Optimization Problem and KKT Conditions ‣ Karush-Kuhn-Tucker Conditions and Optimization of Perceptual Distortion Measures Supporting Document ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement"))
  and
  ([26c](#Ax1.E26.3 "In 26 ‣ F-A The Optimization Problem and KKT Conditions ‣ Karush-Kuhn-Tucker Conditions and Optimization of Perceptual Distortion Measures Supporting Document ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement"))
  $`\Rightarrow\,a_{k}=0`$, and from
  ([26a](#Ax1.E26.1 "In 26 ‣ F-A The Optimization Problem and KKT Conditions ‣ Karush-Kuhn-Tucker Conditions and Optimization of Perceptual Distortion Measures Supporting Document ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement"))
  we obtain

  |  |  |  |  |  |
  |----|----|----|----|----|
  |  | $`\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{L}}{\mathrm{d}a_{k}}\Bigg|_{a_{k}=0}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2X_{k}}\left(1-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{X_{k}^{2}}+3\frac{\sigma^{4}}{X_{k}^{4}}+420\frac{\sigma^{6}}{X_{k}^{6}}+8400\frac{\sigma^{8}}{X_{k}^{8}}\right)-\frac{1}{2a_{k}^{2}X_{k}}-\lambda_{2}\Bigg|_{a_{k}=0}=0,`$ |  |  |
  |  |  | $`\displaystyle\Rightarrow\,-\infty=\lambda_{2},`$ |  | (46) |

  which contradicts
  ([26d](#Ax1.E26.4 "In 26 ‣ F-A The Optimization Problem and KKT Conditions ‣ Karush-Kuhn-Tucker Conditions and Optimization of Perceptual Distortion Measures Supporting Document ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement")),
  and hence $`a_{k}=0`$ is not a solution.
- •
  Case 2: $`\lambda_{1}>0`$, and $`\lambda_{2}=0`$
  ([26b](#Ax1.E26.2 "In 26 ‣ F-A The Optimization Problem and KKT Conditions ‣ Karush-Kuhn-Tucker Conditions and Optimization of Perceptual Distortion Measures Supporting Document ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement"))

  ([26c](#Ax1.E26.3 "In 26 ‣ F-A The Optimization Problem and KKT Conditions ‣ Karush-Kuhn-Tucker Conditions and Optimization of Perceptual Distortion Measures Supporting Document ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement"))
  $`\Rightarrow\,a_{k}=1`$, and from
  ([26a](#Ax1.E26.1 "In 26 ‣ F-A The Optimization Problem and KKT Conditions ‣ Karush-Kuhn-Tucker Conditions and Optimization of Perceptual Distortion Measures Supporting Document ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement"))
  we obtain

  |  |  |  |  |
  |----|----|----|----|
  |  | $`\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{L}}{\mathrm{d}a_{k}}\Bigg|_{a_{k}=1}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2X_{k}}\left(1-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{X_{k}^{2}}+3\frac{\sigma^{4}}{X_{k}^{4}}+420\frac{\sigma^{6}}{X_{k}^{6}}+8400\frac{\sigma^{8}}{X_{k}^{8}}\right)-\frac{1}{2X_{k}}+\lambda_{1}=0,`$ |  |
  |  |  | $`\displaystyle\Rightarrow\,\frac{1}{X_{k}}-\frac{1}{X_{k}}\left(1-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{X_{k}^{2}}+3\frac{\sigma^{4}}{X_{k}^{4}}+420\frac{\sigma^{6}}{X_{k}^{6}}+8400\frac{\sigma^{8}}{X_{k}^{8}}\right)=2\lambda_{1}\overset{\eqref{dual_feasibility}}{>}0,`$ |  |

  $`\Rightarrow\,`$ if
  $`1>\left(\displaystyle 1-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{X_{k}^{2}}+3\frac{\sigma^{4}}{X_{k}^{4}}+420\frac{\sigma^{6}}{X_{k}^{6}}+8400\frac{\sigma^{8}}{X_{k}^{8}}\right)`$,

- •
  Case 3: $`\lambda_{1}>0`$, and $`\lambda_{2}>0`$
  ([26b](#Ax1.E26.2 "In 26 ‣ F-A The Optimization Problem and KKT Conditions ‣ Karush-Kuhn-Tucker Conditions and Optimization of Perceptual Distortion Measures Supporting Document ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement"))

  ([26c](#Ax1.E26.3 "In 26 ‣ F-A The Optimization Problem and KKT Conditions ‣ Karush-Kuhn-Tucker Conditions and Optimization of Perceptual Distortion Measures Supporting Document ‣ PROSE: Perceptual Risk Optimization for Speech Enhancement"))
  imply that no solution exists.

  Case 4: $`\lambda_{1}=0`$, and $`\lambda_{2}=0`$

|  |  |  |

|  |  |  |
