# Statistical Speech Enhancement Based on Probabilistic Integration of Variational Autoencoder and Non-Negative Matrix Factorization

Yoshiaki Bando, Masato Mimura, Katsutoshi Itoyama, Kazuyoshi Yoshii,
Tatsuya Kawahara ^(†)^(†)thanks: Thanks to JSPS KAKENHI No. 15J08765 for
funding.

###### Abstract

This paper presents a statistical method of single-channel speech
enhancement that uses a variational autoencoder (VAE) as a prior
distribution on clean speech. A standard approach to speech enhancement
is to train a deep neural network (DNN) to take noisy speech as input
and output clean speech. Although this supervised approach requires a
very large amount of pair data for training, it is not robust against
unknown environments. Another approach is to use non-negative matrix
factorization (NMF) based on basis spectra trained on clean speech in
advance and those adapted to noise on the fly. This semi-supervised
approach, however, causes considerable signal distortion in enhanced
speech due to the unrealistic assumption that speech spectrograms are
linear combinations of the basis spectra. Replacing the poor linear
generative model of clean speech in NMF with a VAE—a powerful nonlinear
deep generative model—trained on clean speech, we formulate a unified
probabilistic generative model of noisy speech. Given noisy speech as
observed data, we can sample clean speech from its posterior
distribution. The proposed method outperformed the conventional
DNN-based method in unseen noisy environments.

###### Index Terms: 

Single-channel speech enhancement, variational autoencoder, Bayesian
signal processing

## 1 Introduction

Deep neural networks (DNNs) have demonstrated excellent performance in
single-channel speech enhancement \[[1](#bib.bib1), [2](#bib.bib2),
[3](#bib.bib3), [4](#bib.bib4), [5](#bib.bib5), [6](#bib.bib6)\]. The
denoising autoencoder (DAE) \[[1](#bib.bib1)\], for example, is a
typical variant of such networks, which is trained to directly convert a
noisy speech spectrogram to a clean speech spectrogram with a supervised
training. Alternatively, a DNN can be trained to predict time-frequency
(TF) masks called ideal ratio masks (IRMs) that represent ratios of
speech to input signals and are used for obtaining a speech spectrogram
from a noisy spectrogram \[[4](#bib.bib4)\]. Although it is necessary to
prepare as training data a large amount of pairs of clean speech signals
and their noisy versions, these supervised methods often deteriorate in
unknown noisy environments. This calls for semi-supervised methods that
are trained by using only clean speech data in advance and then adapt to
unseen noisy environments.

Statistical source separation methods based on the additivity of speech
and noise spectrograms have also been used for speech
enhancement \[[7](#bib.bib7), [8](#bib.bib8), [9](#bib.bib9),
[10](#bib.bib10), [11](#bib.bib11)\]. Non-negative matrix factorization
(NMF) \[[12](#bib.bib12), [9](#bib.bib9)\], for example, regards a noisy
speech spectrogram as a non-negative matrix and approximates it as the
product of two non-negative matrices (a set of basis spectra and a set
of the corresponding activations). If a partial set of basis spectra is
trained in advance from clean speech spectrograms, the noisy spectrogram
is decomposed into the sum of speech and noise spectrograms in a
semi-supervised manner. Robust principal component analysis
(RPCA) \[[13](#bib.bib13), [14](#bib.bib14)\] is another promising
method that can decompose a noisy spectrogram into a sparse speech
spectrogram and a low-rank noise spectrogram in an unsupervised manner.
These conventional statistical methods, however, have a common problem
that the linear representation or the sparseness assumption of speech
spectrograms is not satisfied in reality and results in considerable
signal distortion.

Recently, deep generative models such as generative adversarial networks
(GANs) and variational autoencoders (VAEs) have gained a lot of
attention for learning a probability distribution over complex data
(e.g., images and audio signals) that cannot be represented by
conventional linear models \[[15](#bib.bib15), [16](#bib.bib16),
[17](#bib.bib17), [18](#bib.bib18), [19](#bib.bib19)\]. GANs and VAEs
are both based on two kinds of DNNs having different roles. In
GANs \[[15](#bib.bib15)\], a generator is trained to synthesize data
that fool a discriminator from a latent space while the discriminator is
trained to detect synthesized data in a minimax-game fashion. In
VAEs \[[16](#bib.bib16), [17](#bib.bib17)\], on the other hand, an
encoder that embeds observed data into a latent space and a decoder that
generates data from the latent space are trained jointly such that the
lower bound of the log marginal likelihood for the observed data is
maximized. Although in general GANs can generate more realistic data,
VAEs provide a principled scheme of inferring the latent representations
of both given and new data.

In this paper we propose a unified probabilistic generative model of
noisy speech spectra by combining a VAE-based generative model of speech
spectra with an NMF-based generative model of noise spectra
(Fig. [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Statistical Speech Enhancement Based on Probabilistic Integration of Variational Autoencoder and Non-Negative Matrix Factorization")).
The VAE is trained in advance from a sufficient amount of clean speech
spectra and its decoder is used as a prior distribution on clean speech
spectra included in noisy speech spectra. Given observed data, we can
estimate both the latent representations of speech spectra as well as
the basis spectra and their activations of noise spectra through
Bayesian inference based on a Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC)
initialized by the encoder of the VAE. Our Bayesian approach can adapt
to both unseen speech and noise spectra by using prior knowledge of
clean speech and the low-rankness assumption on noise instead of fixing
all the parameters in advance.

## 2 Related work

This section overviews DNN-based speech enhancement and introduces the
variational autoencoder (VAE).

### 2.1 DNN-based speech enhancement

Various network architectures and cost functions for enhancing speech
signals have been reported \[[1](#bib.bib1), [2](#bib.bib2),
[3](#bib.bib3), [4](#bib.bib4), [5](#bib.bib5), [6](#bib.bib6)\]. The
popular approach of DNN-based speech enhancement is to train a DNN to
directly represent clean speech \[[6](#bib.bib6)\]. The DNN is trained
using simulated noisy data constructed by adding noise to speech as
input and clean speech as the target. There are several methods that
combine a supervised NMF and a DNN \[[20](#bib.bib20),
[21](#bib.bib21)\]. A DNN is trained to estimate activation vectors of
the pre-trained basis vectors corresponding to speech and noise.
Bayesian WaveNet \[[22](#bib.bib22)\] uses two networks: one, called a
prior network, represents how likely a signal is speech and the other,
called a likelihood network, represents how likely a signal is included
in the observation. These two networks enhance the noisy speech signal
with a maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimation. Another reported method
uses two networks that are trained to represent how likely the input
signal is speech or noise, respectively \[[23](#bib.bib23)\]. The speech
signal is enhanced by optimizing a cost function so that the estimated
speech maximizes the speech-likelihood network and minimizes the
noise-likelihood network. All the above mentioned methods are trained
with datasets of both speech and noise signals. A DNN-based method using
only training data of speech signals was reported \[[24](#bib.bib24)\].
This method represents speech and noise spectra with two autoencoders
(AEs). The AE for speech is pre-trained, whereas that for noise is
trained at the inference for adapting to the observed noise signal.
Since the inference of this framework is under-determined, the estimated
speech is constrained to be represented by a pre-trained NMF model. It,
thus, might have the same problem as the semi-supervised NMF.

### 2.2 Variational autoencoder

A VAE \[[16](#bib.bib16)\] is a framework for learning the probability
distribution of a dataset. In this subsection, we denote by
$`\mathbf{{X}}`$ a dataset that contains $`F`$-dimensional samples
$`{\bm{x}}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{F}`$ ($`t=1,\ldots,T`$). The VAE assumes
that a $`D`$-dimensional latent variable (denoted by
$`{\bm{z}}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}`$)  
follows a standard Gaussian distribution and each sample
$`{\bm{x}}_{t}`$ is stochastically generated from a conditional
distribution
$`p\hskip-0.8pt\left({{\bm{x}}_{t}}\hskip-0.8pt\left|{{\bm{z}}_{t}}\right.\hskip-0.8pt\right)`$:

|     |                               |                                                                                 |                                              |     |
| --- | ----------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle{\bm{z}}_{t}`$ | $`\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}\hskip-0.8pt\left({\bf 0},{{\bf I}_{D}}\right),`$ |                                              | (1) |
|     | $`\displaystyle{\bm{x}}_{t}`$ | $`\displaystyle\sim p\hskip-0.8pt\left({{\bm{x}}\_{t}}\hskip-0.8pt\left         | {{\bm{z}}\_{t}}\right.\hskip-0.8pt\right),`$ |     | (2) |

where $`\mathcal{N}\hskip-0.8pt\left({\mu},{\sigma}\right)`$ represents
a Gaussian distribution with mean parameter $`\mu`$ and variance
parameter $`\sigma`$.
$`p\hskip-0.8pt\left({{\bm{x}}_{t}}\hskip-0.8pt\left|{{\bm{z}}_{t}}\right.\hskip-0.8pt\right)`$
is called a decoder and parameterized as a well-known probability
density function whose parameters are given by nonlinear functions
represented as neural networks. For example, Kingma et
al. \[[16](#bib.bib16)\] reported a VAE model that has the following
Gaussian likelihood function:

|     |                                                                                                    |                                                                                                  |                                                                                                                                                     |
| --- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\hskip-2.84526pt{\bm{x}}_{t}\sim p\hskip-0.8pt\left({{\bm{x}}_{t}}\hskip-0.8pt\left | {{\bm{z}}_{t}}\right.\hskip-0.8pt\right)=\prod_{f}p\hskip-0.8pt\left({{x}_{ft}}\hskip-0.8pt\left | {{\bm{z}}_{t}}\right.\hskip-0.8pt\right)=\prod_{f}\mathcal{N}\hskip-0.8pt\left({\mu^{x}_{f}({\bm{z}}_{t})},{\sigma^{x}_{f}({\bm{z}}_{t})}\right),`$ |     | (3) |

where $`\mu^{x}_{f}:\mathbb{R}^{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}`$ and
$`\sigma^{x}_{f}:\mathbb{R}^{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}`$ are neural
networks representing the mean and variance parameters, respectively.

The objective of VAE training is to find a likelihood function
$`p\hskip-0.8pt\left({{\bm{x}}_{t}}\hskip-0.8pt\left|{{\bm{z}}_{t}}\right.\hskip-0.8pt\right)`$
that maximizes the log marginal likelihood:

|     |                                                                                                    |                                                                                                                                                                                         |                                                                                                            |
| --- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{p\hskip-0.8pt\left({{\bm{x}}_{t}}\hskip-0.8pt\left | {{\bm{z}}_{t}}\right.\hskip-0.8pt\right)}\log p\hskip-0.8pt\left({\mathbf{{X}}}\hskip-0.8pt\right)=\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{p\hskip-0.8pt\left({{\bm{x}}\_{t}}\hskip-0.8pt\left | {{\bm{z}}_{t}}\right.\hskip-0.8pt\right)}\prod_{t}\int p\hskip-0.8pt\left({{\bm{x}}\_{t}}\hskip-0.8pt\left | {{\bm{z}}_{t}}\right.\hskip-0.8pt\right)p\hskip-0.8pt\left({{\bm{z}}_{t}}\hskip-0.8pt\right)d{\bm{z}}\_{t}.`$ |     | (4) |

Since calculating this marginal likelihood is intractable, it is
approximated with a variational Bayesian (VB) framework. The VAE first
approximates the posterior distribution of $`{\bm{z}}_{t}`$ with the
following variational posterior distribution
$`q\hskip-0.8pt\left({{\bm{z}}_{t}}\right)`$ called an encoder:

|     |                                                                                        |                                                                                                                              |                                                                                                                           |     |
| --- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle p\hskip-0.8pt\left({{\bm{z}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{z}}_{T}}\hskip-0.8pt\left | {\mathbf{{X}}}\right.\hskip-0.8pt\right)`$                                                                                   | $`\displaystyle\approx\prod_{t}q\hskip-0.8pt\left({{\bm{z}}_{t}}\right)=\prod_{d,t}q\hskip-0.8pt\left({{z}_{dt}}\right)`$ |     | (5) |
|     |                                                                                        | $`\displaystyle=\prod_{d,t}\mathcal{N}\hskip-0.8pt\left({\mu^{z}_{d}({\bm{x}}_{t})},{\sigma^{z}_{d}({\bm{x}}_{t})}\right),`$ |                                                                                                                           | (6) |

where $`\mu^{z}_{d}:\mathbb{R}^{F}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}`$ and
$`\sigma^{z}_{d}:\mathbb{R}^{F}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}`$ are nonlinear
functions representing the mean and variance parameters, respectively.
These functions are formulated with DNNs. By using the variational
posterior, the log marginal likelihood is lower-bounded as follows:

|     |                                                                                            |                                                                                                                                       |                                                                                                                                                          |                                                     |
| --- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\hskip-5.69054pt\log p\hskip-0.8pt\left({\mathbf{{X}}}\hskip-0.8pt\right)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\sum*{t}\log\int p\hskip-0.8pt\left({{\bm{x}}*{t}}\hskip-0.8pt\left                                                   | {{\bm{z}}_{t}}\right.\hskip-0.8pt\right)p\hskip-0.8pt\left({{\bm{z}}_{t}}\hskip-0.8pt\right)d{\bm{z}}\_{t}`$                                             |                                                     | (7) |
|     |                                                                                            | $`\displaystyle\geq\sum*{t}\int q\hskip-0.8pt\left({{\bm{z}}*{t}}\right)\log\frac{p\hskip-0.8pt\left({{\bm{x}}\_{t}}\hskip-0.8pt\left | {{\bm{z}}_{t}}\right.\hskip-0.8pt\right)p\hskip-0.8pt\left({{\bm{z}}_{t}}\hskip-0.8pt\right)}{q\hskip-0.8pt\left({{\bm{z}}_{t}}\right)}d{\bm{z}}_{t}`$   |                                                     | (8) |
|     |                                                                                            | $`\displaystyle=-\sum*{t}\mathbb{KL}\left[q\hskip-0.8pt\left({{\bm{z}}*{t}}\right)\left                                               | {p\hskip-0.8pt\left({{\bm{z}}_{t}}\hskip-0.8pt\right)}\right.\right]+\sum_{k}\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[\log p\hskip-0.8pt\left({{\bm{x}}_{t}}\hskip-0.8pt\left | {{\bm{z}}\_{t}}\right.\hskip-0.8pt\right)\right],`$ |     | (9) |

where $`\mathbb{KL}\left[\cdot\left|{\cdot}\right.\right]`$ represents
the Kullback-Leibler divergence. The VAE is trained so that
$`p\hskip-0.8pt\left({{\bm{x}}_{t}}\hskip-0.8pt\left|{{\bm{z}}_{t}}\right.\hskip-0.8pt\right)`$
and $`q\hskip-0.8pt\left({{\bm{z}}_{t}}\right)`$ maximize this
variational lower bound. The first term of Eq.
([9](#S2.E9 "In 2.2 Variational autoencoder ‣ 2 Related work ‣ Statistical Speech Enhancement Based on Probabilistic Integration of Variational Autoencoder and Non-Negative Matrix Factorization"))
is analytically tractable and the second term can be approximated with a
Monte-Carlo algorithm. The lower bound can be maximized by using a
stochastic gradient descent (SGD) \[[25](#bib.bib25)\].

## 3 Statistical Speech Enhancement Based on Combination of VAE and NMF

This section describes the proposed probabilistic generative model
called VAE-NMF, that combines a VAE-based speech model and a NMF-based
noise model. We formulate the generative process of an observed complex
spectrogram $`\mathbf{{X}}\in\mathbb{C}^{F\times T}`$ by formulating the
process of a speech spectrogram
$`\mathbf{{S}}\in\mathbb{C}^{F\times T}`$ and a noise spectrogram
$`\mathbf{{N}}\in\mathbb{C}^{F\times T}`$. The characteristics of speech
and noise signals are represented by their priors based on VAE and NMF,
respectively.

### 3.1 VAE-based speech model

![Refer to caption](1710.11439v4/vae.png)

Figure 2: VAE representation of a speech spectrogram.

In our speech model we assume a frame-wise $`D`$-dimensional latent
variable $`\mathbf{{Z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times T}`$. Each time-frame of
the latent variable $`{\bm{z}}_{t}`$ is supposed to represent the
characteristics of a speech spectrum such as fundamental frequency,
spectral envelope, and type of phoneme. The specific representation of
$`{\bm{z}}_{t}`$ is obtained automatically by conducting the VAE
training with a dataset of clean speech spectra. As in the conventional
VAEs, we put the standard Gaussian prior on each element of
$`\mathbf{{Z}}`$:

|     |                                                                           |     |      |
| --- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | ---- |
|     | $`\displaystyle{z}_{dt}\sim\mathcal{N}\hskip-0.8pt\left({0},{1}\right).`$ |     | (10) |

Since the speech spectra are primarily characterized by its power
spectral density (PSD), it follows a zero-mean complex Gaussian
distribution whose variance parameter is formulated with
$`\mathbf{{Z}}`$
(Fig. [2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3.1 VAE-based speech model ‣ 3 Statistical Speech Enhancement Based on Combination of VAE and NMF ‣ Statistical Speech Enhancement Based on Probabilistic Integration of Variational Autoencoder and Non-Negative Matrix Factorization")):

|     |                                                                                                                   |     |      |
| --- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | ---- |
|     | $`\displaystyle{s}_{ft}\sim\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}\hskip-0.8pt\left({0},{\sigma^{s}_{f}({\bm{z}}_{t})}\right),`$ |     | (11) |

where
$`\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}\hskip-0.8pt\left({\mu},{\sigma}\right)`$ is a
complex Gaussian distribution with mean parameter $`\mu`$ and variance
parameter $`\sigma`$.
$`\sigma^{s}_{f}({\bm{z}}_{t}):\mathbb{R}^{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}`$
is a nonlinear function representing the relationship between
$`\mathbf{{Z}}`$ and the speech signal $`\mathbf{{S}}`$. This function
is formulated by using a DNN and obtained by the VAE training.

### 3.2 Generative model of mixture signals

In our Bayesian generative model, the input complex spectrogram
$`\mathbf{{X}}\in\mathbb{C}^{F\times T}`$ is represented as the sum of a
speech spectrogram $`\mathbf{{S}}`$ and a noise spectrogram
$`\mathbf{{N}}`$:

|     |                                              |     |      |
| --- | -------------------------------------------- | --- | ---- |
|     | $`\displaystyle{x}_{ft}={s}_{ft}+{n}_{ft}.`$ |     | (12) |

We put the VAE-based hierarchical prior model
(Eqs. ([10](#S3.E10 "In 3.1 VAE-based speech model ‣ 3 Statistical Speech Enhancement Based on Combination of VAE and NMF ‣ Statistical Speech Enhancement Based on Probabilistic Integration of Variational Autoencoder and Non-Negative Matrix Factorization"))
and
([11](#S3.E11 "In 3.1 VAE-based speech model ‣ 3 Statistical Speech Enhancement Based on Combination of VAE and NMF ‣ Statistical Speech Enhancement Based on Probabilistic Integration of Variational Autoencoder and Non-Negative Matrix Factorization")))
on the speech spectrogram $`\mathbf{{S}}`$. On the other hand, we assume
that the PSD of the noise spectrogram is low-rank and put an NMF-based
prior model on it. More specifically, the PSD of a noise spectrogram can
be represented as the product of $`K`$ spectral basis vectors
$`\mathbf{{W}}=[{\bm{w}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{w}}_{K}]\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{F\times K}`$
and their activation vectors
$`\mathbf{{H}}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{K\times T}`$. The zero-mean complex
Gaussian distribution is put on each TF bin of the noise spectrogram
$`\mathbf{{N}}`$ as follows:

|     |                                                                                                               |     |      |
| --- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | ---- |
|     | $`\displaystyle{n}_{ft}\sim\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}\hskip-0.8pt\left({0},{\sum_{k}{w}_{fk}{h}_{kt}}\right).`$ |     | (13) |

For mathematical convenience, we put conjugate prior distributions on
$`\mathbf{{W}}`$ and $`\mathbf{{H}}`$ as follows:

|     |                                                                                                                                                                    |     |      |
| --- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --- | ---- |
|     | $`\displaystyle{w}_{fk}\sim\mathcal{G}\hskip-0.8pt\left({a_{0}},{b_{0}}\right),\hskip 14.22636pt{h}_{kt}\sim\mathcal{G}\hskip-0.8pt\left({a_{1}},{b_{1}}\right),`$ |     | (14) |

where $`\mathcal{G}\hskip-0.8pt\left({\alpha},{\beta}\right)`$ is a
gamma distribution with the shape parameter $`\alpha>0`$ and the rate
parameter $`\beta>0`$; $`a_{0}`$, $`b_{0}`$, $`a_{1}`$, and $`b_{1}`$
are hyperparameters that should be set in advance.

By marginalizing out the speech and noise complex spectrograms
$`\mathbf{{S}}`$ and $`\mathbf{{N}}`$, we obtain the following Gaussian
likelihood:

|     |                                      |                                                                                                                                                           |     |
| --- | ------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | ---- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\left.{x}\_{ft}\right | \mathbf{{W}},\mathbf{{H}},\mathbf{{Z}}\sim\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}\hskip-0.8pt\left({0},{\sum_{k}{w}_{fk}{h}_{kt}+\sigma^{s}_{f}({\bm{z}}_{t})}\right).`$ |     | (15) |

Since this likelihood function is independent of the phase term of the
input spectrogram $`\mathbf{{X}}`$, it is equivalent to the following
exponential likelihood:

|     |                       |           |            |
| --- | --------------------- | --------- | ---------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --- | ---- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\left. | {x}\_{ft} | ^{2}\right | \mathbf{{W}},\mathbf{{H}},\mathbf{{Z}}\sim\mathrm{Exp}\hskip-0.8pt\left({\sum*{k}{w}*{fk}{h}_{kt}+\sigma^{s}_{f}({\bm{z}}\_{t})}\right),`$ |     | (16) |

where $`|{x}_{ft}|^{2}`$ is the power spectrogram of $`\mathbf{{X}}`$
and $`\mathrm{Exp}\hskip-0.8pt\left({\lambda}\right)`$ is the
exponential distribution with a mean parameter $`\lambda`$. Maximization
of the exponential likelihood on a power spectrogram corresponds to
minimization of Itakura-Saito divergence, which is widely used in audio
source separation \[[12](#bib.bib12), [26](#bib.bib26)\].

### 3.3 Pre-training of VAE-based speech model

The goal of the pre-training of the VAE-based speech model is to find
$`p\hskip-0.8pt\left({{\bm{s}}_{t}}\hskip-0.8pt\left|{{\bm{z}}_{t}}\right.\hskip-0.8pt\right)`$
that maximizes the following marginal likelihood
$`p\hskip-0.8pt\left({\mathbf{{S}}}\hskip-0.8pt\right)`$ from the
dataset of clean speech signal (denoted by
$`\mathbf{{S}}\in\mathbb{C}^{F\times T}`$ in this subsection):

|     |                                                                                                                                       |                                                                                                               |     |
| --- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | ---- |
|     | $`\displaystyle p\hskip-0.8pt\left({\mathbf{{S}}}\hskip-0.8pt\right)=\prod*{t}\int p\hskip-0.8pt\left({{\bm{s}}*{t}}\hskip-0.8pt\left | {{\bm{z}}_{t}}\right.\hskip-0.8pt\right)p\hskip-0.8pt\left({{\bm{z}}_{t}}\hskip-0.8pt\right)p{\bm{z}}\_{t}.`$ |     | (17) |

As stated in
Sec. [2.2](#S2.SS2 "2.2 Variational autoencoder ‣ 2 Related work ‣ Statistical Speech Enhancement Based on Probabilistic Integration of Variational Autoencoder and Non-Negative Matrix Factorization"),
it is difficult to analytically calculate this marginal likelihood. We
approximate it by using the Variational mean-field approximation. Let
$`q\hskip-0.8pt\left({\mathbf{{Z}}}\right)`$ be the variational
posterior distribution of $`\mathbf{{Z}}`$. Since
$`p\hskip-0.8pt\left({\mathbf{{S}}}\hskip-0.8pt\left|{\mathbf{{Z}}}\right.\hskip-0.8pt\right)`$
is independent from the phase term of the speech spectrogram
$`\mathbf{{S}}`$, the variational posterior
$`q\hskip-0.8pt\left({\mathbf{{Z}}}\right)`$ is defined by ignoring the
phase term as follows:

|     |                                                                                                                                                                     |               |                                     |
| --- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------- | ----------------------------------- | ------------- | ---------------------- | --- | ---- |
|     | $`\displaystyle q\hskip-0.8pt\left({\mathbf{{Z}}}\right)=\prod*{d,t}q\hskip-0.8pt\left({{z}*{dt}}\right)=\prod*{d,t}\mathcal{N}\hskip-0.8pt\left({\mu^{z}*{d}\left( | {\bm{s}}\_{t} | ^{2}\right)},{\sigma^{z}\_{d}\left( | {\bm{s}}\_{t} | ^{2}\right)}\right),`$ |     | (18) |

where $`|{\bm{s}}_{t}|^{2}`$ is the power spectrum of $`{\bm{s}}_{t}`$
and $`\mu^{z}_{d}:\mathbb{R}_{+}^{F}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}`$ and
$`\sigma^{z}_{d}:\mathbb{R}_{+}^{F}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}`$ are
nonlinear functions representing the mean and variance parameters of the
Gaussian distribution. These two functions are defined with DNNs. The
marginal likelihood is approximately calculated as follows:

|     |                                        |                                                                                                                                         |                                                                                                                                                   |                                                           |
| --- | -------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- | ------------- | -------------------------- | ------------- | --------------- | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\hskip-14.22636pt\log`$ | $`\displaystyle p\hskip-0.8pt\left({\mathbf{{S}}}\hskip-0.8pt\right)\geq-\mathbb{KL}\left[q\hskip-0.8pt\left({\mathbf{{Z}}}\right)\left | {p\hskip-0.8pt\left({\mathbf{{Z}}}\hskip-0.8pt\right)}\right.\right]+\mathbb{E}\_{q}\left[\log p\hskip-0.8pt\left({\mathbf{{S}}}\hskip-0.8pt\left | {\mathbf{{Z}}}\right.\hskip-0.8pt\right)\right]`$         |               | (19)                       |
|     |                                        | $`\displaystyle=-\sum*{d,t}\frac{1}{2}\left\{\left(\mu^{z}*{d}(                                                                         | {\bm{s}}\_{t}                                                                                                                                     | ^{2})\right)^{2}+\sigma^{z}\_{d}(                         | {\bm{s}}\_{t} | ^{2})-\log\sigma^{z}\_{d}( | {\bm{s}}\_{t} | ^{2})\right\}`$ |     |     |
|     |                                        | $`\displaystyle\hskip 11.38109pt+\sum*{f,t}\mathbb{E}*{q}\left[-\log\sigma^{s}_{f}({\bm{z}}_{t})-\frac{                                 | {s}\_{ft}                                                                                                                                         | ^{2}}{\sigma^{s}_{f}({\bm{z}}_{t})}\right]+{\rm const.}`$ |               | (20)                       |

The DNNs for $`\sigma^{s}_{f}`$, $`\mu^{z}_{d}`$, and $`\sigma^{z}_{d}`$
are optimized by using SGD so that this variational lower bound is
maximized.

### 3.4 Bayesian inference of VAE-NMF

To enhance the speech signal in a noisy observed signal, we calculate
the full posterior distribution of our model:
$`p\hskip-0.8pt\left({\mathbf{{W}},\mathbf{{H}},\mathbf{{Z}}}\hskip-0.8pt\left|{\mathbf{{X}}}\right.\hskip-0.8pt\right)`$.
Since the true posterior is analytically intractable, we approximate it
with a finite number of random samples by using a Markov chain Monte
Carlo (MCMC) algorithm \[[27](#bib.bib27)\]. MCMC alternatively and
iteratively samples one of the latent variables ($`\mathbf{{W}}`$,
$`\mathbf{{H}}`$, and $`\mathbf{{Z}}`$) according to their conditional
posterior distributions.

By fixing the speech parameter $`\mathbf{{Z}}`$, the conditional
posterior distributions
$`p\hskip-0.8pt\left({\mathbf{{W}}}\hskip-0.8pt\left|{\mathbf{{X}},\mathbf{{H}},\mathbf{{Z}}}\right.\hskip-0.8pt\right)`$
and
$`p\hskip-0.8pt\left({\mathbf{{H}}}\hskip-0.8pt\left|{\mathbf{{X}},\mathbf{{W}},\mathbf{{Z}}}\right.\hskip-0.8pt\right)`$
can be derived with a variational approximation \[[26](#bib.bib26),
[27](#bib.bib27)\] as follows:

|     |     |                                                                                                                                                                                                                        |                                                                                                                                                    |           |
| --- | --- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------- | ---------------------------------------------- | --- | ---- |
|     |     | $`\displaystyle{w}\_{fk}                                                                                                                                                                                               | \mathbf{{X}},\mathbf{{H}},\mathbf{{Z}}\!\sim\!\mathcal{GIG}\hskip-0.8pt\left({a*{0}},{b*{0}\!+\!\!\sum*{t}\frac{{h}*{kt}}{\lambda*{ft}}},{\sum*{t} | {x}\_{ft} | ^{2}\frac{\phi*{ftk}^{2}}{{h}*{kt}}}\right),`$ |     | (21) |
|     |     | $`\displaystyle{h}\_{kt}                                                                                                                                                                                               | \mathbf{{X}},\mathbf{{W}},\mathbf{{Z}}\!\sim\!\mathcal{GIG}\hskip-0.8pt\left({a*{1}},{b*{1}\!+\!\!\sum*{f}\frac{{w}*{fk}}{\lambda*{ft}}},{\sum*{f} | {x}\_{ft} | ^{2}\frac{\phi*{ftk}^{2}}{{w}*{fk}}}\right),`$ |     | (22) |
|     |     | $`\displaystyle\hskip-5.69054pt\lambda_{ft}\!=\!\sum_{k}{w}_{fk}{h}_{kt}+\sigma^{s}_{f}({\bm{z}}_{t}),\hskip 2.84526pt\phi_{ftk}=\frac{{w}_{fk}{h}_{kt}}{\sum_{k}{w}_{fk}{h}_{kt}\!+\!\sigma^{s}_{f}({\bm{z}}_{t})},`$ |                                                                                                                                                    | (23)      |

where
$`\mathcal{GIG}\hskip-0.8pt\left({\gamma},{\rho},{\tau}\right)\propto x^{\gamma-1}\!\exp(-\rho x-\tau/x)`$
is the generalized inverse Gaussian distribution and $`\lambda_{ft}`$
and $`\phi_{ftk}`$ are auxiliary variables.

The latent variable of speech $`\mathbf{{Z}}`$ is updated by using a
Metropolis method \[[27](#bib.bib27)\] because it is hard to
analytically derive the conditional posterior
$`p\hskip-0.8pt\left({\mathbf{{Z}}}\hskip-0.8pt\left|{\mathbf{{X}},\mathbf{{W}},\mathbf{{H}}}\right.\hskip-0.8pt\right)`$.
The latent variable is sampled at each time frame by using the following
Gaussian proposal distribution
$`q\hskip-0.8pt\left({{\bm{z}}^{*}_{t}}\left|{{\bm{z}}_{t}}\right.\right)`$
whose mean is the previous sample $`{\bm{z}}_{t}`$:

|     |                                   |                                                                  |                                                                                                    |     |
| --- | --------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | ---- |
|     | $`\displaystyle{\bm{z}}^{*}_{t}`$ | $`\displaystyle\sim q\hskip-0.8pt\left({{\bm{z}}^{\*}\_{t}}\left | {{\bm{z}}_{t}}\right.\right)=\mathcal{N}\hskip-0.8pt\left({{\bm{z}}_{t}},{\sigma{\bf I}}\right),`$ |     | (24) |

where $`\sigma`$ is a variance parameter of the proposal distribution.
This candidate $`{\bm{z}}^{*}_{t}`$ is randomly accepted with the
following probability:

|     |                                      |                  |                                                                                       |                                                                                                                                                                                     |
| --- | ------------------------------------ | ---------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | ---- |
|     | $`\displaystyle a*{{\bm{z}}^{\*}*{t} | {\bm{z}}\_{t}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\min\left(1,\frac{p\hskip-0.8pt\left({{\bm{x}}\_{t}}\hskip-0.8pt\left | {\mathbf{{W}},\mathbf{{H}},{\bm{z}}^{_}\_{t}}\right.\hskip-0.8pt\right)p\hskip-0.8pt\left({{\bm{z}}^{_}_{t}}\hskip-0.8pt\right)}{p\hskip-0.8pt\left({{\bm{x}}_{t}}\hskip-0.8pt\left | {\mathbf{{W}},\mathbf{{H}},{\bm{z}}_{t}}\right.\hskip-0.8pt\right)p\hskip-0.8pt\left({{\bm{z}}_{t}}\hskip-0.8pt\right)}\right).`$ |     | (25) |

### 3.5 Reconstruction of complex speech spectrogram

In this paper we obtain the enhanced speech with Wiener filtering by
maximizing the conditional posterior
$`p\hskip-0.8pt\left({\mathbf{{S}}}\hskip-0.8pt\left|{\mathbf{{X}},\mathbf{{W}},\mathbf{{H}},\mathbf{{Z}}}\right.\hskip-0.8pt\right)`$.
Let $`\hat{\mathbf{{S}}}\in\mathbb{C}^{F\times T}`$ be the speech
spectrogram that maximizes the conditional posterior. It is given by the
following equation:

|     |                                                                                                                                   |     |      |
| --- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | ---- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\hat{s}_{ft}=\frac{\sigma^{s}_{f}({\bm{z}}_{t})}{\sum_{k}{w}_{fk}{h}_{kt}+\sigma^{s}_{f}({\bm{z}}_{t})}{x}_{ft}.`$ |     | (26) |

We simply use the mean values of the sampled latent variables as
$`\mathbf{{W}}`$, $`\mathbf{{H}}`$, and $`\mathbf{{Z}}`$ in
Eq. ([26](#S3.E26 "In 3.5 Reconstruction of complex speech spectrogram ‣ 3 Statistical Speech Enhancement Based on Combination of VAE and NMF ‣ Statistical Speech Enhancement Based on Probabilistic Integration of Variational Autoencoder and Non-Negative Matrix Factorization")).

## 4 Experimental Evaluation

This section reports experimental results with noisy speech signals
whose noise signals were captured in actual environments.

### 4.1 Experimental settings

To compare VAE-NMF with a DNN-based supervised method, we used CHiME-3
dataset \[[28](#bib.bib28)\] and DEMAND noise database¹¹ 1
[http://parole.loria.fr/DEMAND/](http://parole.loria.fr/DEMAND/). The
CHiME-3 dataset was used for both the training and evaluation. The
DEMAND database was used for constructing another evaluation dataset for
unseen noise conditions. The evaluation with the CHiME-3 was conducted
by using its development set, which consists of 410 simulated noisy
utterances in each of four different noisy environments: on a bus (BUS),
in a cafe (CAF), in a pedestrian area (PED) and on a street junction
(STR). The average signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of the noisy speech
signals was 5.8 dB. The evaluation with the DEMAND was conducted by
using 20 simulated noisy speech signals in each of four different noisy
environments: on a subway (SUB), in a cafe (CAF), at a town square
(SQU), and in a living room (LIV). We generated these signals by mixing
the clean speech signals of the CHiME-3 development set with the noise
signals in the DEMAND database. The SNR of these noisy speech signals
was set to be 5.0 dB. The sampling rate of these signals was 16 kHz. The
enhancement performance was evaluated by using the signal-to-distortion
ratio (SDR) \[[29](#bib.bib29)\].

we trained a VAE that had two networks of
$`p\hskip-0.8pt\left({{\bm{s}}_{t}}\hskip-0.8pt\left|{{\bm{z}}_{t}}\right.\hskip-0.8pt\right)`$
and $`q\hskip-0.8pt\left({{\bm{z}}_{t}}\right)`$ as shown in

The dimension of the latent variables $`D`$ was set to be 10. The
training data were about 15 hours of clean speech signals in the WSJ-0
corpus \[[30](#bib.bib30)\]. Their spectrograms were obtained with a
short-time Fourier transform (STFT) with a window length of 1024 samples
and a shifting interval of 256 samples. To make the prior distribution
robust against a scale of the speech power, we randomly changed the
average power of the spectrogram between 0.0 and 10.0 at each parameter
update.

The parameters for VAE-NMF were as follows. The number of bases $`K`$
was set to be $`5`$. The hyperparameters $`a_{0}`$, $`b_{0}`$,
$`a_{1}`$, $`b_{1}`$, and $`\sigma`$ were set to be $`1.0`$, $`1.0`$,
and $`1.0`$, $`K/scale`$, and $`0.01`$, respectively. The $`scale`$
represents the empirical average power of the input noisy spectrogram.
After drawing 100 samples for burn-in, we drew 50 samples to estimate
the latent variables. These parameters had been determined empirically.
The latent variables of noise $`\mathbf{{W}}`$ and $`\mathbf{{H}}`$ were
randomly initialized. Since the latent variable of speech
$`\mathbf{{Z}}`$ depends on the initial state, the initial sample was
drawn from
$`q\hskip-0.8pt\left({{\bm{z}}_{t}}\left|{{\bm{s}}_{t}}\right.\right)`$
by setting the observation $`{\bm{x}}_{t}`$ as the speech signal
$`{\bm{s}}_{t}`$.

We compared VAE-NMF with a DNN-based supervised method and the
unsupervised RPCA. We implemented a DNN that outputs IRMs (DNN-IRM). It
had five hidden layers with ReLU activation functions. It takes as an
input 11 frames of noisy 100-channel log-Mel-scale filterbank features
and predicts one frame of IRMs²² 2 SDRs were evaluated by dropping 2048
samples (5 frames) at both ends.. We trained DNN-IRM with the training
dataset of CHiME-3, which was generated by using the WSJ-0 speech
utterances and noise signals. The noise signals were recorded in the
same environments as those in the evaluated data.

## 5 Conclusion

We presented a semi-supervised speech enhancement method, called
VAE-NMF, that involves a probabilistic generative model of speech based
on a VAE and that of noise based on NMF. Only the speech model is
trained in advance by using a sufficient amount of clean speech. Using
the speech model as a prior distribution, we can obtain posterior
estimates of clean speech by using an MCMC sampler while adapting the
noise model to noisy environments. We experimentally confirmed that
VAE-NMF outperformed the conventional supervised DNN-based method in
unseen noisy environments.

One interesting future direction is to extend VAE-NMF to the
multichannel scenario. Since complicated speech signals and a spatial
mixing process can be represented by a VAE and a well-studied
phase-aware linear model (e.g., \[[3](#bib.bib3), [2](#bib.bib2),
[32](#bib.bib32)\]), respectively, it would be effective to integrate
these models in a unified probabilistic framework. We also investigate
GAN-based training of the speech model to accurately learn a probability
distribution of speech.
