###### Abstract

We explore unsupervised speech enhancement using diffusion models as
expressive generative priors for clean speech. Existing approaches guide
the reverse diffusion process using noisy speech through an approximate,
noise-perturbed likelihood score, combined with the unconditional score
via a trade-off hyperparameter. In this work, we propose two alternative
algorithms that directly model the conditional reverse transition
distribution of diffusion states. The first method integrates the
diffusion prior with the observation model in a principled way, removing
the need for hyperparameter tuning. The second defines a diffusion
process over the noisy speech itself, yielding a fully tractable and
exact likelihood score. Experiments on the WSJ0-QUT and VoiceBank-DEMAND
datasets demonstrate improved enhancement metrics and greater robustness
to domain shifts compared to both supervised and unsupervised baselines.

###### Index Terms: 

Unsupervised learning, speech enhancement, diffusion models, conditional
transition modeling.

## I Introduction

Diffusion models have recently emerged as a powerful class of generative
models, achieving impressive results in image, audio, and video
generation \[1, 2\]. These models learn expressive generative priors for
complex natural data by gradually adding noise through a forward
process, then training a reverse denoising process to remove it. Beyond
their success in generation tasks, diffusion models have been extended
to solve inverse problems \[3\], owing to their ability to perform both
unconditional and conditional data generation.

In the field of speech processing, diffusion models have been
successfully leveraged for speech enhancement (SE), an inverse problem
aimed at recovering clean speech from a noisy recording \[4, 5, 6, 7, 8,
9\]. While SE can encompass tasks such as dereverberation, we focus on
single-channel speech denoising. Traditionally, deep learning–based SE
has been dominated by predictive methods, which map noisy speech
directly to clean speech via supervised training with paired data.
Examples include regression-based convolutional or recurrent
architectures that optimize mean-squared error, time-frequency masking,
or perceptual loss functions \[10, 11, 12\]. In contrast,
diffusion-based SE incorporates a prior distribution over clean speech
with a generative training objective, rather than directly learning a
noisy-to-clean mapping.

Two main approaches exist for applying diffusion models to SE.
Supervised methods learn the conditional distribution of clean speech
given noisy input \[4, 6, 7, 8, 9\], enabling reverse diffusion
conditioned on the noisy signal. In contrast, unsupervised methods train
only on clean speech to learn a prior \[13, 14, 15, 16\], combining it
at test time with a noise model via Bayes’ rule. Inference alternates
posterior-guided denoising steps with noise parameter updates. Compared
to supervised approaches, this unsupervised setting allows for reusing
the trained prior for other speech inverse problems and may provide
robustness to noise domain mismatch \[13, 14, 17\].

While the unsupervised framework is well studied in image
processing \[18, 3\], its application to SE remains limited \[13, 14\].
In existing methods, the posterior score is obtained by combining the
learned prior score with the gradient of a noisy-speech likelihood.
However, the likelihood term is time-dependent and intractable,
typically approximated under simplified assumptions such as an
uninformative speech prior \[19\]. Its influence is further controlled
by a trade-off hyperparameter. Moreover, these methods update only the
mean of the prior (unconditional) reverse diffusion process using the
posterior score, while keeping the variance fixed, which can lead to
inaccurate posterior modeling and requires tuning of the guidance
strength.

In this paper, we propose a novel unsupervised SE framework that
performs posterior sampling by explicitly modeling the conditional
(reverse) transition distribution of the diffusion states. Our first
algorithm integrates the diffusion prior and observation model in a
principled way, eliminating the need for a guidance hyperparameter. The
second algorithm extends this approach by introducing a diffusion
process over the noisy speech itself, enabling exact computation of the
time-dependent likelihood. We evaluate both methods on the WSJ0-QUT and
VoiceBank-DEMAND benchmarks and observe consistent improvements (mainly
with the second algorithm) over existing unsupervised approaches across
standard enhancement metrics. Notably, the second algorithm exhibits
robustness to domain shifts between training and test conditions, often
matching or surpassing supervised baselines.

## II Background and related work

We review the diffusion-based unsupervised SE framework \[13, 14\],
which forms the foundation of our approach. Speech signals are first
represented in the STFT (STFT) domain, resulting in a complex-valued 2D
array of shape $`F\times L`$ ($`F`$ frequency bins, $`L`$ time frames).
For notational convenience, this array is vectorized into
$`\mathbf{s}\in\mathbb{C}^{FL}`$. Speech enhancement is performed using
a prior distribution over clean speech signals learned by a diffusion
model. This prior is defined via a continuous-time diffusion process
$`\{\mathbf{s}_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T]}`$, which gradually corrupts clean data
$`\mathbf{s}_{0}=\mathbf{s}`$ into Gaussian noise as the diffusion time
$`t`$ increases (not to be confused with the time index of the STFT
representation). The corruption is governed by a forward SDE (SDE) \[1,
13\]:

```math
\mathsf{d}\mathbf{s}_{t}=\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{s}_{t})\mathsf{d}t+g_{t}\mathsf{d}\mathbf{w}, \tag{1}
```

where $`\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{s}_{t})=-\gamma\mathbf{s}_{t}`$ is the drift
coefficient ($`\gamma>0`$), $`g_{t}`$ controls noise variance, and
$`\mathbf{w}`$ is a standard Wiener process. The solution of (1) defines
a diffusion process whose conditional marginal distributions are
characterized by the perturbation kernel
$`p_{0t}(\mathbf{s}_{t}|\mathbf{s})=\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathsf{e}^{-\gamma t}\mathbf{s},\sigma_{t}^{2}\mathbf{I})`$,
where $`\sigma_{t}^{2}`$ is determined from the SDE. Under mild
conditions \[20\], this process is reversible via a reverse SDE:

```math
\mathsf{d}\mathbf{s}_{t}=[\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{s}_{t})-g_{t}^{2}\nabla_{\mathbf{s}_{t}}\log p_{t}(\mathbf{s}_{t})]\mathsf{d}t+g_{t}\mathsf{d}\mathbf{\bar{w}}, \tag{2}
```

where $`\mathbf{\bar{w}}`$ is a backward Wiener process, and
$`\nabla_{\mathbf{s}_{t}}\log p_{t}(\mathbf{s}_{t})`$ (the score
function) is approximated by a DNN (DNN)-based score model
$`\mathbf{S}_{\theta}(\mathbf{s}_{t},{t})`$. The model parameters are
learned by solving the following problem \[21\]:

```math
\theta^{*}={\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{t,\mathbf{s},\bm{\zeta},\mathbf{s}_{t}|\mathbf{s}}}{\Bigr[\|{\sigma_{t}}{\mathbf{S}_{\theta}(\mathbf{s}_{t},{t})}+{\bm{\zeta}}\|_{2}^{2}\Bigl]}, \tag{3}
```

where $`\bm{\zeta}\sim\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})`$
represents zero-mean Gaussian noise. The trained score model
$`{\mathbf{S}_{\theta^{*}}(\mathbf{s}_{t},{t})}`$ then replaces the
score function in (2), and the resulting reverse SDE is solved
numerically to generate clean speech samples starting from Gaussian
noise.

To perform speech enhancement, the noisy speech signal is modeled as
$`\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{s}+\mathbf{n}`$, where
$`\mathbf{n}\sim\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}(\bm{0},\mbox{{diag}}(\mathbf{v}_{\phi}))`$
represents complex Gaussian noise with structured variance
$`\mathbf{v}_{\phi}=\text{vec}(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{H})`$, parameterized by
low-rank non-negative matrices $`\mathbf{W},\mathbf{H}`$. The parameters
$`\phi=\{\mathbf{W},\mathbf{H}\}`$ are optimized using the EM (EM)
algorithm \[22\]. The M-step maximizes the expected log-likelihood
$`\mathbb{E}_{p(\mathbf{s}|\mathbf{x})}\left\{\log p_{\phi}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{s})\right\}`$,
with the expectation approximated by Monte Carlo sampling from the
posterior
$`p(\mathbf{s}|\mathbf{x})\propto p_{\phi}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{s})p(\mathbf{s})`$
in the E-step.

Posterior sampling is performed by replacing the unconditional score in
(2) with a conditional one:
$`\nabla_{\mathbf{s}_{t}}\log p(\mathbf{s}_{t}|\mathbf{x})=\nabla_{\mathbf{s}_{t}}\log p_{\phi}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{s}_{t})+\nabla_{\mathbf{s}_{t}}\log p_{t}(\mathbf{s}_{t})`$.
The second term is approximated by the trained score model
$`\mathbf{S}_{\theta^{*}}(\mathbf{s}_{t},{t})`$. Since
$`p_{\phi}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{s}_{t})`$ is intractable for $`t>0`$, it
is approximated under an uninformative prior assumption on
$`\mathbf{s}`$ \[13\], yielding

```math
\tilde{p}_{\phi}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{s}_{t})\sim\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}\Big(\mathsf{e}^{\gamma t}\mathbf{s}_{t},\,{\sigma_{t}^{2}}\mathsf{e}^{2\gamma t}\mathbf{I}+\mbox{{diag}}(\mathbf{v}_{\phi})\Big). \tag{4}
```

The resulting conditional reverse SDE is then expressed as

```math
\mathsf{d}\mathbf{s}_{t}=\Big[\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{s}_{t})-g_{t}^{2}\Big(\mathbf{S}_{\theta^{*}}(\mathbf{s}_{t},{t})+\lambda_{t}\nabla_{\mathbf{s}_{t}}\log\tilde{p}_{\phi}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{s}_{t})\Big)\Big]\mathsf{d}t+g_{t}\mathsf{d}\mathbf{\bar{w}}, \tag{5}
```

where $`\lambda_{t}>0`$ balances the likelihood’s impact. This gradient
guidance approach enables the reverse process to generate speech signals
that are consistent with $`\mathbf{x}`$. The above reverse SDE is
numerically solved to estimate clean speech for the current value of
$`\phi`$, which is then used to update $`\phi`$. The EM steps are
repeated until convergence, typically within five iterations \[13\]. A
more efficient algorithm was recently proposed \[14\], in which $`\phi`$
is updated at each reverse iteration using an intermediate speech
estimate derived from Tweedie’s formula \[23\]:

```math
\hat{\mathbf{s}}_{0,t}=\mathbb{E}_{p_{t0}(\mathbf{s}|\mathbf{s}_{t})}[\mathbf{s}]\approx\frac{\mathbf{s}_{t}+\sigma_{t}^{2}\mathbf{S}_{\theta^{*}}(\mathbf{s}_{t},t)}{\mathsf{e}^{-\gamma t}}. \tag{6}
```

This method avoids a full reverse SDE process at each step to generate
speech samples for updating $`\phi`$, significantly improving
computational efficiency. These unsupervised approaches stand in
contrast to supervised frameworks \[4, 5\], which directly learn the
conditional score
$`\nabla_{\mathbf{s}_{t}}\log p(\mathbf{s}_{t}|\mathbf{x})`$ during
training from paired clean and noisy speech data.

## III Proposed framework

We present DEPSE (Diffusion-based Explicit Posterior Sampling for Speech
Enhancement), which integrates the diffusion prior and the observation
model by deriving the conditional transition distribution. The proposed
framework concerns only the E-step of the EM algorithm, while the M-step
remains identical to that used in prior work \[14\]. Section III-A
revisits diffusion-based prior sampling as used in \[13, 14\].
Section III-B introduces DEPSE-IL, which removes the need for the
balancing hyperparameter $`\lambda_{t}`$ while still approximating the
intractable likelihood (IL). Section III-C presents DEPSE-TL, which
defines a diffusion SDE over noisy speech, resulting in a tractable
likelihood (TL).

### III-A Diffusion prior sampling formulation

We adopt the Euler–Maruyama method as a numerical solver for the reverse
SDE (2) with time discretization. This yields a finite sequence of
latent variables, denoted by $`\{\mathbf{s}_{i}\}_{i=0}^{N}`$, which
form a Markov chain
$`\mathbf{s}_{0}\rightarrow\mathbf{s}_{1}\rightarrow\ldots\rightarrow\mathbf{s}_{N}`$,
with

```math
p(\mathbf{s}_{0},\ldots,\mathbf{s}_{N})=p(\mathbf{s}_{N})\prod_{i=1}^{N}p(\mathbf{s}_{{i-1}}|\mathbf{s}_{i}). \tag{7}
```

Discretizing (2) and inserting the score model leads to:

```math
{\mathbf{s}}_{{i-1}}={\mathbf{s}}_{i}-\mathbf{f}_{i}\Delta{\tau}+g_{\tau_{i}}^{2}\mathbf{S}_{\theta^{*}}(\mathbf{s}_{i},\tau_{i})\Delta{\tau}+g_{\tau_{i}}\sqrt{\Delta{\tau}}\bm{\zeta}, \tag{8}
```

with $`\mathbf{s}_{N}\sim\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}(\bm{0},{\bf I})`$.
Here, $`\{\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{N}\}`$ is an equally spaced sequence of
time steps in $`[0,T]`$ with a discretization width of
$`\Delta{\tau}=T/N`$,
$`\bm{\zeta}\sim\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}(\bm{0},{\bf I})`$, and
$`\mathbf{f}_{i}=-\gamma\mathbf{s}_{i}`$. By applying a similar
discretization to the forward SDE in (1), we obtain:

```math
p(\mathbf{s}_{i}|\mathbf{s}_{0})=\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathsf{e}^{-\gamma\tau_{i}}\mathbf{s}_{0},\sigma_{\tau_{i}}^{2}\mathbf{I}),\quad i\geq 1. \tag{9}
```

Since we have access to an approximate score function, we can improve
the numerical SDE solver by incorporating a score-based Markov Chain
Monte Carlo (MCMC) method, such as Langevin MCMC, used as a corrector
step in prior works \[1, 13, 14\]. Combined with (8), unconditional
sampling is then characterized by a backward transition distribution:

```math
p(\mathbf{s}_{{i-1}}|\mathbf{s}_{i})=\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}\Big(\bm{\mu}^{\mathsf{back}}(\mathbf{s}_{i}),\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{back}}_{i}\Big), \tag{10}
```

with

```math
\left\{\mathopen{}\begin{array}[]{l}\bm{\mu}^{\mathsf{back}}(\mathbf{s}_{i})=\mathbf{h}({\mathbf{s}}_{i})-\mathbf{f}_{i}\Delta{\tau}+g_{\tau_{i}}^{2}\mathbf{S}_{\theta^{*}}(\mathbf{h}({\mathbf{s}}_{i}),\tau_{i})\Delta{\tau}\\[5.69054pt]
\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{back}}_{i}=g_{\tau_{i}}^{2}\Delta{\tau}{\bf I}.\end{array}\right. \tag{11}
```

where $`\mathbf{h}({\mathbf{s}}_{i})`$ is the result of one-step
Langevin MCMC:

```math
\mathbf{h}({\mathbf{s}}_{i})=\mathbf{s}_{i}+\epsilon_{\tau_{i}}{\mathbf{S}_{\theta^{*}}(\mathbf{s}_{i},\tau_{i})}+\sqrt{2\epsilon_{\tau_{i}}}\bm{\zeta},\quad\bm{\zeta}\sim\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}). \tag{12}
```

Here, $`\epsilon_{\tau_{i}}=(\sigma_{\tau_{i}}\cdot r)^{2}`$ denotes the
Langevin step size ($`r>0`$) \[24\]. Starting from Gaussian noise, i.e.,
$`\mathbf{s}_{N}\sim\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}(\bm{0},{\bf I})`$, the
above conditional sampling procedure is iterated to ultimately generate
a speech signal $`\mathbf{s}_{0}`$ from the learned distribution.

### III-B Explicit posterior sampling for speech enhancement

While diffusion prior sampling effectively captures the distribution of
clean speech $`\mathbf{s}`$, it does not incorporate any information
about the noisy observation $`\mathbf{x}`$. Therefore, we target the
conditional distribution for SE, expressed as follows:

```math
p_{\phi}(\mathbf{s}_{0},\ldots,\mathbf{s}_{N}|\mathbf{x})=p(\mathbf{s}_{N})\prod_{i=1}^{N}p_{\phi}(\mathbf{s}_{{i-1}}|\mathbf{s}_{i},\mathbf{x}). \tag{13}
```

We aim to approximate the conditional transition distribution
$`p_{\phi}(\mathbf{s}_{{i-1}}|\mathbf{s}_{i},\mathbf{x})`$ given the
current $`\phi`$, which is expressed as

1: Input: $`\mathbf{x}`$, $`\{\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{N}\}`$

2:
$`{\mathbf{s}}_{N}\sim\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbf{x},\sigma^{2}_{\tau_{N}}\mathbf{I})`$

3: for $`i=N,\ldots,1`$ do

4:   Update
$`\{\bm{\mu}^{\mathsf{back}}(\mathbf{s}_{i}),\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{back}}_{i}\}`$
using (11)

5:   Update
$`\{\bm{\mu}^{\texttt{IL}}_{\phi}(\mathbf{s}_{i},\mathbf{x}),\bm{\Sigma}^{\texttt{IL}}_{s,i}(\phi)\}`$
using (18)

6:
  $`\mathbf{s}_{{i-1}}=\bm{\mu}^{\texttt{IL}}_{\phi}(\mathbf{s}_{i},\mathbf{x})+\sqrt{\bm{\Sigma}^{\texttt{IL}}_{s,i}(\phi)}\bm{\zeta},\hskip 9.24994pt\bm{\zeta}\sim\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}(\bm{0},\,{\bf I})`$

7:
  $`\hat{\mathbf{s}}_{0,i}=(\mathbf{s}_{i}+\sigma_{\tau_{i}}^{2}\mathbf{S}_{\theta^{*}}(\mathbf{s}_{i},\tau_{i}))/\mathsf{e}^{-\gamma\tau_{i}}`$

8:
  $`\phi=\{\mathbf{W},\mathbf{H}\}\leftarrow\mathsf{NMF}(|\mathbf{x}-\hat{\mathbf{s}}_{0,i}|^{2})`$

9: end for

10: Output: $`\hat{\mathbf{s}}={\mathbf{s}}_{0}`$

Algorithm 1 DEPSE-IL

```math
p_{\phi}(\mathbf{s}_{{i-1}}|\mathbf{s}_{i},\mathbf{x})\propto p_{\phi}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{s}_{{i-1}})\,p(\mathbf{s}_{{i-1}}|\mathbf{s}_{i}). \tag{14}
```

The second term is provided by the prior transition distribution in
(10), and for the likelihood term, we write

```math
p_{\phi}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{s}_{{i-1}})=\int p_{\phi}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{s}_{0})\,p(\mathbf{s}_{0}|\mathbf{s}_{{i-1}})\,\mathsf{d}\mathbf{s}_{0}. \tag{15}
```

Similar to \[13, 14\], we make an uninformative prior assumption for
$`\mathbf{s}_{0}`$, leading to
$`p(\mathbf{s}_{0}|\mathbf{s}_{{i-1}})\propto p(\mathbf{s}_{{i-1}}|\mathbf{s}_{0})`$.
Combined with (9), we obtain:

```math
p_{\phi}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{s}_{{i-1}})\approx\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}\Big(\mathsf{e}^{\gamma\tau_{i-1}}\mathbf{s}_{i-1},\,\bm{\Sigma}_{x,i}^{\texttt{IL}}(\phi)\Big), \tag{16}
```

where
$`\bm{\Sigma}_{x,i}^{\texttt{IL}}(\phi)=\frac{\sigma_{\tau_{i-1}}^{2}}{\mathsf{e}^{-2\gamma\tau_{i-1}}}{\bf I}+\mbox{{diag}}(\mathbf{v}_{\phi})`$.
The conditional transition distribution then takes the following form

```math
p_{\phi}(\mathbf{s}_{{i-1}}|\mathbf{s}_{i},\mathbf{x})=\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}\Big(\bm{\mu}^{\texttt{IL}}_{\phi}(\mathbf{s}_{i},\mathbf{x}),\,\bm{\Sigma}^{\texttt{IL}}_{s,i}(\phi)\Big) \tag{17}
```

with

```math
\small\left\{\mathopen{}\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\bm{\mu}^{\texttt{IL}}_{\phi}(\mathbf{s}_{i},\mathbf{x})\;=\;\bm{\Sigma}^{\texttt{IL}}_{s,i}(\phi)\biggl(\frac{\bm{\mu}^{\mathsf{back}}(\mathbf{s}_{i})}{\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{back}}_{i}}\;+\;\frac{\mathbf{x}}{\mathsf{e}^{-\gamma\tau_{i-1}}\bm{\Sigma}_{x,i}^{\texttt{IL}}(\phi)}\biggr),\\[8.53581pt]
\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}^{\texttt{IL}}_{s,i}(\phi)\;=\;\frac{\mathsf{e}^{-2\gamma\tau_{i-1}}\bm{\Sigma}_{x,i}^{\texttt{IL}}(\phi)\,\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{back}}_{i}}{\mathsf{e}^{-2\gamma\tau_{i-1}}\bm{\Sigma}_{x,i}^{\texttt{IL}}(\phi)\;+\;\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{back}}_{i}}.\end{array}\right. \tag{18}
```

Speech enhancement is performed through iterative sampling from the
conditional transition distribution (17), with the parameter $`\phi`$
updated at each step using the Tweedie-based speech estimate given in
(6). The complete DEPSE-IL procedure is summarized in Algorithm 1. In

### III-C Incorporating noisy speech diffusion process

We extend DEPSE-IL by introducing a diffusion process over the noisy
speech signal $`\mathbf{x}`$, inspired by \[24, 1, 26\], which define a
measurement-space diffusion to simplify likelihood computation in the
reverse process. Unlike DEPSE-IL and earlier methods \[13, 14\], which
approximate the time-dependent likelihood with an uninformative prior
assumption (see (4), (16)), this second approach, DEPSE-TL, yields a
tractable likelihood and avoids such approximations. We define the set
of latent variables $`\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=0}^{N}`$
($`\mathbf{x}_{0}=\mathbf{x}`$), following the same forward SDE as in
(1). Similar to (9), we can write
$`p(\mathbf{x}_{i-1}|\mathbf{x})=\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathsf{e}^{-\gamma\tau_{i-1}}\mathbf{x},\,\sigma^{2}_{\tau_{i-1}}{\bf I})`$.
Given the observation model $`\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{s}+\mathbf{n}`$, the
time-dependent likelihood distribution can be computed in closed-form:

```math
p_{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{{i-1}}|\mathbf{s}_{{i-1}})=\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}\Big(\mathbf{s}_{{i-1}},\,\bm{\Sigma}_{x,i}^{\texttt{TL}}(\phi)\Big), \tag{19}
```

with
$`\bm{\Sigma}_{x,i}^{\texttt{TL}}(\phi)={\mathsf{e}^{-2\gamma\tau_{i-1}}}\mbox{{diag}}(\mathbf{v}_{\phi})`$.
The corresponding posterior distribution takes the following form:

```math
p_{\phi}(\mathbf{s}_{{i-1}}|\mathbf{s}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{i-1})\propto p_{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{i-1}|\mathbf{s}_{{i-1}})\,p(\mathbf{s}_{{i-1}}|\mathbf{s}_{i}). \tag{20}
```

Substituting the expressions for the two distributions on the right, the
conditional transition distribution is derived as:

```math
p_{\phi}(\mathbf{s}_{{i-1}}|\mathbf{s}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{i-1})=\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}\Big(\bm{\mu}^{\texttt{TL}}_{\phi}(\mathbf{s}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{i-1}),\,\bm{\Sigma}^{\texttt{TL}}_{s,i}(\phi)\Big), \tag{21}
```

where the mean and covariance are given by:

```math
\small\left\{\mathopen{}\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\bm{\mu}^{\texttt{TL}}_{\phi}(\mathbf{s}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{i-1})\;=\;\bm{\Sigma}^{\texttt{TL}}_{s,i}(\phi)\biggl(\frac{\bm{\mu}^{\mathsf{back}}(\mathbf{s}_{i})}{\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{back}}_{i}}\;+\;\frac{\mathbf{x}_{i-1}}{\bm{\Sigma}_{x,i}^{\texttt{TL}}(\phi)}\biggr),\\[8.53581pt]
\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}^{\texttt{TL}}_{s,i}(\phi)\;=\;\frac{\bm{\Sigma}_{x,i}^{\texttt{TL}}(\phi)\,\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{back}}_{i}}{\bm{\Sigma}_{x,i}^{\texttt{TL}}(\phi)\;+\;\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{back}}_{i}}.\end{array}\right. \tag{22}
```

## IV Experiments

Baselines. We compare the proposed algorithms with UDiffSE+
(unsupervised-generative) \[14\], Conv-TasNet (supervised, adapted for
SE and trained with the SNR objective) \[12\], and SGMSE+
(supervised-generative) \[4\]. These are not state-of-the-art (SOTA), as
we focus on robustness under domain mismatch rather than SOTA
performance.

Model architectures. For the unsupervised and SGMSE+ algorithms, we use
a lightweight variant of the Noise Conditional Score Network (NCSN++)
\[4\], with 5.2 M parameters. All networks, including Conv-TasNet, are
trained from scratch.

Hyperparameter setting. Speech data are sampled at 16 kHz. STFT is
computed using a Hann window of size 510 with 75% overlap. SDE
hyperparameters follow \[13\]. The reverse SDE is discretized into
$`N=30`$ steps. In UDiffSE+, we set $`\lambda_{\tau_{i}}=1.5`$ for even
$`i`$, and $`0`$ otherwise, as suggested in \[14\].

Dataset. We use WSJ0-QUT \[27\] and VoiceBank-DEMAND (VB-DMD) \[28\].
WSJ0-QUT is constructed by mixing clean speech from the Wall Street
Journal corpus (WSJ0) \[29\] with noise from QUT-Noise \[30\] (e.g.,
car, café, street, living room, kitchen). VB-DMD combines clean speech
from the Voice Bank corpus (VB) with noise from the DEMAND dataset
\[31\] (e.g., living room, office space, bus, open-area cafeteria,
public square, restaurant, subway). SE models are trained either on the
WSJ0-QUT training set (25 hours) or on the VB-DMD corpus (9.39 hours),
and evaluated on the test sets of WSJ0-QUT (1.48 hours) or VB-DMD test
set (0.58 hour). Unsupervised models are trained only on the
corresponding clean speech data. Noisy speech signals in WSJ0-QUT are
generated with SNRs of {-5, 0, 5} dB (for both training and testing). In
VB-DMD, SNRs are {0, 5, 10, 15} dB for training and {2.5, 7.5, 12.5,
17.5} dB for testing.

Evaluation metrics. We report standard SE metrics: scale-invariant

Results. Tables I and II report the average metric values for evaluation
on the WSJ0-QUT and VB-DMD test sets, respectively, both with matched
and mismatched training sets. Boldfaced and underlined scores denote
statistically significant improvements ($`p<0.05`$) based on paired
t-tests for related samples. Among the unsupervised algorithms, DEPSE-TL
consistently outperforms DEPSE-IL and UDiffSE+ in terms of SI-SDR, PESQ,
and ESTOI, across almost all evaluation setups. For instance, DEPSE-TL
achieves a notable SI-SDR improvement over UDiffSE+ when trained and
evaluated on VB-DMD (13.87 dB vs. 10.40 dB); similarly, it outperforms
UDiffSE+ on PESQ (2.17 vs. 2.05) and ESTOI (0.59 vs. 0.52) when trained
on VB and tested on WSJ0-QUT. These results show that performance
improves without a trade-off hyperparameter or likelihood approximation.

We also observe that DEPSE-IL performs comparably to or slightly better
than UDiffSE+ across all settings. DEPSE-TL, on the other hand, exhibits
more performance boost by providing a tractable likelihood. Noteworthy,
the superiority of DEPSE-TL in SI-SDR does not imply better SI-SIR and
SI-SAR. In fact, DEPSE-TL tends to introduce less artifacts (higher
SI-SAR) than UDiffSE+ and DEPSE-IL, but retains slightly more residual
interference (lower SI-SIR). Overall, the gain in SI-SAR outweighs the
loss in SI-SIR, resulting in higher SI-SDR and thus improved perceptual
quality and intelligibility, as reflected by the PESQ and ESTOI metrics.

The supervised baselines, SGMSE+ and Conv-TasNet, achieve the highest
performance across most metrics, except when trained on WSJ0-QUT and
evaluated on VB-DMD, where DEPSE-TL outperforms them. When training and
testing are conducted on different corpora, supervised models exhibit
significant performance drops. For example, training on VB-DMD and
evaluating on WSJ0-QUT leads to a 64.6% SI-SDR decrease for SGMSE+ (8.53
$`\rightarrow`$ 3.02) and a 53.8% drop for Conv-TasNet (12.63
$`\rightarrow`$ 5.83). In contrast, DEPSE-TL experiences an SI-SDR drop
of 31.6% (3.89 $`\rightarrow`$ 2.66), highlighting the higher robustness
of DEPSE-TL to training-test domain mismatch. This robustness of
unsupervised algorithms is more pronounced in Table II.

## V Conclusion

We introduced two diffusion-based, unsupervised speech enhancement
algorithms that sample from an explicitly derived conditional transition
distribution. Unlike previous approaches, which rely on posterior-score
guidance with approximated likelihoods and adjust only the mean of the
prior and require a tuned hyperparameter, our first method computes both
the conditional mean and variance without tuning. Our second method
further introduces a forward diffusion over noisy speech, enabling exact
likelihood computation. Experiments show that this method consistently
outperforms prior unsupervised baselines in speech quality,
intelligibility, and robustness under mismatched conditions. The results
also demonstrate greater robustness to shifts between training and test
conditions compared to supervised baselines.
