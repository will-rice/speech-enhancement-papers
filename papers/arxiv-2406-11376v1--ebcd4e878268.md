# Spatially constrained vs. unconstrained filtering in neural spatiospectral filters for multichannel speech enhancement

Annika Briegleb   Walter Kellermann ^(†)^(†)thanks: This work has been
accepted to EUSIPCO 2024. Affiliation: Multimedia Communications and
Signal Processing  
Friedrich-Alexander-Universität Erlangen-Nürnberg, Erlangen, Germany  
{annika.briegleb, walter.kellermann}@fau.de

###### Abstract

When using artificial neural networks for multichannel speech
enhancement, filtering is often achieved by estimating a complex-valued
mask that is applied to all or one reference channel of the input
signal. The estimation of this mask is based on the noisy multichannel
signal and, hence, can exploit spatial and spectral cues simultaneously.
While it has been shown that exploiting spatial and spectral cues
jointly is beneficial for the speech enhancement result, the mechanics
of the interplay of the two inside the neural network are still largely
unknown. In this contribution, we investigate how two conceptually
different NSSF exploit spatial cues depending on the training target
signal and show that, while one NSSF always performs spatial filtering,
the other one is selective in leveraging spatial information depending
on the task at hand. These insights provide better understanding of the
information the NSSFs use to make their prediction and, thus, allow to
make informed decisions regarding their design and deployment.

###### Index Terms: 

spatial filtering, multichannel speech enhancement, DNN interpretability

## I Introduction

\Acp

ANN used for mask-based multichannel speech enhancement, denoted as
NSSF, can leverage spatial and spectral information jointly to estimate
the desired signal \[1\]. However, the interaction between spatial and
spectral filtering in these NSSF is still largely unknown. Yet,
understanding how, especially, spatial information is used inside an
NSSF would provide insights into the proverbial black box, and allows to
identify improvements and suitable application scenarios for this
specific NSSF.

For mask-based NSSF that operate according to the filter-and-sum
approach and estimate one mask for each channel, e.g., \[2, 3, 4, 5\],
it can be expected that spatial filtering is indeed performed due to the
combination of all channels that enables signal phase alignment
(cf. \[6\]). However, in \[7\] the JNF (JNF) has been proposed based on
\[8\], which uses all channels to estimate a single-channel mask for
speech enhhancement. While spatial selectivity has been shown for the
JNF for a specific spatially constrained target speaker extraction
scenario \[7\], it is unclear whether spatial information is extracted
inside the NSSF for other scenarios and how the spatial information
influences the estimated mask. Thus, in the following, we will analyze
how well spatial information is represented in the JNF in both spatially
constrained and unconstrained scenarios. Note that the spatial
constraint will be imposed via the training scenario of the NSSF and not
via a constrained optimization criterion (cf., e.g., the derivation of
the MVDR (MVDR) BF (BF) \[9\]), or a cost function (cf., e.g.,
independent vector analysis \[10\]). For comparison, we will also show
the spatial information represented in an NSSF that uses the
filter-and-sum approach, the COSPA (COSPA) \[2\]. Moreover, we will
discuss the influence of two different training target signal options on
the spatial filtering behavior of both NSSF.

Sec. II introduces the signal model, the spatially constrained and
unconstrained training scenarios, the training target signals, and the
two NSSF under consideration. Sec. III provides details about the
experimental setup. The results are presented and discussed in Sec. IV,
and Sec. V concludes the paper.

## II Spatially constrained vs. unconstrained filtering with NSSFs

A signal $`X_{m}(f,\tau)`$ in the STFT (STFT) domain, with frequency bin
index $`f=1,\dots,F`$ and time frame index $`\tau=1,\dots,T`$, captured
by microphone $`m=1,\dots,M`$, is given as

```math
X_{m}(f,\tau)=\sum_{q=1}^{Q}D_{qm}(f,\tau)+N_{\text{mic},m}(f,\tau). \tag{1}
```

$`D_{qm}(f,\tau)=G^{\ast}_{qm}(f,\tau)S_{q}(f,\tau)`$ is the signal of
speaker $`q`$ captured by the $`m`$-th microphone given as the dry
source signal $`S_{q}(f,\tau)`$ filtered by the transfer function
$`G_{qm}(f,\tau)`$ from the position of source $`q`$ to the $`m`$-th
microphone. The sum of all sources
$`q\in\mathcal{D},|\mathcal{D}|\geq 1`$, is considered as the desired
signal. $`N_{\text{mic},m}(f,\tau)`$ is the sensor noise of
microphone $`m`$. $`\mathbf{X}_{m}`$ denotes channel $`m`$ for the
entire observation interval, and the multichannel signal is denoted by
$`\mathbf{X}`$. When considering a filter-and-sum approach using the
estimated masks $`\mathcal{M}_{m}(f,\tau)`$, the estimated desired
signal $`\hat{S}(f,\tau)`$ is found as

```math
\hat{S}(f,\tau)=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathcal{M}_{m}(f,\tau)X_{m}(f,\tau). \tag{2}
```

When only one mask $`\mathcal{M}`$ is estimated and applied to a
reference channel, the estimated desired signal is given as

```math
\hat{S}(f,\tau)=\mathcal{M}(f,\tau)X_{\text{ref}}(f,\tau). \tag{3}
```

In the following, we will discuss the spatially constrained and
unconstrained scenarios which will be used to analyze the spatial
filtering behaviour of the NSSF.

### II-A Analysis scenarios

In order to identify whether and where spatial information is
represented inside the NSSF, the NSSF have to be trained in a way that
allows to uncover the spatial information represented in their hidden
features. We propose two analysis scenarios for training. The spatially
unconstrained scenario, comprises $`Q=2`$ speakers placed at two
different positions in a room. There are no constraints on the positions
of the speakers except that the difference of their doa $`\theta_{q}`$
(cf. Sec. III-A) is sufficiently large for spatially selective
processing. The two speakers are active alternatingly, hence, only one
speaker is active at any given time. This alternating activity pattern
allows to associate any time frame $`\tau`$ in a signal with the spatial
information of the corresponding active speaker. The NSSF will be
trained to preserve both speakers in $`\hat{\mathbf{S}}`$, i.e.,
$`\mathcal{D}=\{1,2\}`$. This scenario and training mode are chosen
purely to analyze, depending on the training target signal
(cf. Sec. II-B), whether the NSSF extract spatial information without
explicitly being told to do so.

The spatially constrained scenario is a target speaker extraction
scenario with $`Q=2`$ speakers at different positions in a room that are
active alternatingly. One of the speakers, the target speaker, will
always be positioned at a predefined fixed target doa
$`\theta_{\text{target}}`$ relative the microphone array. The NSSF are
trained to extract only the target speaker, i.e., $`|\mathcal{D}|=1`$.
Since speaker identities change across the training and test datasets,
the NSSF have to learn during training which doa is the desired one.
Hence, the setup of the scene provides a spatial constraint for the
NSSF.

### II-B Training target signals

Apart from the training scenario, the task of an ANN (ANN) is defined
via the training target signal, i.e., the desired signal to be estimated
provided to the ANN during training. For speech enhancement and target
speaker extraction, the target signal is typically the noise-free
desired signal. However, this could either be the dry source signal
$`\mathbf{S}_{q\in\mathcal{D}}`$ or the noise-free signal captured by a
reference microphone $`\mathbf{D}_{q\in\mathcal{D}\text{,ref}}`$ or a
processed version of any of the two. The choice of the target signal
crucially affects the spatial filtering and the task the NSSF has to
perform. The dry target signal adds dereverberation to the task of the
NSSF, while, e.g., a BF-filtered target signal implicitly imposes
emulating the BF as a task to the NSSF.

For our investigations, we will consider the dry source signal
$`\mathbf{S}_{q\in\mathcal{D}}`$ and a DSB (DSB)-filtered version of the
signal $`\mathbf{D}_{q\in\mathcal{D}}`$ (DSB target) as training target
signals for both NSSF. We choose these two since the JNF has been
proposed with a dry target signal \[7\] and COSPA has been proposed with
a BF-filtered target signal \[2\]. We choose the DSB as BF since our
focus in this paper is purely on spatial filtering. Note that when
training with the dry target signal for the spatially unconstrained
training scenario, where both speakers are considered desired speakers,
it is not necessary to exploit spatial cues to perform the task.

### II-C NSSFs

The two NSSF chosen for analysis differ significantly in their
architectural and conceptional design. Nevertheless, they both use
layers with memory, such as LSTM (LSTM) \[11\] or GRU (GRU) \[12\]
layers, which have already been shown to be crucial for extracting
spatial information \[6\]. These layers usually require input data with
two dimensions: the sequence dimension and the feature dimension. The
sequence dimension, which for time series is usually the time axis, is
not modified during processing. We will briefly review the architectures
of the two NSSF with focus on the layers with memory in the following.

Fig. 1: Architecture of the FT-JNF (adapted from \[7\]). Decompression

The JNF \[7\] shown in Fig. 1 consists of two BLSTM (BLSTM) layers
followed by a fully connected layer with a hyperbolic tangent activation
function. The real and imaginary part of the complex-valued input signal
are stacked for processing (indicated by factor $`2`$ in Fig. 1). The
first BLSTM layer uses a wide-band arrangement of the input data, where
the frequency axis is used as the sequence axis for processing. The
second BLSTM layer uses the time axis as the sequence axis
(‘narrow-band’ arrangement \[7\]). This arrangement of the features
preserves both the original time and frequency dimensions of the input
signal $`\mathbf{X}`$. We denote the features at the input of the first
BLSTM layer as $`\widetilde{\mathbf{h}}_{0}`$, the features after the
first BLSTM layer as $`\widetilde{\mathbf{h}}_{1}`$ and the features
after the second BLSTM layer as $`\widetilde{\mathbf{h}}_{2}`$. Unlike
in Fig. 1, the feature vectors will be represented time frame-wise as
vectors of size $`2FM,2FU_{1},2FU_{2}`$ in Sec. IV, where
$`U_{1},U_{2}`$ are the number of hidden units in the first and second
BLSTM layer respectively. The output of the fully connected layer is
decompressed according to \[13\] to obtain the mask $`\mathcal{M}`$ used
for estimating the desired signal $`\hat{\mathbf{S}}`$ according to
Eq. (3).

COSPA \[2\] consists of three building blocks, where only the middle
one, the compandor, can perform channel-dependent processing. As
depicted in Fig. 2, the compandor consists of two complex-valued fully
connected layers with a complex-valued GRU layer in between. We denote
the time frame-wise features before and after the GRU layer as
$`\widetilde{\mathbf{h}}_{\text{in/out}}(\tau)\in\mathbb{C}^{U_{\text{in/out}}}`$,
where $`U_{\text{in/out}}`$ denote the dimensionality of the features
before and after the GRU layer. COSPA estimates $`\hat{\mathbf{S}}`$
using Eq. (2).

## III Experimental Setup

In Sec. IV, we investigate whether and where spatial information can be
identified in the JNF, as a representative for multichannel NSSF that
apply single-channel masking, using the spatially unconstrained and
constrained training scenarios. For COSPA, we verify and extend the
results from \[6\]. We describe the experimental setup in the following.

### III-A Data

Both scenarios are simulated using a uniform linear microphone array and
the parameters given in Table I. We define the endfire directions of the
array as $`0\degree`$ and $`180\degree`$. All utterances are taken from
the TIMIT database \[14\]. For the spatially unconstrained scenario, the
training dataset named 2spk2pos-train consists of $`3000`$ sequences of
length $`7`$ s with two alternating speakers placed randomly in the room
but at least $`15\degree`$ apart from each other. Speaker 1 is active in
the first and third segment of the sequence and Speaker 2 in the second
segment. Speaker identities change across the dataset and the change in
activity happens randomly between 1 s and 3 s and 5 s and 6 s in each
sequence. The corresponding test set 2spk2pos contains $`180`$
sequences. Furthermore, we generate two more test sets with 50 sequences
each, where two different speakers are placed at the same position in
the room (2spk1pos) or one speaker is placed at two different positions
in the room (1spk2pos) to create the two sources respectively. These
test datasets allow to show that if a discrimination between the two
sources in the 2spk2pos test set is observable, it is due to spatial
information.

For the spatially constrained training scenario, the interfering speaker
is placed at least $`15\degree`$ away from the target speaker with
$`\theta_{\text{target}}=90\degree`$. The target speaker’s segment is
active for $`3`$ to $`5`$ s and can start anytime in the $`7`$ s long
sequence. The corresponding training set named 2spk2pos-1fix-train
contains $`3000`$ sequences and the test set 2spk2pos-1fix $`50`$
sequences. We generate the two test sets 2spk1pos-1fix and 1spk2pos-1fix
similarly to the unconstrained scenario with $`50`$ sequences each.

### III-B NSSFs

For the JNF, we use $`U_{1}\!=\!256`$, $`U_{2}\!=\!128`$, an STFT frame
length of $`512`$ time-domain samples and a $`50\%`$ frame shift as in
\[7\]. For COSPA, we use $`U_{\text{in}}\!=\!U_{\text{out}}\!=\!128`$,
an STFT frame length of $`1024`$ and a $`50\%`$ frame shift as in \[2\].
We train both NSSF on a SNR (SNR) cost function \[2\].

### III-C Measuring spatial information inside NSSFs

To assess the spatial information represented in the features
$`\widetilde{\mathbf{h}}_{*}(\tau)`$, we use a discriminative analysis
via $`k`$-means clustering \[15\] as proposed in \[6\]. If feature
vectors $`\widetilde{\mathbf{h}}_{*}(\tau)`$ contain discriminative
information about their dominant source, they should be clustered into
distinct clusters according to their dominant source. Due to the
alternating speaker activity in both training scenarios, the dominant
source of any time frame $`\tau`$ is known. We use $`k=Q+1=3`$ to
provide one cluster for each active source (which can be a speaker or
speaker position) and an additional cluster, the pause cluster, for time
frames without spatial cues, i.e., frames of silence. Clustering is
performed on the features $`\mathbf{h}_{*}(\tau)`$ which are the
features $`\widetilde{\mathbf{h}}_{*}(\tau)`$ normalized sequence-wise
by subtracting the mean and dividing by the standard deviation of the
features per hidden unit. A cluster is said to represent a source if the
majority of time frames assigned to this cluster belong to the
respective source. If, for a given sequence, both sources claim the same
cluster, the sequence is unresolved. We assess the expressiveness of the
clusters with respect to the spatial information contained in the
features using a grouping score, which measures how many feature vectors
belonging to the same source have been assigned to the same cluster. For
two sources, a grouping score of $`50\%`$ indicates no source-wise
clustering at all and a grouping score of $`100\%`$ indicates perfect
separation of the feature vectors into the two speaker clusters
according to their dominant source. Feature vectors assigned to the
pause cluster are neglected for computing the grouping score, since they
are assumed to be inexpressive with respect to spatial information.
Furthermore, we report the average percentage of unresolved sequences.
If no doa-dependent discrimination can be found for an experiment, the
grouping score is computed for all permutations of source-to-cluster
assignments and the highest score is kept. There are no unresolved
sequences for these cases. Since $`k`$-means clustering is
initialization-dependent, we report the results as average over five
trials with independent initialization.

## IV Results

We start by discussing the spatial information represented in both NSSF
for the spatially unconstrained training scenario. Table II confirms the
results for COSPA from \[6\] for the DSB target signal and extends them
by the results for the dry target signal. For the DSB target signal, the
grouping score above $`90\%`$ for $`\mathbf{h}_{\text{out}}(\tau)`$
indicates that the features $`\mathbf{h}_{\text{out}}(\tau)`$ contain
discriminative information about the two sources. The high grouping
score for $`\mathbf{h}_{\text{out}}(\tau)`$ in the 1spk2pos scenario and
the low grouping score in the 2spk1pos scenario show that the
discriminative information is indeed based on spatial cues, since even
the same speaker identity can be split into the two given positions and
two different speaker identities placed at the same position can no
longer be differentiated. Hence, COSPA extracts spatial information from
the input signal in the spatially unconstrained scenario for the DSB
target.

while spatial information is not as strongly represented in the features
of the compandor for the dry training target signal, it still provides
some discriminative information about the sources, even for a target
signal that does not enforce spatial filtering and for a training
scenario without spatial constraint.

Thus, while COSPA extracts spatial cues from the input signal even in
the spatially unconstrained scenario, the JNF only seems to do so if it
is enforced, e.g., by the training target. Since spatial filtering
behavior has been shown for the JNF for the spatially constrained
scenario in \[7\], we investigate next whether the spatial information
can be found in the hidden features of the JNF for this scenario.

The corresponding results for the DSB training target are presented in
Table III in the second block. Here, the grouping score for
$`\mathbf{h}_{1}(\tau)`$ for the 2spk2pos-1fix test set is higher than
for the unconstrained scenario and even higher for
$`\mathbf{h}_{2}(\tau)`$. For $`\mathbf{h}_{2}(\tau)`$, there are
significantly less unresolved sequences than for the spatially
unconstrained scenario. Hence, as expected, the features of the JNF
reflect more spatial information in the spatially constrained than in
the unconstrained scenario. Furthermore, while the first BLSTM layer
seems to be responsible for extracting the spatial information in the
spatially unconstrained scenario, the second BLSTM layer increases the
amount of spatially discriminative information in the features
$`\mathbf{h}_{2}(\tau)`$ significantly for the spatially constrained
scenario, which can especially be seen by the increase of the grouping
score by $`11.6`$ percentage points for the 1spk2pos-1fix test set and
the strong decrease in unresolved sequences from
$`\mathbf{h}_{1}(\tau)`$ to $`\mathbf{h}_{2}(\tau)`$. Fig. 4 shows
exemplary feature maps and the corresponding time-domain input signal
for this scenario, where the increased distinctiveness of the features
for the two sources is directly visible. For the dry target signal (last
block in Table III), the effect of the spatially constrained scenario on
the behavior of the JNF is even more drastic. While no spatial
information could be detected in the features of the JNF in the
spatially unconstrained scenario, the grouping score for
$`\mathbf{h}_{2}(\tau)`$ is higher than $`80\%`$ for both the
2spk2pos-1fix and the 1spk2pos-1fix test sets, which indicates clear
presence of spatial information in the features. On the other hand, no
spatial information can be detected in $`\mathbf{h}_{1}(\tau)`$. These
results clearly show that the JNF uses spatial information selectively
based on the training scenario and the training target signal.

## V Conclusion

We have investigated spatial filtering for two conceptually different
NSSF: COSPA as a filter-and-sum approach and the JNF as a multichannel
approach with single-channel masking. While it was expected that COSPA
performs spatial filtering under all circumstances due to the $`M`$
masks that are combined by the filter-and-sum approach, it is still
crucial to observe that the amount of spatial filtering varies with the
training target. For the FT-JNF, it was unclear how the spatial
information can contribute to the overall result in the first place
since it only uses a single-channel masking approach to estimate the
desired signal. Thus, it is even more important to observe that the
spatially constrained scenario forces the FT-JNF to leverage spatial
information explicitly and that the network internally makes use of the
spatial cues even though it only performs single-channel masking. These
insights into the NSSFs allow to understand that not every so-called
NSSF necessarily uses both spatial and spectral information under all
circumstances. This allows to make informed decisions about the design
and deployment of the NSSFs (e.g., \[16, 17\]) depending on the task,
i.e., training scenario and training target signal, at hand.
