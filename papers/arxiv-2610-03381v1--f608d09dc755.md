# Multiclass Speech Classification Under Noise Disparity

Mahdi Amiri    Sayantan Biswas    Mingchi Hou    Pascal Frossard    Ina
Kodrasi ^(†)^(†)thanks: This work was supportecd by the Swiss National
Science Foundation projects CRSII5_202228 on “Characterisation of motor
speech disorders and processes” and 200021_215187 on “Pathological
Speech Enhancement”.

###### Abstract

We investigate noise disparity in multi-class speech classification
tasks and develop a strategy to prevent classifiers from exploiting
class-dependent noise characteristics. Building on our previous work for
binary classification, we introduce a multi-class cross-augmentation
scheme that exposes each class to the noise characteristics of the other
classes, thereby removing the association between individual noise
conditions and class labels. We compare this training-based approach
with speech enhancement (SE) as a preprocessing strategy, which aims to
suppress noise-related cues directly from the input. Experiments on
multi-class emotion recognition show that cross-augmentation effectively
mitigates the effect of noise disparity across a range of
signal-to-noise-ratios, while SE has a detrimental effect on model
performance.

###### Index Terms: 

speech emotion recognition, spurious correlation, speech enhancement,
noise augmentation

## 1 Introduction

Self-supervised learning (SSL) foundation models have emerged as
powerful general-purpose speech representations, achieving
state-of-the-art performance across a wide range of classification tasks
such as emotion recognition \[23, 22\], pathological speech
detection \[2, 16, 15, 14\], or speaker verification \[8, 20\]. Models
such as wav2vec 2.0 \[4\] and XLS-R \[3\] provide rich speech
representations without requiring task-specific feature engineering, and
have therefore become increasingly prevalent in downstream speech
classification tasks.

However, the strong representational capacity of SSL models also makes
them susceptible to exploiting _spurious correlations_ in the training
data. A particularly problematic setting arises when noise
characteristics are systematically associated with class labels, a
phenomenon we refer to as _noise disparity_. Such disparity can
naturally arise when recordings from different classes are collected
under different acoustic conditions, with different recording devices or
in different environments. In this situation, noise becomes predictive
of the class label, allowing a classifier to achieve high performance by
exploiting noise-related cues rather than the speech characteristics
relevant to the target task. Importantly, this problem can remain hidden
when evaluation data share the same noise characteristics as the
training data, while causing substantial performance degradation when
the acoustic conditions change. For example, in \[26\] we showed that
binary pathological speech classifiers trained on the TORGO \[24\] and
UASpeech \[19\] datasets perform considerably better on non-speech than
on speech segments, demonstrating that they exploit recording-related
characteristics present in non-speech segments rather than
pathology-relevant properties of the speech signal. Similar reliance on
non-task-related cues has been observed in other speech classification
tasks \[10, 11\].

An intuitive way to mitigate noise disparity is to suppress the noise
from the recordings before classification. Speech enhancement (SE)
models \[21, 18\] are widely used as preprocessing modules for
downstream speech tasks \[25\], and therefore provide a natural
candidate for this purpose. In principle, if class-discriminative noise
is sufficiently suppressed, the classifier cannot exploit noise-related
cues. However, SE does not simply remove noise, as enhancement models
can also modify the speech signal and introduce artifacts whose
characteristics depend on the input noise, signal-to-noise ratio (SNR),
and enhancement model itself \[13\]. If these artifacts vary
systematically with the original noise conditions, they can themselves
become predictive of the class labels. Consequently, SE may replace one
source of spurious information with another, rather than eliminating the
underlying shortcut.

An alternative strategy is to instead modify the training data itself,
removing the association between noise and class label directly. To
suppress noise-related cues for the _binary_ pathological speech
detection task, we previously proposed an augmentation-based approach
that combines each training sample with a proxy of the noise extracted
from the opposite class \[1\]. This augmentation exposes both classes to
comparable noise characteristics, suppressing the association between
noise and class labels so that a classifier can only succeed by relying
on genuine speech content. This formulation, however, is defined for
pairs of samples. In the multiclass setting, a sample can be matched
with noise from several other classes, and it is not clear a priori
which noise, or which combination of noises, should be added.

In this paper, we propose an extension of the binary augmentation
approach from \[1\] to the multiclass setting, using multiclass speech
emotion recognition as an exemplary task. Additionally, we investigate
whether SE can provide an alternative means of mitigating noise-related
shortcuts. Our main contributions are as follows:

- •
  We demonstrate that noise disparity can induce spurious
  class-discriminative cues in multiclass speech classification,
  resulting in models that rely on noise characteristics rather than
  task-relevant speech information.
- •
  We extend our augmentation strategy \[1\] from binary to multiclass
  classification by comparing three cross-class noise augmentation
  schemes. We show that augmenting each sample with noise from all other
  classes effectively suppresses the noise-disparity shortcut across SNR
  conditions, while random and batch-pairing strategies are ineffective.
- •
  We systematically investigate SE as a commonly used preprocessing
  strategy for handling noisy inputs. We show that in the presence of
  noise disparity, SE models introduce spurious enhancement-dependent
  artifacts that also impair classification performance.

## 2 Preliminaries

Consider a $`C`$-class speech classification task, where
$`y_{i}^{c}(k)`$ denotes the $`i`$-th noisy speech sample from class
$`c`$ at time index $`k`$. Each noisy sample is modeled as

```math
y_{i}^{c}(k)=s_{i}^{c}(k)+n_{i}^{c}(k), \tag{1}
```

where $`s_{i}^{c}(k)`$ and $`n_{i}^{c}(k)`$ denote the corresponding
clean speech and noise signals, respectively. In practice only the noisy
mixture $`y_{i}^{c}(k)`$ is observed, while the clean speech and noise
components are not available. We define _noise disparity_ as a setting
in which the noise distribution depends on the class $`c`$, i.e.,
samples belonging to the same class are corrupted according to the same
noise distribution, while the noise distribution differs across classes.
This happens when recordings from different classes are collected under
different acoustic conditions. Consequently, noise characteristics can
become correlated with the class label and provide spurious cues for
classification.

To characterize the signal and noise levels, we define the average power
of a signal $`x(k)`$ of length $`K`$ as

```math
P_{x}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}x^{2}(k). \tag{2}
```

Accordingly, for a noisy signal $`y(k)=s(k)+n(k)`$, we define the SNR as

```math
\mathrm{SNR}_{y}^{n}=10\log{10}\frac{P_{s}}{P_{n}}. \tag{3}
```

The notations $`P_{x}`$ and $`\mathrm{SNR}_{y}^{n}`$ are used throughout
the remainder of this paper to denote the corresponding signal powers
and SNRs, respectively. For ease of readability, the time index $`k`$ is
omitted in the following.

## 3 Noise disparity in the two-class setting

We proposed a strategy for mitigating noise disparity in the two-class
classification setting in \[1\]. The underlying idea is to cross-augment
samples from the two classes with each other’s noise, such that the
resulting samples have matched noise characteristics. If this is
achieved, noise is no longer predictive of the class label and a
classifier can then only succeed by relying on genuine speech content.

Specifically, consider a sample $`y_{i}^{a}`$ from class $`c_{a}`$ and a
sample $`y^{b}_{j}`$ from the other class $`c_{b}`$, with $`a\neq b`$.
Their augmented versions are given by

```math
\displaystyle{\hat{y}^{a}}_{i}=\underbrace{s^{a}_{i}+n^{a}_{i}}_{y^{a}_{i}}+\Gamma_{b_{j}\rightarrow a_{i}}{\hat{n}^{b}}_{j},\;\;\hat{y}^{b}_{j}=\underbrace{s^{b}_{j}+n^{b}_{j}}_{y^{b}_{j}}+\Gamma_{a_{i}\rightarrow b_{j}}\hat{n}^{a}_{i}, \tag{4}
```

where $`\hat{n}^{a}_{i}`$ and $`\hat{n}^{b}_{j}`$ are the estimated
noises from samples $`y^{a}_{i}`$ and $`y^{b}_{j}`$, respectively, and
$`\Gamma_{b_{j}\rightarrow a_{i}}`$ and
$`\Gamma_{a_{i}\rightarrow b_{j}}`$ are scalar coefficients used to
scale these noise estimates when augmenting each sample.
Following \[1\], noise is estimated using the non-speech segments of
each sample, which are extracted with a voice activity detector
(VAD) \[6\]. For the two augmented samples $`{\hat{y}^{a}}_{i}`$ and
$`\hat{y}^{b}_{j}`$ in (4) to have the same noise characteristics, the
scalars $`\Gamma_{b_{j}\rightarrow a_{i}}`$ and
$`\Gamma_{a_{i}\rightarrow b_{j}}`$ should be computed such that (i) the
SNR of $`{\hat{y}^{a}}_{i}`$ and $`\hat{y}^{b}_{j}`$ with respect to
each of the individual noises is the same and (ii) the SNR of
$`{\hat{y}^{a}}_{i}`$ and $`\hat{y}^{b}_{j}`$ with respect to the total
noise is the same. These conditions are expressed through the system of
equations:

```math
\left\{\begin{aligned} \text{SNR}_{\hat{y}^{a}_{i}}^{n^{a}_{i}}&=\text{SNR}_{\hat{y}^{b}_{j}}^{\Gamma_{a_{i}\rightarrow b_{j}}{\hat{n}^{a}}_{i}}\\
\text{SNR}_{{\hat{y}^{a}}_{i}}^{\Gamma_{b_{j}\rightarrow a_{i}}{\hat{n}^{b}}_{j}}&=\text{SNR}_{{\hat{y}^{b}}_{j}}^{n^{b}_{j}}\\
\text{SNR}_{{\hat{y}^{a}}_{i}}^{n^{a}_{i}+\Gamma_{b_{j}\rightarrow a_{i}}{\hat{n}^{b}}_{j}}&=\text{SNR}_{{\hat{y}^{b}}_{j}}^{n^{b}_{j}+\Gamma_{a_{i}\rightarrow b_{j}}{\hat{n}^{a}}_{i}}\end{aligned}\right. \tag{5}
```

As shown in \[1\], the scalars $`\Gamma_{a_{i}\rightarrow b_{j}}`$ and
$`\Gamma_{b_{j}\rightarrow a_{i}}`$ satisfying (5) are given by

```math
\Gamma_{a_{i}\rightarrow b_{j}}=\sqrt{\frac{{P_{s}}^{b}_{j}}{{P_{s}}^{a}_{i}}},\;\;\Gamma_{b_{j}\rightarrow a_{i}}=\sqrt{\frac{{P_{s}}^{a}_{i}}{{P_{s}}^{b}_{j}}}, \tag{6}
```

ensuring that the two augmented samples $`{\hat{y}^{a}}_{i}`$ and
$`\hat{y}^{b}_{j}`$ have matched noise characteristics. Thus, in the
two-class setting, each sample can be cross-augmented with a noise
estimate from the other class using a uniquely defined scaling
coefficient.

## 4 Noise disparity in the multiclass setting

For $`C>2`$ classes, the formulation of Section 3 does not specify which
noise a sample should be augmented with, since the noises of $`C-1`$
other classes are available. The most direct extension is to augment
each sample with the estimated noise of every other class, so that all
augmented samples contain the same $`C`$ noise sources.
Generalizing (5), the augmented samples have matched noise
characteristics if their SNR is the same with respect to each individual
noise source and with respect to any combination of noise sources, which
amounts to $`2^{C}-1`$ conditions. Under the same assumptions as
in \[1\], these conditions are satisfied by the pairwise coefficients
of (6). However, stacking several noise signals onto a single sample
lowers its effective SNR, with the reduction increasing as the number of
classes grows. We therefore also consider two variants that randomly
introduce noise from only a single class per sample. In this case, the
formulation is equivalent to considering only pairwise conditions as in
the binary classification setting, without a guarantee of matching all
noise characteristics across all classes. In addition, we evaluate SE as
an alternative approach that aims to suppress noise entirely prior to
classification, rather than balancing it across classes.

1\. Augmentation with noise from all other classes.  Each noisy sample
is augmented with a scaled noise estimate from every other class, i.e.,

```math
\displaystyle{\hat{y}^{a}}_{i}=\underbrace{s^{a}_{i}+n^{a}_{i}}_{y^{a}_{i}}+\sum_{b\neq a}\Gamma_{b_{j}\rightarrow a_{i}}{\hat{n}^{b}}_{j}, \tag{7}
```

with each $`\Gamma_{b_{j}\rightarrow a_{i}}`$ determined as in (6).
Since every augmented sample contains contributions from the noise of
all classes and satisfies all possible conditions, no individual noise
pattern remains distinctive of any particular class. However, because
multiple noise components are added at once, the SNR of the augmented
sample decreases as the number of classes increases.

2\. Random augmentation with a single noise.  At each training epoch,
one noise sample is drawn from a randomly selected different class and
added using (6). Thus, only one noise component is introduced at a time,
and the SNR does not degrade as the number of classes increases. In
contrast to the previous strategy, this approach does not satisfy all
possible conditions in the multiclass setting and therefore does not
guarantee effective suppression of noise disparity.

3\. Pairing samples in each batch. Within each training batch, samples
from different classes are randomly paired and cross-augmented via (4),
which applies the exact symmetric two-class matching condition rather
than the random matching used in the previous strategy. Samples that
cannot be paired within a batch are instead assigned noise from a
randomly chosen sample of another class. We consider this variant in
addition to strategy 2 to test whether enforcing an exact mutual noise
match for at least some class pairs per batch is more effective than the
weaker additive exposure in strategy 2.

4\. Using speech enhancement as a preprocessing step.  An alternative
approach to mitigating noise disparity is to suppress noise before
training. To the best of our knowledge, the effectiveness of SE as a
preprocessing step for this purpose has not been systematically studied.
We therefore apply an enhancement model to the noisy samples prior to
training the model, aiming to reduce class-specific noise cues.
Specifically, we consider a complex-valued spectrogram-based regression
model (CR) \[21\] and a Schrödinger Bridge-based model (SB) \[17\],
representing discriminative and generative state-of-the-art SE
paradigms. Their implementation and training configurations are detailed
in Section 5.2.

## 5 Experimental Settings

### 5.1 Databases

To investigate noise disparity in a controlled setting, we require a
dataset in which the association between class labels and noise
characteristics can be explicitly controlled. To this end, we follow the
procedure of \[1\] and construct a synthetic noisy dataset by adding
selected environmental noises to clean speech samples. This allows us to
introduce noise disparity while keeping the underlying speech signal
unchanged.

_Clean dataset._  For the clean dataset, we use the IEMOCAP
dataset \[7\] which is a widely used benchmark for multiclass emotion
recognition. IEMOCAP comprises five sessions, each featuring one male
and one female speaker. Although the original dataset contains ten
emotion classes, we follow the commonly used four-class protocol,
considering the angry, neutral, sad, and happy classes (with excited
merged into happy) \[22, 23\]. These four classes comprise $`5,531`$
samples with a total duration of approximately $`7`$ hours.

_Noise dataset._  For generating the noisy samples, we use the DEMAND
noise dataset \[27\]. Specifically, we select the DKITCHEN, NPARK,
OMEETING, and PCAFETER noises. Each noise type is assigned to exactly
one emotion class, such that the noise characteristics are
systematically associated with the class labels. We consider three
scenarios corresponding to high, moderate, and random SNRs:

- A.
  Samples from all classes are corrupted with their respective noises at
  an SNR of $`20`$ dB.
- B.
  Samples from all classes are corrupted with their respective noises at
  an SNR of $`10`$ dB.
- C.
  Samples from all classes are corrupted with their respective noises at
  an SNR randomly chosen from the range of $`10`$ to $`20`$ dB.

### 5.2 Speech enhancement models

We consider two state-of-the-art SE models, the discriminative CR model
from \[21\] and the generative SB model from \[18\]. Both SE models
follow a U-Net architecture. We took the best checkpoint trained on the
widely used SE benchmark dataset WSJ0-CHiME3 \[12\]. This benchmark is
composed of clean speech from the English Wall Street Journal
dataset \[9\] and noise from the ChiME3 dataset \[5\].

### 5.3 Training Protocol

For conducting our experiments, we use a wav2vec2-based \[4\] approach.
We obtain the feature representation from the raw audio signal using the
last layer of the wav2vec 2.0 base model. We then pass these
representations through two linear layers, which project the
representation into 256 and 2 dimensions, respectively. We also use a
ReLU nonlinearity between the linear layers. We segment signals into
5-second chunks with 50% overlap. The final residual segment of each
utterance is also retained.

Training is done using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of
$`1\times 10^{-3}`$. We also use ReduceLROnPlateau with a patience of 5
and a factor of 0.5. Models are trained until a maximum number of
$`100`$ epochs or until the learning rate decreases below $`10^{-4}`$ of
the initial learning rate. The best model is chosen based on the loss
value on the validation set.

Training and evaluation is done through the commonly used
leave-one-session-out cross-validation setting \[23\]. At each fold, we
use one of the sessions as the test set, and the remaining four sessions
are split into a training set and a validation set with a ratio of 0.8
and 0.2, respectively. Performance is evaluated using the Unweighted
Accuracy (UA), which is commonly used for evaluating emotion recognition
systems with imbalanced emotion classes \[23\].

## 6 Experimental Results

In this section, we compare the effectiveness of different training
strategies for mitigating the effects of noise disparity. As a baseline,
we consider standard training in which the model is trained directly on
the noisy training data without any augmentation or preprocessing. This
baseline is compared with the proposed augmentation strategies. In
addition, we investigate the SE-based training strategy where the noisy
training data are first enhanced using either the CR or SB model and the
classifier is subsequently trained on the enhanced data. Furthermore, we
train and test the model on clean data to obtain a sense of its
performance when there is no noise disparity in the dataset. _The
obtained UA in this case is $`\mathbf{59.3\%}`$_.

For the augmentation-based approaches described in Section 4, augmenting
each signal with noise from another class according to (7) requires an
estimate of the corresponding noise signal. To investigate the effect of
potential errors in the estimated noise signals, we consider two
settings:

1.  1.  Oracle.  Since we generate the synthetic noisy dataset ourselves,
        the exact noise signal in each sample is known. In the oracle
        setting, we use this ground-truth noise signal during augmentation.
        This setting allows us to evaluate the augmentation strategy without
        errors in the noise estimate.
2.  2.  Practical.  In practice, the noise signal is unknown and must be
        estimated from the noisy speech. Following \[1\], we use the VAD
        from \[6\] to separate speech and non-speech segments of the noisy
        samples. Assuming that the non-speech segments contain no speech
        information, we use them as an estimate of the background noise for
        augmentation. Errors in this estimate therefore reflect the noise
        estimation errors encountered in a practical setting.

All models are evaluated on two common test sets, i.e., the original
noisy test set, which exhibits the same class-dependent noise as the
training data, and the corresponding clean test set. A model that relies
only on speech content cannot have a higher accuracy on the noisy test
set than on the clean one. A higher accuracy on the noisy test set
therefore indicates reliance on class-dependent noise cues, and the gap
between the two serves as our main measure of spurious-cue exploitation.
Since SE-based models are trained on enhanced data, and would also
receive enhanced inputs at deployment, we additionally evaluate them on
the noisy test set enhanced with the SB and the CR model. The clean-data
reference (UA of $`59.3\%`$) indicates what can be achieved from speech
content alone. Accuracy on enhanced data exceeding it therefore
indicates that the classifier exploits cues introduced or preserved by
SE rather than task-related information. Results for the augmentation
strategies are given in Table 1, and for the SE-based strategies in
Table 2.

As can be seen in Table 1, when the model is trained under standard
training, its performance on the noisy test set is much higher than on
the clean test set across all SNR settings. This confirms that the model
learns noise-discriminant cues rather than actual task-related cues.
When augmenting each sample with oracle noise from every other class,
our proposed method can effectively close the performance gap between
the noisy and clean test sets. This shows that given a precise estimate
of the noise, our proposed method can make training robust to spurious
correlations. Under the practical setup in the SNR setting of $`20`$ dB,
we also successfully close the gap between the noisy and clean test
sets, though this comes at the cost of lower overall performance
compared to the oracle setup. Under the practical setup in the SNR
settings of $`10`$ dB and random, the gap between noisy and clean test
set performance is reduced by a large margin but does not fully close.
We argue that with a better estimate of the noise, this remaining gap
could be closed to match that of the oracle scenario.

In the training strategy in which noise from another class is randomly
selected for each sample, clean set accuracy increases and noisy set
accuracy decreases compared with standard training. However, this
strategy is overall not effective, with the performance gap between
noisy and clean test sets remaining large. This trend can also be
observed in the strategy where we pair samples within each batch and
cross-augment their noise. We argue this occurs because the noise is
changed for each sample at every training epoch, encouraging the model
to become invariant to the augmented noise and instead focus on the
components that remain fixed across epochs. As a result, the model can
still exploit the original class-dependent noise characteristics,
leading to a persistent performance gap between the noisy and clean test
sets.

The enhancement-based strategies in Table 2 show a different behavior.
While training with either CR or SB results in low performance on the
noisy and clean test sets, performance is substantially higher on the
enhanced test sets, particularly when the same enhancement model is used
for training and testing. Notably, performance on the enhanced test sets
is also substantially higher than when training and testing on the clean
data (i.e., $`59.3`$%). Since the clean data contain only the
task-relevant speech information in our controlled setup, the additional
discriminative information in the enhanced data must originate from the
enhancement of the noisy signal. Thus, rather than removing the
class-dependent noise cues, SE appears to preserve or introduce
noise-dependent artifact cues that can be exploited by the classifier.
Consequently, SE does not provide an effective solution for suppressing
noise disparity.

In summary, experimental results show that augmenting each sample with
the noise of every other class is the only strategy that removes the
reliance on class-dependent noise cues in multiclass settings. In
contrast, single-class augmentation leaves a substantial gap between
noisy and clean test set performance, while SE introduces new spurious
cues rather than removing existing ones.

## 7 Conclusion

We investigated noise disparity between classes in multiclass speech
classification, using a synthetic dataset built by introducing
controlled noise disparity into the IEMOCAP corpus \[7\]. Evaluating
this setup across several SNR conditions and test sets, we showed that
noise disparity leads models to learn spurious, noise-based cues rather
than task-relevant information. To address this, we proposed and
compared three cross-augmentation strategies, finding that augmenting
each sample with noise from every other class provides the strongest
robustness. We further showed that speech enhancement applied as a
preprocessing step does not resolve this issue, but instead introduces
its own noise- and enhancement-specific artifacts.
