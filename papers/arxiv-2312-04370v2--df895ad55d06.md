###### Abstract

Diffusion models are a new class of generative models that have shown
outstanding performance in image generation literature. As a
consequence, studies have attempted to apply diffusion models to other
tasks, such as speech enhancement. A popular approach in adapting
diffusion models to speech enhancement consists in modelling a
progressive transformation between the clean and noisy speech signals.
However, one popular diffusion model framework previously laid in image
generation literature did not account for such a transformation towards
the system input, which prevents from relating the existing
diffusion-based speech enhancement systems with the aforementioned
diffusion model framework. To address this, we extend this framework to
account for the progressive transformation between the clean and noisy
speech signals. This allows us to apply recent developments from image
generation literature, and to systematically investigate design aspects
of diffusion models that remain largely unexplored for speech
enhancement, such as the neural network preconditioning, the training
loss weighting, the stochastic differential equation (SDE) , or the
amount of stochasticity injected in the reverse process. We show that
the performance of previous diffusion-based speech enhancement systems
cannot be attributed to the progressive transformation between the clean
and noisy speech signals. Moreover, we show that a proper choice of
preconditioning, training loss weighting, SDE and sampler allows to
outperform a popular diffusion-based speech enhancement system while
using fewer sampling steps, thus reducing the computational cost by a
factor of four.

## I Introduction

The presence of noise and reverberation in an acoustic scene can
substantially reduce speech intelligibility, for both normal-hearing and
hearing-impaired listeners \[[1](#bib.bib1), [2](#bib.bib2)\]. It can
also reduce the performance of downstream tasks, such as automatic
speech recognition (ASR)  \[[3](#bib.bib3), [4](#bib.bib4)\] and speaker
identification (SID)  \[[5](#bib.bib5), [6](#bib.bib6)\]. Therefore,
speech enhancement techniques that aim to restore speech intelligibility
and quality by removing additive and convolutional distortions from
noise and reverberation are crucial for a wide range of applications,
such as telecommunications and hearing aid technology.

For the past years, the vast majority of studies on speech enhancement
have favored deep neural network-based techniques, due to their superior
performance over traditional statistical methods \[[7](#bib.bib7),
[8](#bib.bib8), [9](#bib.bib9)\]. Deep neural network-based systems can
be categorized into discriminative and generative models. Discriminative
models learn a mapping from the input noisy speech to the output clean
speech. They are trained in a supervised manner by presenting them with
noisy and clean speech signals in pairs. On the other hand, generative
models learn a probability distribution over clean speech. This
probability distribution can be conditioned on an input noisy speech
signal to perform speech enhancement. While discriminative models
account for the vast majority of neural network-based speech enhancement
systems \[[8](#bib.bib8)\], generative models represent an attractive
approach, as learning the inherent properties of speech should enable
robustness to arbitrary additive and convolutional distortions.
Moreover, discriminative models have shown to produce unpleasant speech
distortions that can be detrimental to downstream ASR
tasks \[[10](#bib.bib10), [11](#bib.bib11)\]. Generative approaches
include generative adversarial networks (GANs)  \[[12](#bib.bib12)\],
variational autoencoders (VAEs)  \[[13](#bib.bib13)\] and normalizing
flows (NFs)  \[[14](#bib.bib14)\], which have all been applied to speech
enhancement \[[15](#bib.bib15), [16](#bib.bib16), [17](#bib.bib17)\].

Diffusion models \[[18](#bib.bib18), [19](#bib.bib19),
[20](#bib.bib20)\] have recently gained significant interest for the
task of speech enhancement \[[21](#bib.bib21), [22](#bib.bib22),
[23](#bib.bib23), [24](#bib.bib24), [25](#bib.bib25), [26](#bib.bib26),
[27](#bib.bib27), [28](#bib.bib28), [29](#bib.bib29), [30](#bib.bib30),
[31](#bib.bib31), [32](#bib.bib32), [33](#bib.bib33), [34](#bib.bib34),
[35](#bib.bib35)\]. As a new class of generative models, they have met
great success in image generation \[[20](#bib.bib20), [36](#bib.bib36),
[37](#bib.bib37)\], audio generation \[[38](#bib.bib38),
[39](#bib.bib39), [40](#bib.bib40)\] and video
generation \[[41](#bib.bib41), [42](#bib.bib42)\]. They consist in
simulating a diffusion process by progressively adding Gaussian noise to
the training data until it can be approximated as pure Gaussian noise. A
neural network is then trained to undo this process and new samples can
be generated by starting from new random noise realizations.
In \[[21](#bib.bib21), [22](#bib.bib22)\], a discrete Markov chain
formulation inspired by \[[20](#bib.bib20)\] was adopted, where the mean
of the diffusion process was a linear interpolation between the clean
and noisy speech signals. Speech enhancement was then performed by
sampling from random noise centered around the noisy speech signal and
solving the reverse diffusion process.

In \[[23](#bib.bib23), [24](#bib.bib24)\], a continuous SDE formulation
inspired by \[[19](#bib.bib19)\] was proposed, where a novel drift term
allowed for a progressive transformation of the mean of the diffusion
process from the clean speech signal towards the noisy speech signal.
Compared to the discrete Markov chain formulation in \[[21](#bib.bib21),
[22](#bib.bib22)\], a continuous setting is more general and allows to
use an arbitrary numerical solver to integrate the reverse process. It
was also argued to allow for the derivation of an objective function
that does not explicitly involve the difference between the clean speech
signal and the noisy speech signal as opposed to \[[21](#bib.bib21),
[22](#bib.bib22)\], thus making the training task purely generative. The
introduced drift term was also argued to enable the system to
reconstruct speech corrupted by environmental noises that are different
from the stationary Gaussian noise used in the diffusion
process \[[24](#bib.bib24)\].

Another common approach to diffusion-based speech enhancement consists
in combining the diffusion model with a discriminative model that
predicts a first estimate of the clean speech signal \[[25](#bib.bib25),
[28](#bib.bib28), [29](#bib.bib29), [30](#bib.bib30),
[32](#bib.bib32)\]. The diffusion model is then tasked with removing
unwanted distortions introduced by the discriminative model using e.g.
denoising diffusion restoration models \[[43](#bib.bib43),
[29](#bib.bib29)\], stochastic regeneration \[[25](#bib.bib25)\] or
stochastic refinement \[[44](#bib.bib44), [30](#bib.bib30)\]. This
approach avoids typical generative artifacts such as vocalizing and
breathing effects, and considerably reduces the computational overhead
from the diffusion model, since the number of steps in the reverse
diffusion process can be reduced without losing
performance \[[25](#bib.bib25)\]. Finally, another promising approach
consists in using an unconditional diffusion model to solve inverse
problems \[[43](#bib.bib43), [45](#bib.bib45), [46](#bib.bib46)\]. This
was successfully applied to different audio applications such as music
restoration \[[47](#bib.bib47)\], speech
dereverberation \[[48](#bib.bib48)\] and source
separation \[[49](#bib.bib49)\]. This approach paves the way for a new
class of diffusion-based speech enhancement models that can be trained
in an unsupervised way.

Despite these recent advances, several aspects of diffusion-based speech
enhancement models remain poorly understood. In particular, the role of
the drift term that pulls the mean of the diffusion process towards the
noisy speech in \[[23](#bib.bib23), [24](#bib.bib24)\] is unclear. In
our view, the noisy speech signal can be seen as a conditioner for the
speech enhancement task in the same way as a text prompt in image
generation, the only difference being that in speech enhancement, the
conditioner has the same modality as the training data. Since such a
drift is not needed to obtain good results in image generation, it is
unclear why the success of diffusion-based speech enhancement should be
attributed to it. Moreover, it was shown by Karras et
al. \[[50](#bib.bib50)\] that multiple diffusion models that initially
differed in their formulation can be framed in a common framework,
allowing to better compare them. However, due to the nature of the drift
term used for speech enhancement in \[[23](#bib.bib23),
[24](#bib.bib24)\], the resulting diffusion model cannot be directly
included in the framework from \[[50](#bib.bib50)\]. As a consequence,
it is difficult to translate recent advances from image generation
literature to speech enhancement. Finally, while some design aspects of
diffusion-based speech enhancement were analyzed in subsequent
works \[[51](#bib.bib51), [52](#bib.bib52)\], many remain unexplored,
such as the neural network preconditioning \[[50](#bib.bib50)\], the
loss weighting, the SDE , or the amount of stochasticity injected when
integrating the reverse process.

Our contributions in this work are as follows:

- •
  We extend a diffusion model framework previously laid in image
  generation literature \[[50](#bib.bib50)\] to include a recently
  proposed diffusion-based speech enhancement system \[[23](#bib.bib23),
  [24](#bib.bib24)\] where the long-term mean of the diffusion process
  is non-zero and equal to the conditioner. Compared
  to \[[33](#bib.bib33)\] where we used a change of variable to consider
  the SDE satisfied by the environmental noise and fit in the framework
  laid in \[[50](#bib.bib50)\], we instead propose here a more general
  approach that models the clean speech signal and accounts for an
  eventual drift of the diffusion process.
- •
  Using this new framework, we show that the success of diffusion-based
  speech enhancement models cannot be attributed to the addition of a
  drift term that pulls the mean of the diffusion process towards the
  conditioner. This also proves that a mismatch between the final
  distribution of the forward process and the prior distribution used to
  initialize the reverse process is not necessarily responsible for a
  drop in performance as suggested in \[[52](#bib.bib52)\].
- •
  We systematically investigate the design space of diffusion models for
  speech enhancement by experimenting with different neural network
  preconditionings \[[50](#bib.bib50)\], loss weightings, SDEs , and
  amounts of stochasticity injected when integrating the reverse
  process.
- •
  This investigation results in a system that outperforms a popular
  diffusion-based baseline while using fewer sampling steps, thus
  allowing for a fourfold decrease in computational cost.

## II Related work

### II-A Diffusion models

A diffusion model progressively transforms clean training data
$`{\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\mskip-1.5mu\in\mskip-1.5mu\mathbb{R}^{d}}`$ with
distribution $`{p_{\mathrm{data}}}`$ into isotropic Gaussian noise. This
transformation can be modeled as a diffusion process
$`{\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\mskip-1.5mu\in\mskip-1.5mu\mathbb{R}^{d}}`$
indexed by a continuous variable
$`{t\mskip-1.5mu\in\mskip-1.5mu[0,\mskip-1.5mu1]}`$ satisfying the
following general SDE ,

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}=\boldsymbol{f}(t,\boldsymbol{x}_{t})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+g(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{t},
``` |  | (1) |

where $`{\boldsymbol{f}(t,\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}}`$ is
the drift coefficient, $`{g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}}`$ is the
diffusion coefficient and
$`{\boldsymbol{\omega}_{t}\mskip-1.5mu\in\mskip-1.5mu\mathbb{R}^{d}}`$
is a standard Wiener process. The goal of a diffusion model is to solve
the associated reverse-time SDE  \[[53](#bib.bib53)\], such that new
samples can be generated starting from random Gaussian noise
realizations at $`{t\!=\!1}`$. The reverse-time SDE associated with
Eq. ([1](#S2.E1 "In II-A Diffusion models ‣ II Related work ‣ Investigating the Design Space of Diffusion Models for Speech Enhancement"))
is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}=\left[\boldsymbol{f}(t,\boldsymbol{x}_{t})-g^{2}\mskip-0.75mu(t)\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}_{t}}\!\log p_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+g(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{t},
``` |  | (2) |

where $`{\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}_{t}}\!\log p_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})}`$
is termed the score function, which is intractable as it involves
marginalizing over the unknown distribution of the training data
$`{p_{\mathrm{data}}}`$,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
p_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p_{0t}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}|\boldsymbol{x}_{0})p_{\mathrm{data}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}_{0},
``` |  | (3) |

where the conditional probability density function (PDF) $`{p_{0t}}`$ is
commonly referred to as the perturbation kernel. Even though
$`{\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}_{t}}\!\log p_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})}`$ is
intractable, a neural network can be trained to approximate it via score
matching \[[54](#bib.bib54), [55](#bib.bib55), [56](#bib.bib56)\],

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\boldsymbol{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},t)\simeq\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}_{t}}\!\log p_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}),
``` |  | (4) |

where $`{\boldsymbol{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}`$ is the score model. New
samples can then be generated from random Gaussian noise realizations by
substituting
$`{\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}_{t}}\!\log p_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})}`$ with
$`{\boldsymbol{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},t)}`$ in
Eq. ([2](#S2.E2 "In II-A Diffusion models ‣ II Related work ‣ Investigating the Design Space of Diffusion Models for Speech Enhancement"))
and integrating
Eq. ([2](#S2.E2 "In II-A Diffusion models ‣ II Related work ‣ Investigating the Design Space of Diffusion Models for Speech Enhancement"))
using an arbitrary numerical solver (e.g. Euler-Maruyama or Runge-Kutta
methods). The number of sampling steps $`{n_{\mathrm{steps}}}`$ used to
discretize the time axis and integrate the reverse process controls the
number of neural network evaluations. Therefore,
$`{n_{\mathrm{steps}}}`$ defines a compromise between sample quality and
computational cost.

The drift coefficient is commonly chosen to have the form
$`{\boldsymbol{f}(t,\boldsymbol{x}_{t})\!=\!f(t)\mskip 1.5mu\boldsymbol{x}_{t}}`$,
such that the SDE in
Eq. ([1](#S2.E1 "In II-A Diffusion models ‣ II Related work ‣ Investigating the Design Space of Diffusion Models for Speech Enhancement"))
becomes

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}=f(t)\mskip 1.5mu\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+g(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{t}.
``` |  | (5) |

The perturbation kernel of this SDE turns out to be Gaussian and can be
calculated using Eqs. (5.50) and (5.51) in \[[57](#bib.bib57)\],

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
p_{0t}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}|\boldsymbol{x}_{0})=\mathcal{N}\big(\boldsymbol{x}_{t};s(t)\mskip 1.5mu\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\sigma^{2}\mskip-0.75mu(t)\mskip 1.5mu\mathbf{I}\big),
``` |  | (6) |

where

[TABLE]

and $`{\mathcal{N}(\,\cdot\,;\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})}`$
denotes a multivariate Gaussian PDF with mean
$`{\boldsymbol{\mu}\mskip-1.5mu\in\mskip-1.5mu\mathbb{R}^{d}}`$ and
covariance matrix
$`{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mskip-1.5mu\in\mskip-1.5mu\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}}`$.
This allows to directly sample $`{\boldsymbol{x}_{t}}`$ during training
for any $`{t}`$ without having to numerically solve the forward SDE in
Eq. ([5](#S2.E5 "In II-A Diffusion models ‣ II Related work ‣ Investigating the Design Space of Diffusion Models for Speech Enhancement")).
However, during inference, since $`{\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}`$ is unknown,
noise cannot be sampled around $`{s(t)\mskip 1.5mu\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}`$
to initialize the reverse process at $`{t\!=\!1}`$, unless
$`{s(1)\!=\!0}`$. In practice, it is common to use the zero-mean prior
distribution
$`{\mathcal{N}\big(\mskip 1.5mu\cdot\,;\mathbf{0},\sigma^{2}\mskip-0.75mu(1)\mskip 1.5mu\mathbf{I}\big)}`$,
even if this does not match $`{p_{01}}`$.

### II-B Common SDEs

The joint parametrization of $`{f(t)}`$, $`{g(t)}`$, $`{s(t)}`$ and
$`{\sigma(t)}`$ is commonly referred to as the noise schedule. One can
first fix $`{f(t)}`$ and $`{g(t)}`$ and derive the corresponding
$`{s(t)}`$ and $`{\sigma(t)}`$ from
Eq. ([7](#S2.E7 "In II-A Diffusion models ‣ II Related work ‣ Investigating the Design Space of Diffusion Models for Speech Enhancement")),
or alternatively one can first fix $`{s(t)}`$ and $`{\sigma(t)}`$ and
derive the corresponding $`{f(t)}`$ and $`{g(t)}`$ after reverting
Eq. ([7](#S2.E7 "In II-A Diffusion models ‣ II Related work ‣ Investigating the Design Space of Diffusion Models for Speech Enhancement")),

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
f(t)=\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}\Big[\log s(t)\Big]\quad\text{and}\quad g(t)=s(t)\sqrt{\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}\left[\frac{\sigma^{2}\mskip-0.75mu(t)}{s^{2}\mskip-0.75mu(t)}\right]}.
``` |  | (8) |

Recent studies \[[58](#bib.bib58), [59](#bib.bib59), [60](#bib.bib60),
[61](#bib.bib61)\] argued that the noise schedule should be defined in
terms of the log of the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of the diffusion
process $`{\lambda(t)}`$,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\lambda(t)=\log\frac{s^{2}\mskip-0.75mu(t)}{\sigma^{2}\mskip-0.75mu(t)}.
``` |  | (9) |

Common choices for the drift and diffusion coefficients can be
categorized into the variance exploding (VE) and variance preserving
(VP) assumptions \[[19](#bib.bib19)\].

#### II-B1 VE assumption

The VE assumption consists in setting $`{f(t)\!=\!0}`$, which leads to

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
s(t)=1\quad\text{and}\quad g(t)=\sqrt{\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}\Big[\sigma^{2}\mskip-0.75mu(t)\Big]}.
``` |  | (10) |

Song et al. \[[19](#bib.bib19)\] proposed the following noise schedule,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\sigma^{2}\mskip-0.75mu(t)=\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}^{2}\left[\left(\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{max}}}{\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}}\right)^{2t}-1\right],
``` |  | (11) |

where
$`{\sigma_{\mathrm{min}},\sigma_{\mathrm{max}}\mskip-1.5mu\in\mskip-1.5mu\mathbb{R}^{+},\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}\!<\!\sigma_{\mathrm{max}}}`$
are two hyperparameters. As a consequence,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
g(t)=\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}\left(\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{max}}}{\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}}\right)^{t}\sqrt{2\log\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{max}}}{\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}}}.
``` |  | (12) |

#### II-B2 VP assumption

The VP assumption consists in fixing
$`{s^{2}\mskip-0.75mu(t)\!+\!\sigma^{2}\mskip-0.75mu(t)\!=\!1}`$, which
leads to

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
f(t)=-\frac{1}{2}\beta(t)\quad\text{and}\quad g(t)=\sqrt{\beta(t)},
``` |  | (13) |

where $`{\beta:[0,\mskip-1.5mu1]\to\mathbb{R}^{+}}`$ is a
hyperparameter. Song et al. \[[19](#bib.bib19)\] proposed a linear noise
schedule of the following form,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\displaystyle\beta(t)=\beta_{\mathrm{min}}+t\,(\beta_{\mathrm{max}}-\beta_{\mathrm{min}}),
``` |  | (14) |

where
$`{\beta_{\mathrm{min}},\beta_{\mathrm{max}}\mskip-1.5mu\in\mskip-1.5mu\mathbb{R}^{+},\beta_{\mathrm{min}}\!<\!\beta_{\mathrm{max}}}`$.
The perturbation kernel can then be derived using

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
s(t)=e^{-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\beta(\xi)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi}\quad\text{and}\quad\sigma^{2}\mskip-0.75mu(t)=1-e^{-\int_{0}^{t}\beta(\xi)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi}.
``` |  | (15) |

Another common setting under the VP assumption is the family of cosine
schedule \[[62](#bib.bib62), [60](#bib.bib60)\],

|     |                                                         |     |      |
|-----|---------------------------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       \lambda(t)=-2\log\left(\tan\frac{\pi t}{2}\right)+2\nu,
       ```                                                      |     | (16) |

where $`{\nu}`$ is a parameter controlling the center of the log-SNR
distribution. The derivations of the perturbation kernel parameters
$`{s(t)}`$ and $`{\sigma(t)}`$ and the SDE coefficients $`{f(t)}`$ and
$`{g(t)}`$ from $`{\lambda(t)}`$ are detailed in
Appendix [A](#A1 "Appendix A Cosine schedule parameters ‣ Investigating the Design Space of Diffusion Models for Speech Enhancement").

### II-C Elucidating the Design Space of Diffusion-Based Generative Models (EDM)

Karras et al. \[[50](#bib.bib50)\] showed that previous diffusion model
formulations can be framed in a common framework denoted here as EDM.
More specifically, they showed that

- •
  The general SDE in
  Eq. ([5](#S2.E5 "In II-A Diffusion models ‣ II Related work ‣ Investigating the Design Space of Diffusion Models for Speech Enhancement"))
  is equivalent to a simpler, unscaled SDE satisfied by the unscaled
  process
  $`{\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}\!=\!\frac{\boldsymbol{x}_{t}}{s(t)}}`$,

  |  |  |  |  |
  |----|----|----|----|
  |  |
  ``` math
  \mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}=\frac{g(t)}{s(t)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{t}.
  ``` |  | (17) |

  The perturbation kernel of the unscaled process is

  |  |  |  |  |
  |----|----|----|----|
  |  |
  ``` math
  \bar{p}_{0t}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}|\boldsymbol{x}_{0})=\mathcal{N}\big(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t};\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\bar{\sigma}^{2}\mskip-0.75mu(t)\mskip 1.5mu\mathbf{I}\big),
  ``` |  | (18) |

  where

  |  |  |  |  |
  |----|----|----|----|
  |  |
  ``` math
  \bar{\sigma}^{2}\mskip-0.75mu(t)=\frac{\sigma^{2}\mskip-0.75mu(t)}{s^{2}\mskip-0.75mu(t)}=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{g^{2}\mskip-0.75mu(\xi)}{s^{2}\mskip-0.75mu(\xi)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi.
  ``` |  | (19) |

  Karras et al. \[[50](#bib.bib50)\] suggested using $`{s(t)\!=\!1}`$.
  This is intuitive since the network is conditioned on the state index
  $`{t}`$ and can thus learn to undo the deterministic scaling
  $`{s(t)}`$ before denoising. This is in line with studies defining the
  noise schedule in terms of the log-SNR
  $`{\lambda(t)}`$ \[[58](#bib.bib58), [59](#bib.bib59),
  [60](#bib.bib60), [61](#bib.bib61)\], as
  $`{\bar{\sigma}^{2}\mskip-0.75mu(t)\!=\!e^{-\lambda(t)}}`$. The
  perturbation kernel of the scaled process
  $`{\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\!=\!s(t)\mskip 1.5mu\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}}`$
  can be rewritten as

  |  |  |  |  |
  |----|----|----|----|
  |  |
  ``` math
  p_{0t}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}|\boldsymbol{x}_{0})=\mathcal{N}\big(\boldsymbol{x}_{t};s(t)\mskip 1.5mu\boldsymbol{x}_{0},s^{2}\mskip-0.75mu(t)\bar{\sigma}^{2}\mskip-0.75mu(t)\mskip 1.5mu\mathbf{I}\big).
  ``` |  | (20) |
- •
  The marginal distribution $`{p_{t}}`$ can be written as

  |  |  |  |  |
  |----|----|----|----|
  |  |
  ``` math
  p_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})=s(t)^{-d}\bar{p}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t},\bar{\sigma}(t)),
  ``` |  | (21) |

  where $`{\bar{p}}`$ is a mollified version of $`{p_{\mathrm{data}}}`$
  obtained by adding i.i.d. Gaussian noise to the samples,

  |  |  |  |  |
  |----|----|----|----|
  |  |
  ``` math
  \bar{p}(\cdot,\sigma)=p_{\mathrm{data}}\ast\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\sigma^{2}\mskip 1.5mu\mathbf{I}),
  ``` |  | (22) |

  where $`{\ast}`$ is the convolution operator between PDFs . As a
  consequence, the score function can be rewritten as

  |  |  |  |  |
  |----|----|----|----|
  |  |
  ``` math
  \nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}_{t}}\!\log p_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}_{t}}\!\log\bar{p}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t},\bar{\sigma}(t)),
  ``` |  | (23) |

  which means that it can be evaluated on the unscaled process
  $`{\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}}`$. Let $`{\boldsymbol{D}}`$ denote the
  optimal denoiser that minimizes a weighted $`{\mathcal{L}_{2}}`$-loss
  between the clean training data $`{\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}`$ and the
  output from the unscaled diffused data $`{\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}}`$,

  |  |  |  |  |
  |----|----|----|----|
  |  |
  ``` math
  \mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}\mathbb{E}_{t,\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}|\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}\Big[w(t)\left\|\boldsymbol{D}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t},t)-\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\right\|_{2}^{2}\Big],
  ``` |  | (24) |

  where $`{w:[0,\mskip-1.5mu1]\to\mathbb{R}^{+}}`$ is the loss weighting
  function. Karras et al. \[[50](#bib.bib50)\] showed that the score
  function can be expressed as a function of the optimal denoiser
  $`{\boldsymbol{D}}`$,

  |  |  |  |  |
  |----|----|----|----|
  |  |
  ``` math
  \nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}_{t}}\!\log\bar{p}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t},\bar{\sigma}(t))=\frac{\boldsymbol{D}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t},t)-\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}}{s(t)\bar{\sigma}^{2}\mskip-0.75mu(t)}.
  ``` |  | (25) |
- •
  The fitted denoiser $`{\boldsymbol{D}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}`$ trained
  to minimize
  Eq. ([24](#S2.E24 "In 2nd item ‣ II-C Elucidating the Design Space of Diffusion-Based Generative Models (EDM) ‣ II Related work ‣ Investigating the Design Space of Diffusion Models for Speech Enhancement"))
  can be expressed as a function of the raw neural network layers
  $`{\boldsymbol{F}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}`$. Such parametrization is
  referred to as preconditioning and has the form

  [TABLE]

  Karras et al. \[[50](#bib.bib50)\] suggested the following
  parametrization for $`{c_{\mathrm{skip}}}`$, $`{c_{\mathrm{out}}}`$,
  $`{c_{\mathrm{in}}}`$, $`{c_{\mathrm{noise}}}`$ and $`{w}`$ based on
  first principles (effective input unit variance, effective target unit
  variance and uniform effective loss weight),

  [TABLE]

### II-D Score-based Generative Model for Speech Enhancement (SGMSE)

Welker et al. \[[23](#bib.bib23)\] and Richter et
al. \[[24](#bib.bib24)\] adapted the diffusion model from Song et
al. \[[19](#bib.bib19)\] to perform speech enhancement by conditioning
the clean speech generation process on the noisy speech signal. They
considered complex-valued short-time Fourier transform (STFT)
representations of the signals flattened into $`{d\!=\!KT}`$-dimensional
vectors, where $`{K}`$ is the number of frequency bins and $`{T}`$ is
the number of frames. They trained the score model using pairs of clean
and noisy speech signals
$`{\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{y}\mskip-1.5mu\in\mskip-1.5mu\mathbb{C}^{d}}`$
in a supervised manner such that

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\boldsymbol{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{y},t)\simeq\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}_{t}}\!\log p_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}|\boldsymbol{y}).
``` |  | (28) |

They defined their diffusion equation as

[TABLE]

that is
$`{\boldsymbol{f}(t,\boldsymbol{x}_{t})\!=\!-\gamma(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\!-\!\boldsymbol{y})}`$
where $`{\gamma}`$ is a hyperparameter denoted as the stiffness, and
$`{g(t)}`$ is the same as the VE SDE from \[[19](#bib.bib19)\] and shown
in
Eq. ([12](#S2.E12 "In II-B1 VE assumption ‣ II-B Common SDEs ‣ II Related work ‣ Investigating the Design Space of Diffusion Models for Speech Enhancement")).
This SDE was inspired by the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) SDE
 \[[63](#bib.bib63)\] and results in an exponential decay of the mean of
the diffusion process from $`{\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}`$ towards
$`{\boldsymbol{y}}`$. We refer to this SDE as the OUVE SDE .

The drift coefficient
$`{\boldsymbol{f}(t,\boldsymbol{x}_{t})\!=\!-\gamma(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\!-\!\boldsymbol{y})}`$
in
Eq. ([29](#S2.E29 "In II-D Score-based Generative Model for Speech Enhancement (SGMSE) ‣ II Related work ‣ Investigating the Design Space of Diffusion Models for Speech Enhancement"))
cannot be written in the form $`{f(t)\mskip 1.5mu\boldsymbol{x}_{t}}`$,
which means that the perturbation kernel cannot be calculated using
Eqs. ([6](#S2.E6 "In II-A Diffusion models ‣ II Related work ‣ Investigating the Design Space of Diffusion Models for Speech Enhancement"))
and
([7](#S2.E7 "In II-A Diffusion models ‣ II Related work ‣ Investigating the Design Space of Diffusion Models for Speech Enhancement")).
Moreover, the model cannot be framed in the EDM framework, since we
cannot find a scaling factor $`{s(t)}`$ such that the mean of the
diffusion process is equal to $`{s(t)\mskip 1.5mu\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}`$.
As a consequence, there does not seem to be an immediate way of applying
the Heun-based sampler proposed in \[[50](#bib.bib50)\] to integrate the
reverse process. Nonetheless, the perturbation kernel is Gaussian and
can be calculated using Eqs. (5.50) and (5.51) in \[[57](#bib.bib57)\],

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
p_{0t}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}|\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{y})=\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}\big(\boldsymbol{x}_{t};\boldsymbol{\mu}(t),\sigma^{2}\mskip-0.75mu(t)\mskip 1.5mu\mathbf{I}\big),
``` |  | (30) |

where

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}(t)=e^{-\gamma t}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}-\boldsymbol{y})+\boldsymbol{y},
``` |  | (31) |
|  |
``` math
\displaystyle\sigma^{2}\mskip-0.75mu(t)=\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}^{2}}{1+\gamma/\log\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{max}}}{\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}}}\left[\left(\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{max}}}{\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}}\right)^{2t}-e^{-2\gamma t}\right],
``` |  | (32) |

and
$`{\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}(\,\cdot\,;\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})}`$
denotes a multivariate complex Gaussian PDF with mean
$`{\boldsymbol{\mu}\mskip-1.5mu\in\mskip-1.5mu\mathbb{C}^{d}}`$,
covariance matrix
$`{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mskip-1.5mu\in\mskip-1.5mu\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}}`$
and zero pseudo-covariance matrix. The training loss
in \[[23](#bib.bib23), [24](#bib.bib24)\] was²² 2
[https://github.com/sp-uhh/sgmse/blob/c76f7ad04ec477ce0741387ec10e903dd3fac3d9/sgmse/model.py#L115](https://github.com/sp-uhh/sgmse/blob/c76f7ad04ec477ce0741387ec10e903dd3fac3d9/sgmse/model.py#L115)

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{y}}\mathbb{E}_{t,\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{x}_{t}|(\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{y})}\Big[\left\|\sigma(t)\boldsymbol{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{y},t)+\boldsymbol{z}\right\|_{2}^{2}\Big],
``` |  | (33) |

$`{-\boldsymbol{F}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\big(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{y},\log t\big)}`$.
We believe this was unintended and we thus scale the output after the
output convolution in our implementation.

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\boldsymbol{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{y},t)=-\frac{1}{t}\boldsymbol{F}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\big(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{y},\log t\big).
``` |  | (34) |

To initialize the reverse process, noise was sampled around the noisy
speech signal $`{\boldsymbol{y}}`$ instead of $`{\boldsymbol{\mu}(1)}`$,
since the clean speech signal $`{\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}`$ is unknown during
inference. There was thus a prior mismatch similar to
Sect. [II-A](#S2.SS1 "II-A Diffusion models ‣ II Related work ‣ Investigating the Design Space of Diffusion Models for Speech Enhancement").
This was argued to be responsible for a drop in performance in speech
enhancement \[[52](#bib.bib52)\].

## III Proposal to extend EDM to include SGMSE

### III-A Non-zero long-term mean SDE

We propose to extend the EDM framework to account for a non-zero
long-term mean $`{\boldsymbol{y}}`$ of the diffusion process. Consider
the following general SDE ,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}=f(t)(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}-\boldsymbol{y})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+g(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{t}.
``` |  | (35) |

This SDE can be solved using the change of variable
$`{\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}\!=\!\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\!-\!\boldsymbol{y}}`$
to obtain its perturbation kernel,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
p_{0t}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}|\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{y})=\mathcal{N}\big(\boldsymbol{x}_{t};s(t)(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}-\boldsymbol{y})+\boldsymbol{y},s^{2}\mskip-0.75mu(t)\bar{\sigma}^{2}\mskip-0.75mu(t)\mskip 1.5mu\mathbf{I}\big),
``` |  | (36) |

where

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
s(t)=\exp\int_{0}^{t}f(\xi)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi\quad\text{and}\quad\bar{\sigma}^{2}\mskip-0.75mu(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{g^{2}\mskip-0.75mu(\xi)}{s^{2}\mskip-0.75mu(\xi)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi.
``` |  | (37) |

We obtain the same scaling factor $`{s(t)}`$ as in
Eq. ([7](#S2.E7 "In II-A Diffusion models ‣ II Related work ‣ Investigating the Design Space of Diffusion Models for Speech Enhancement")),
but applied to the shifted process
$`{\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}\!=\!\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\!-\!\boldsymbol{y}}`$.
For the OUVE SDE , it can be identified that

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\displaystyle s(t)=e^{-\gamma t},
``` |  | (38) |
|  |
``` math
\displaystyle\bar{\sigma}^{2}\mskip-0.75mu(t)=\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}^{2}}{1+\gamma/\log\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{max}}}{\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}}}\left[\left(e^{\gamma}\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{max}}}{\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}}\right)^{2t}-1\right].
``` |  | (39) |

Note that the OUVE SDE does not fall under the VE assumption since
$`{f(t)\neq 0}`$.

``` |  | (40) |

where
$`{\bar{\boldsymbol{n}}_{t}\!\sim\!\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}\big(0,\bar{\sigma}^{2}\mskip-0.75mu(t)\mskip 1.5mu\mathbf{I}\big)}`$
and

Compared to the EDM preconditioning in

### III-C Sampling

We cannot directly apply the Heun-based sampler
from \[[50](#bib.bib50)\] for our SDE in
Eq. ([35](#S3.E35 "In III-A Non-zero long-term mean SDE ‣ III Proposal to extend EDM to include SGMSE ‣ Investigating the Design Space of Diffusion Models for Speech Enhancement"))
because
Eq. ([23](#S2.E23 "In 2nd item ‣ II-C Elucidating the Design Space of Diffusion-Based Generative Models (EDM) ‣ II Related work ‣ Investigating the Design Space of Diffusion Models for Speech Enhancement"))
does not hold anymore. However, a similar property can be derived.
Intuitively, we would like to evaluate the score function on the
unshifted and unscaled process instead of the unscaled process. More
specifically, it can be shown (see
Appendix [C](#A3 "Appendix C Proof of Eq. () ‣ Investigating the Design Space of Diffusion Models for Speech Enhancement"))
that

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
p_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}|\boldsymbol{y})=s(t)^{-d}\Big[\tilde{p}_{\mathrm{data},\boldsymbol{y}}\ast\mathcal{N}\big(\mathbf{0},\bar{\sigma}^{2}\mskip-0.75mu(t)\mskip 1.5mu\mathbf{I}\big)\Big]\!\!\left(\frac{\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}}{s(t)}\right),
``` |  | (42) |

where
$`{\tilde{p}_{\mathrm{data},\boldsymbol{y}}:\boldsymbol{x}\mapsto p_{\mathrm{data}}(\boldsymbol{x}\!+\!\boldsymbol{y})}`$
is a shifted version of $`{p_{\mathrm{data}}}`$ and $`{\ast}`$ is the
convolution operator between PDFs . Let
$`\bar{\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}}_{t}\!=\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}/s(t)\!=\!(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}-\boldsymbol{y})/s(t)`$
denote the unshifted and unscaled process, and $`{\bar{\tilde{p}}}`$ the
distribution between the brackets in
Eq. ([42](#S3.E42 "In III-C Sampling ‣ III Proposal to extend EDM to include SGMSE ‣ Investigating the Design Space of Diffusion Models for Speech Enhancement")),

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\bar{\tilde{p}}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{y},\sigma)=\tilde{p}_{\mathrm{data},\boldsymbol{y}}\ast\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\sigma^{2}\mskip 1.5mu\mathbf{I}).
``` |  | (43) |

Therefore,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}_{t}}\!\log p_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}|\boldsymbol{y})=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}_{t}}\!\log\bar{\tilde{p}}(\bar{\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}}_{t},\boldsymbol{y},\bar{\sigma}(t)).
``` |  | (44) |

This equation is similar to
Eq. ([23](#S2.E23 "In 2nd item ‣ II-C Elucidating the Design Space of Diffusion-Based Generative Models (EDM) ‣ II Related work ‣ Investigating the Design Space of Diffusion Models for Speech Enhancement"))
except that the diffusion process is now both unshifted and unscaled. As
a consequence, the Heun-based sampler from \[[50](#bib.bib50)\] can be
applied by evaluating the score model on the unshifted and unscaled
process.

### III-D Consequences

#### III-D1 Alternative definition for the OUVE SDE and OUVP SDE

The OUVE SDE is defined in \[[23](#bib.bib23), [24](#bib.bib24)\] by
setting the diffusion coefficient $`{g(t)}`$ to that of the VE SDE
in \[[19](#bib.bib19)\]. However, as pointed out
in \[[50](#bib.bib50)\], defining $`{g(t)}`$ is of little use in
practice as opposed to the perturbation kernel parameters $`{s(t)}`$ and
$`{\sigma(t)}`$. As a matter of fact, the resulting $`{\sigma(t)}`$ is
different from that of the original VE SDE . From
Eq. ([38](#S3.E38 "In III-A Non-zero long-term mean SDE ‣ III Proposal to extend EDM to include SGMSE ‣ Investigating the Design Space of Diffusion Models for Speech Enhancement"))
it can be seen that the stiffness $`{\gamma}`$ is responsible for a
simple scaling of the unshifted diffusion process by a factor
$`{s(t)\!=\!e^{-\gamma t}}`$. With this in mind, a more practical
definition for the OUVE SDE in terms of the $`{s(t)}`$ and
$`{\bar{\sigma}(t)}`$ in
Eq. ([36](#S3.E36 "In III-A Non-zero long-term mean SDE ‣ III Proposal to extend EDM to include SGMSE ‣ Investigating the Design Space of Diffusion Models for Speech Enhancement"))
can be formulated as follows,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
s(t)=e^{-\gamma t}\quad\text{and}\quad\bar{\sigma}^{2}\mskip-0.75mu(t)=\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}^{2}\left[\left(\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{max}}}{\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}}\right)^{2t}-1\right].
``` |  | (45) |

That is, the variance of the unscaled process is the same as the VE SDE
. We refer to this SDE as OUVE₂. The resulting drift and diffusion
coefficients $`{f(t)}`$ and $`{g(t)}`$ in
Eq. ([35](#S3.E35 "In III-A Non-zero long-term mean SDE ‣ III Proposal to extend EDM to include SGMSE ‣ Investigating the Design Space of Diffusion Models for Speech Enhancement"))
are

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
f(t)=-\gamma,\quad g(t)=e^{-\gamma t}\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}\!\left(\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{max}}}{\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}}\right)^{t}\!\!\sqrt{2\log\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{max}}}{\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}}}.
``` |  | (46) |

Similarly, a new SDE termed the OUVP SDE which combines the OU SDE and
the VP SDE from \[[19](#bib.bib19)\] can be defined by applying the
additional scaling $`{s(t)\!=\!e^{-\gamma t}}`$ to the already existing
scaling from the VP SDE . Namely,

|  |  |  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle f(t)`$ | $`\displaystyle=-\gamma-\frac{1}{2}\beta(t),`$ | $`\displaystyle g(t)`$ | $`\displaystyle=e^{-\gamma t}\sqrt{\beta(t)},`$ |  | (47) |
|  | $`\displaystyle s(t)`$ | $`\displaystyle=e^{-\gamma t-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\beta(\xi)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi},`$ | $`\displaystyle\bar{\sigma}^{2}\mskip-0.75mu(t)`$ | $`\displaystyle=e^{\int_{0}^{t}\beta(\xi)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi}-1.`$ |  | (48) |

Note that similar to the OUVE SDE proposed in \[[23](#bib.bib23),
[24](#bib.bib24)\], the OUVE₂ and OUVP SDEs do not fall under the VE and
VP assumptions. In a recent work \[[64](#bib.bib64)\], an alternative VP
-based formulation for speech enhancement is proposed, where the mean of
the diffusion process is first interpolated between the clean speech
signal $`{\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}`$ and the noisy speech signal
$`{\boldsymbol{y}}`$ before scaling. However, the drift coefficient
cannot be written in the form
$`{f(t)(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\!-\!\boldsymbol{y})}`$ as in
Eq. ([35](#S3.E35 "In III-A Non-zero long-term mean SDE ‣ III Proposal to extend EDM to include SGMSE ‣ Investigating the Design Space of Diffusion Models for Speech Enhancement")),
which prevents from framing the model in our extended EDM framework.

#### III-D2 The role of the drift term

Previous studies on diffusion-based speech
enhancement \[[23](#bib.bib23), [24](#bib.bib24)\] have attributed their
success to the addition of the stiffness term $`{\gamma}`$ to provoke a
progressive drift of the mean of the diffusion process towards the noisy
speech $`{\boldsymbol{y}}`$. This was argued to enable the system to
reconstruct speech corrupted by environmental noises that differ from
the stationary Gaussian noise of the diffusion
process \[[24](#bib.bib24)\]. However, as seen in
Eq. ([38](#S3.E38 "In III-A Non-zero long-term mean SDE ‣ III Proposal to extend EDM to include SGMSE ‣ Investigating the Design Space of Diffusion Models for Speech Enhancement")),
the addition of the stiffness term $`{\gamma}`$ is only responsible for
a deterministic scaling of the unshifted diffusion process by a factor
$`{e^{-\gamma t}}`$. This means the need for $`{\gamma}`$ is in
contradiction with the suggested $`{s(t)\!=\!1}`$
in \[[50](#bib.bib50)\] and the intuition that the network can learn to
undo this scaling. In our view, the process should be seen as fully
generative with $`{\boldsymbol{y}}`$ only serving as a conditioner,
without the need for a progressive transformation between the
distributions of $`{\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}`$ and $`{\boldsymbol{y}}`$. The
noisy speech $`{\boldsymbol{y}}`$ should be treated similarly to a text
prompt in image generation, the only difference being that in speech
enhancement, the conditioner has the same modality as the output.

## IV Experimental setup

### IV-A Datasets

We consider two noisy speech datasets, namely VBDMD \[[65](#bib.bib65)\]
and our own MultiCorpus dataset. The speech enhancement systems are
evaluated in matched conditions using the training, validation and test
splits from the same dataset (no cross-dataset evaluation).

#### IV-A1 VBDMD

The VoiceBank+DEMAND (VBDMD) 28-speaker dataset \[[65](#bib.bib65)\] is
a publicly available and popular benchmark dataset for single-channel
speech enhancement. The noisy speech mixtures were generated by mixing
clean speech utterances from the Voice Bank corpus \[[66](#bib.bib66)\]
with 10 noise types from the DEMAND database \[[67](#bib.bib67)\] at
SNRs of
$`0\text{\,}\mathrm{dB}5\text{\,}\mathrm{dB}10\text{\,}\mathrm{dB}15\text{\,}\mathrm{dB}`$.
The test mixtures were obtained using two different speakers and five
different noise types at SNRs of
$`2.5\text{\,}\mathrm{dB}7.5\text{\,}\mathrm{dB}12.5\text{\,}\mathrm{dB}17.5\text{\,}\mathrm{dB}`$.
We remove speakers p226 and p287 from the training set to use them for
validation similarly to \[[21](#bib.bib21), [22](#bib.bib22),
[23](#bib.bib23), [24](#bib.bib24)\]. The duration of the training,
validation and test sets is
$`8\text{\,}\mathrm{h}`$ $`45\text{\,}\mathrm{min}`$,
$`37\text{\,}\mathrm{min}`$ and $`34\text{\,}\mathrm{min}`$
respectively.

#### IV-A2 MultiCorpus

We generate a diverse and acoustically adverse MultiCorpus dataset by
mixing reverberant speech and reverberant noise using speech utterances,
noise segments and binaural room impulse responses (BRIRs) from an
ensemble of five different speech corpora, five noise databases and five
BRIR databases. The selected databases are the same as
in \[[68](#bib.bib68)\]. Namely for the speech, we use
TIMIT \[[69](#bib.bib69)\], LibriSpeech (100-hour
version) \[[70](#bib.bib70)\], WSJ SI-84 \[[71](#bib.bib71)\],
Clarity \[[72](#bib.bib72)\] and VCTK \[[66](#bib.bib66)\]. For the
noise, we use TAU \[[73](#bib.bib73)\], NOISEX \[[74](#bib.bib74)\],
ICRA \[[75](#bib.bib75)\], DEMAND \[[67](#bib.bib67)\] and
ARTE \[[76](#bib.bib76)\]. Finally for the BRIRs , we use
Surrey \[[77](#bib.bib77)\], ASH \[[78](#bib.bib78)\],
BRAS \[[79](#bib.bib79)\], CATT \[[80](#bib.bib80)\] and
AVIL \[[81](#bib.bib81)\]. Each mixture is simulated by convolving one
clean speech utterance and up to three noise segments with BRIRs from
different and random spatial locations between
$`-90\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolDegree}`$ and
$`90\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolDegree}`$ in the same room. The
convolution outputs are then mixed at a random SNR uniformly distributed
between $`-5\text{\,}\mathrm{dB}`$ and $`10\text{\,}\mathrm{dB}`$. The
SNR is defined as the energy ratio between the direct-sound part of the
speech signal and the background signal, which consists of the
direct-sound and reverberant parts of the noise signals as well as the
reverberant part of the speech signal. The direct-sound part of the
speech signal includes early reflections up to a boundary of
$`50\text{\,}\mathrm{ms}`$, which was shown to be beneficial for speech
intelligibility \[[82](#bib.bib82)\]. The different signals are then
created by splitting the BRIRs into a direct-sound and a reverberant
component using a windowing procedure described in \[[83](#bib.bib83)\],
and averaged across left and right channels. For the speech,
$`80\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}`$ of the utterances in each corpus is reserved
for training and $`20\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}`$ for testing. For the noise,
$`80\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}`$ of each recording is reserved for training
and $`20\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}`$ for testing. For the BRIRs , every second
BRIR from each room is reserved for training and the rest for testing. A
validation set is created using the same split as the training set, but
with different random mixture realizations. The duration of the
training, validation and test sets is $`10\text{\,}\mathrm{h}`$,
$`30\text{\,}\mathrm{min}`$ and $`30\text{\,}\mathrm{min}`$
respectively.

### IV-B Data pre-processing

We use the same data representation as in \[[23](#bib.bib23),
[24](#bib.bib24)\]. The clean and noisy speech signals are sampled at
$`16\text{\,}\mathrm{kHz}`$. The STFT is calculated using a frame length
of 512 samples, a hop size of 128 samples and a Hann window. We discard
the Nyquist component to obtain $`{K\!=\!256}`$ frequency bins. An
amplitude transformation is then applied to each STFT coefficient
$`{c\mskip-1.5mu\in\mskip-1.5mu\mathbb{C}}`$ to compensate for their
heavy-tailed distribution,

|     |                                       |     |      |
|-----|---------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       \tilde{c}=A|c|^{\alpha}e^{i\angle c},
       ```                                    |     | (49) |

where $`{\tilde{c}}`$ is the transformed coefficient,
$`{A\mskip-1.5mu\in\mskip-1.5mu\mathbb{R}^{+}}`$ is a scaling factor and
$`{\alpha\mskip-1.5mu\in\mskip-1.5mu\mathbb{R}^{+}}`$ is a compression
factor. We use the same values for $`{A}`$ and $`{\alpha}`$ as
in \[[23](#bib.bib23), [24](#bib.bib24)\], namely $`{A\!=\!0.15}`$ and
$`{\alpha\!=\!0.5}`$.

### IV-C Neural network

We use the same NCSN++M neural network architecture as
in \[[51](#bib.bib51), [25](#bib.bib25)\]. This is a scaled-down version
of the NCSN++ neural network initially proposed by Song et
al. \[[19](#bib.bib19)\] and used in \[[23](#bib.bib23),
[24](#bib.bib24)\]. It consists of a multi-resolution
U-Net \[[84](#bib.bib84)\]-like architecture with an additional parallel
progressive growing path. Compared to \[[23](#bib.bib23),
[24](#bib.bib24)\], the number of layers in the encoder/decoder was
reduced to four, the number of residual blocks in each layer was reduced
to one, and all attention blocks were removed except in the bottleneck
layer. This reduces the number of parameters from
$`65\text{\,}\mathrm{M}`$ to $`27.8\text{\,}\mathrm{M}`$ without losing
performance \[[51](#bib.bib51), [25](#bib.bib25)\].

### IV-D Training

The neural network is trained for 300 epochs using the Adam
optimizer \[[85](#bib.bib85)\] and a learning rate of $`{1e^{-4}}`$. Due
to the varying duration of the training sequences, the complex-valued
spectrograms in $`{\mathbb{C}^{K\times T}}`$ with variable number of
frames $`{T}`$ are batched using a bucketing
strategy \[[86](#bib.bib86)\] with 10 buckets and a dynamic batch size
of $`32\text{\,}\mathrm{s}`$. Exponential moving average of the neural
network weights is applied with a decay rate of 0.999, as this was
reported to greatly improve results for image
generation \[[87](#bib.bib87), [19](#bib.bib19)\]. We randomly sample
$`{t\!\sim\!\mathcal{U}(t_{\epsilon},1)}`$ with
$`{t_{\epsilon}\!=\!0.01}`$ during training to avoid instabilities for
very small $`{t}`$ values similarly to previous
studies \[[19](#bib.bib19), [24](#bib.bib24), [64](#bib.bib64)\].

### IV-E Baselines

We use Conv-TasNet \[[88](#bib.bib88)\], DCCRN \[[89](#bib.bib89)\],
MANNER \[[90](#bib.bib90)\] and SGMSE+M \[[51](#bib.bib51)\] as
baselines. They are all trained on full-length mixtures for 100 epochs
using the Adam optimizer \[[85](#bib.bib85)\] and a bucketing
strategy \[[86](#bib.bib86)\] with 10 buckets and a dynamic batch size
of $`128\text{\,}\mathrm{s}`$, except SGMSE+M which is trained for 300
epochs with a dynamic batch size of $`32\text{\,}\mathrm{s}`$ as in
Sect. [IV-D](#S4.SS4 "IV-D Training ‣ IV Experimental setup ‣ Investigating the Design Space of Diffusion Models for Speech Enhancement").

#### IV-E1 Conv-TasNet

An end-to-end fully convolutional network designed for single-channel
multi-speaker speech separation. It can be applied to speech enhancement
as described in \[[91](#bib.bib91), [92](#bib.bib92)\] by setting the
number of separated sources to $`{K\!=\!1}`$. We use a learning rate of
$`{1e^{-3}}`$ and the SNR loss instead of the scale-invariant
signal-to-noise ratio (SI-SNR)  \[[93](#bib.bib93)\] loss to avoid
scaling the output signal. This was shown to provide superior
performance in terms of multiple perceptual
metrics \[[91](#bib.bib91)\]. It has $`4.9\text{\,}\mathrm{M}`$
parameters.

#### IV-E2 DCCRN

A causal U-Net \[[84](#bib.bib84)\]-based network operating in the STFT
domain. It leverages complex-valued convolutions \[[94](#bib.bib94)\] in
the encoder/decoder and a long short-term memory
network \[[95](#bib.bib95)\] in the bottleneck layer to predict a
complex-valued mask. We train it with the SNR loss instead of the SI-SNR
loss similar to Conv-TasNet. We set the total number of encoder channels
to
$`{[32,\mskip-1.5mu64,\mskip-1.5mu128,\mskip-1.5mu256,\mskip-1.5mu256,\mskip-1.5mu256]}`$
and use the “E” enhancement strategy. We use regular batch
normalization \[[96](#bib.bib96)\] along the real and imaginary axes
separately instead of complex batch normalization \[[94](#bib.bib94)\]
as this improves training time and stability without reducing
performance. This is in line with recent evidence that the performance
of DCCRN cannot be attributed to complex-valued
operations \[[97](#bib.bib97)\]. We use a learning rate of $`{1e^{-4}}`$
and gradient clipping with a maximum norm of 5 to further improve
stability. It has $`3.7\text{\,}\mathrm{M}`$ parameters.

#### IV-E3 MANNER

A multi-view attention network combining channel
attention \[[98](#bib.bib98)\] with local and global attention along two
signal scales similar to dual-path models \[[99](#bib.bib99)\]. It is
trained with a combination of a time-domain $`{\mathcal{L}_{1}}`$-loss
and a multi-resolution STFT loss \[[100](#bib.bib100)\] as
in \[[101](#bib.bib101)\]. We use the “small” version provided
in \[[90](#bib.bib90)\], which presents multi-attention blocks in the
last encoder/decoder layers only, as this greatly reduces computational
cost without significantly reducing performance \[[90](#bib.bib90)\]. We
use the OneCycleLR scheduler \[[102](#bib.bib102)\] with a minimum and
maximum learning rate of $`{1e^{-5}}`$ and $`{1e^{-2}}`$ respectively as
in \[[90](#bib.bib90)\]. It has $`21.2\text{\,}\mathrm{M}`$ parameters.

#### IV-E4 SGMSE+M

A diffusion-based system with the same neural network backbone as
described in
Sect. [IV-C](#S4.SS3 "IV-C Neural network ‣ IV Experimental setup ‣ Investigating the Design Space of Diffusion Models for Speech Enhancement"),
but using the original preconditioning, OUVE SDE and predictor-corrector
(PC) sampler as in \[[23](#bib.bib23), [24](#bib.bib24)\]. The
preconditioning corresponds to
Eq. ([41](#S3.E41 "In III-B Training loss and preconditioning ‣ III Proposal to extend EDM to include SGMSE ‣ Investigating the Design Space of Diffusion Models for Speech Enhancement"))
and the SDE corresponds to
Eq. ([29](#S2.E29 "In II-D Score-based Generative Model for Speech Enhancement (SGMSE) ‣ II Related work ‣ Investigating the Design Space of Diffusion Models for Speech Enhancement")).
We use $`{\gamma\!=\!1.5}`$ and one step of annealed Langevin dynamics
correction with size $`{r\!=\!0.5}`$ as suggested
in \[[24](#bib.bib24)\]. The data pre-processing and neural network
training match
Sect. [IV-B](#S4.SS2 "IV-B Data pre-processing ‣ IV Experimental setup ‣ Investigating the Design Space of Diffusion Models for Speech Enhancement")
and
Sect. [IV-D](#S4.SS4 "IV-D Training ‣ IV Experimental setup ‣ Investigating the Design Space of Diffusion Models for Speech Enhancement")
respectively. By comparing with this baseline, we are able to assess the
influence of the preconditioning, the SDE and the solver. It has
$`27.8\text{\,}\mathrm{M}`$ parameters.

### IV-F Objective metrics

We evaluate the performance of the systems in terms of perceptual
evaluation of speech quality (PESQ)  \[[103](#bib.bib103)\], extended
short-term objective intelligibility (ESTOI)  \[[104](#bib.bib104)\] and
SNR . The results are reported in terms of average metric improvement
between the unprocessed input mixture and the enhanced output signal.
The improvements are denoted as
$`{\Delta\text{\lx@glossaries@gls@link{acronym}{pesq}{{{}}PESQ} }}`$,
$`{\Delta\text{\lx@glossaries@gls@link{acronym}{estoi}{{{}}ESTOI} }}`$
and $`{\Delta\text{\lx@glossaries@gls@link{acronym}{snr}{{{}}SNR} }}`$
respectively. Note that for the MultiCorpus dataset, the reference
signal of each metric is defined as the direct-sound component of the
speech signal averaged across left and right channels (see
Sect. [IV-A2](#S4.SS1.SSS2 "IV-A2 MultiCorpus ‣ IV-A Datasets ‣ IV Experimental setup ‣ Investigating the Design Space of Diffusion Models for Speech Enhancement")
for details).

### IV-G Experiments

We investigate three design aspects of diffusion-based speech
enhancement in light of the extended EDM framework proposed in
Sect. [III](#S3 "III Proposal to extend EDM to include SGMSE ‣ Investigating the Design Space of Diffusion Models for Speech Enhancement"):
the preconditioning and loss weighting, the SDE , and the amount of
stochasticity injected when integrating the reverse process. Note that
these design aspects do not affect the computational complexity of the
system, since the neural network architecture and the training procedure
are the same. For each experiment, the reverse diffusion process is
integrated by uniformly discretizing the time axis between $`{t\!=\!1}`$
and $`{t\!=\!0}`$. The number of discretization steps
$`{n_{\mathrm{steps}}}`$ is varied in powers of 2 between 1 and 64. To
initialize the reverse process, we sample from
$`{\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}\big(\mskip 1.5mu\cdot\,;\boldsymbol{y},\sigma^{2}\mskip-0.75mu(1)\mskip 1.5mu\mathbf{I}\big)}`$
as in \[[23](#bib.bib23), [24](#bib.bib24)\].

#### IV-G1 Preconditioning and loss weighting

Eq. ([41](#S3.E41 "In III-B Training loss and preconditioning ‣ III Proposal to extend EDM to include SGMSE ‣ Investigating the Design Space of Diffusion Models for Speech Enhancement"))
shows the preconditioning and loss weighting corresponding
to \[[23](#bib.bib23), [24](#bib.bib24)\]. However, this preconditioning
is different from
Eq. ([27](#S2.E27 "In 3rd item ‣ II-C Elucidating the Design Space of Diffusion-Based Generative Models (EDM) ‣ II Related work ‣ Investigating the Design Space of Diffusion Models for Speech Enhancement")),
which was derived from first principles and suggested
in \[[50](#bib.bib50)\]. We thus investigate the effect of each
preconditioning parameter $`{c_{\mathrm{skip}}}`$,
$`{c_{\mathrm{out}}}`$, $`{c_{\mathrm{in}}}`$, $`{c_{\mathrm{noise}}}`$
and $`{c_{\mathrm{shift}}}`$ as well as the loss weighing $`{w}`$. To do
so, we incrementally change the preconditioning parameters and loss
weighting from the values used in \[[23](#bib.bib23), [24](#bib.bib24)\]
and shown in
Eq. ([41](#S3.E41 "In III-B Training loss and preconditioning ‣ III Proposal to extend EDM to include SGMSE ‣ Investigating the Design Space of Diffusion Models for Speech Enhancement"))
to the values suggested in \[[50](#bib.bib50)\] and shown in
Eq. ([27](#S2.E27 "In 3rd item ‣ II-C Elucidating the Design Space of Diffusion-Based Generative Models (EDM) ‣ II Related work ‣ Investigating the Design Space of Diffusion Models for Speech Enhancement")).
For $`{c_{\mathrm{shift}}}`$, since this parameter was introduced by us
in
Sect. [III-B](#S3.SS2 "III-B Training loss and preconditioning ‣ III Proposal to extend EDM to include SGMSE ‣ Investigating the Design Space of Diffusion Models for Speech Enhancement")
to include SGMSE in EDM, there is no recommended value
in \[[50](#bib.bib50)\]. Since we see no theoretical foundation to set
$`{c_{\mathrm{shift}}}`$ to $`{\boldsymbol{y}}`$ as
in \[[23](#bib.bib23), [24](#bib.bib24)\] and
Eq. ([41](#S3.E41 "In III-B Training loss and preconditioning ‣ III Proposal to extend EDM to include SGMSE ‣ Investigating the Design Space of Diffusion Models for Speech Enhancement")),
we set $`{c_{\mathrm{shift}}}`$ to zero when investigating its effect.
For the parameter $`{\sigma_{\mathrm{data}}}`$, the value used
in \[[50](#bib.bib50)\] was 0.5, as this corresponds to the variance of
pixel values in popular image datasets when centered around
$`{[-1,\mskip-1.5mu1]}`$. However in our case, the variance of complex
STFT coefficients after applying the transform in
Eq. ([49](#S4.E49 "In IV-B Data pre-processing ‣ IV Experimental setup ‣ Investigating the Design Space of Diffusion Models for Speech Enhancement"))
is around 0.1. We thus set $`{\sigma_{\mathrm{data}}\!=\!0.1}`$. We use
the same OUVE SDE and PC sampler as in \[[23](#bib.bib23),
[24](#bib.bib24)\].

principles. We then experiment with the following SDEs :

- •
  OUVE: Proposed in \[[23](#bib.bib23), [24](#bib.bib24)\] and shown in
  Eq. ([29](#S2.E29 "In II-D Score-based Generative Model for Speech Enhancement (SGMSE) ‣ II Related work ‣ Investigating the Design Space of Diffusion Models for Speech Enhancement")).
  It allows for a progressive drift of the mean of the diffusion process
  towards the noisy speech $`{\boldsymbol{y}}`$. We use
  $`{\sigma_{\min}\!=\!0.05}`$, $`{\sigma_{\max}\!=\!0.5}`$ and
  $`{\gamma\!=\!1.5}`$ as in \[[23](#bib.bib23), [24](#bib.bib24)\].
- •
  OUVE₂: Alternative definition for the OUVE SDE proposed in
  Sect. [III-D1](#S3.SS4.SSS1 "III-D1 Alternative definition for the OUVE SDE and OUVP SDE ‣ III-D Consequences ‣ III Proposal to extend EDM to include SGMSE ‣ Investigating the Design Space of Diffusion Models for Speech Enhancement"),
  which results in the same unscaled variance
  $`{\bar{\sigma}^{2}\mskip-0.75mu(t)}`$ as the VE SDE
  from \[[19](#bib.bib19)\]. We set $`{\sigma_{\min}\!=\!0.04}`$,
  $`{\sigma_{\max}\!=\!1.7}`$ and $`{\gamma\!=\!1.5}`$ empirically based
  on preliminary experiments.
- •
  VE: Original VE SDE from image generation
  literature \[[19](#bib.bib19)\] and described in
  Sect. [II-B](#S2.SS2 "II-B Common SDEs ‣ II Related work ‣ Investigating the Design Space of Diffusion Models for Speech Enhancement").
  Similar to OUVE₂ but sets $`{s(t)\!=\!1}`$ (i.e. $`{\gamma\!=\!0}`$),
  meaning no drift of the mean of the diffusion process occurs. To the
  best of our knowledge, this was not used for speech enhancement
  before. We set $`{\sigma_{\min}\!=\!0.04}`$ and
  $`{\sigma_{\max}\!=\!1.7}`$ as for OUVE₂.
- •
  OUVP: Combination of the OU and VP SDEs proposed in
  Sect. [III-D1](#S3.SS4.SSS1 "III-D1 Alternative definition for the OUVE SDE and OUVP SDE ‣ III-D Consequences ‣ III Proposal to extend EDM to include SGMSE ‣ Investigating the Design Space of Diffusion Models for Speech Enhancement").
  We set $`{\beta_{\min}\!=\!0.01}`$, $`{\beta_{\max}\!=\!1}`$ and
  $`{\gamma\!=\!1.5}`$ empirically based on preliminary experiments.
- •
  VP: Original VP SDE from image generation
  literature \[[19](#bib.bib19)\] and described in
  Sect. [II-B](#S2.SS2 "II-B Common SDEs ‣ II Related work ‣ Investigating the Design Space of Diffusion Models for Speech Enhancement").
  Similar to OUVP but without the additional drift of the mean of the
  diffusion process (i.e. $`{\gamma\!=\!0}`$). To the best of our
  knowledge, this was not used for speech enhancement before. We set
  $`{\beta_{\min}\!=\!0.01}`$, $`{\beta_{\max}\!=\!1}`$ and
  $`{\gamma\!=\!1.5}`$ as for OUVP.
- •
  Cosine: Shifted-cosine noise schedule from image
  generation \[[60](#bib.bib60)\] and shown in
  Eq. ([16](#S2.E16 "In II-B2 VP assumption ‣ II-B Common SDEs ‣ II Related work ‣ Investigating the Design Space of Diffusion Models for Speech Enhancement")).
  To the best of our knowledge, this was not used for speech enhancement
  before, except in our previous study \[[33](#bib.bib33)\]. We clamp
  $`{\lambda(t)\!\geq\!\lambda_{\min}}`$ and
  $`{\beta(t)\!\leq\!\beta_{\max}}`$ to prevent instabilities around
  $`{t\!=\!1}`$. We set $`{\nu\!=\!1.5}`$, $`{\lambda_{\min}\!=\!-12}`$
  and $`{\beta_{\max}\!=\!10}`$ empirically based on preliminary
  experiments.
- •
  BBED: Brownian Bridge with Exponential Diffusion Coefficient (BBED)
  proposed in \[[52](#bib.bib52)\]. It replaces the exponential drift
  from the OUVE SDE with a linear interpolation between the clean speech
  $`{\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}`$ and the noisy speech $`{\boldsymbol{y}}`$.
  This reduces the mismatch between the mean of the diffusion process at
  $`{t\!=\!1}`$ and the noisy speech $`{y}`$ around which noise is
  sampled to initialize the reverse process. This was argued to improve
  speech enhancement performance compared to the OUVE SDE
   \[[52](#bib.bib52)\]. We set $`{c\!=\!0.01}`$ and $`{k\!=\!10}`$ as
  in \[[52](#bib.bib52)\] and use the change of variable
  $`{t\leftarrow t_{\max}\mskip-1.5mu\cdot\mskip-1.5mut}`$ with
  $`{t_{\max}\!=\!0.999}`$ to prevent instabilities around
  $`{t\!=\!1}`$.

The hyper-parameters for each SDE significantly affect the performance.
For each SDE , either preliminary experiments are conducted to find the
best hyper-parameters, or the values suggested in previous studies are
used. The preliminary experiments are not detailed here for brevity.

The different SDEs are illustrated in the one-dimensional case in
Fig. [2](#S4.F2 "Fig. 2 ‣ IV-G2 SDE ‣ IV-G Experiments ‣ IV Experimental setup ‣ Investigating the Design Space of Diffusion Models for Speech Enhancement"),
where the different drifts between the clean speech
$`{\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}`$ and the noisy speech $`{\boldsymbol{y}}`$ can
be observed. It can be seen that the VE SDE shows no drift towards the
noisy speech $`{\boldsymbol{y}}`$ and the perturbation kernel stays
centered around the clean speech $`{\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}`$. The VP SDE
shows a very slight drift compared to the OUVP SDE . The cosine and BBED
SDEs show a very small mismatch between the mean of the diffusion
process at $`{t\!=\!1}`$ and the noisy speech $`{\boldsymbol{y}}`$. Note
that the reverse process is still initialized by sampling from a
Gaussian centered around $`{\boldsymbol{y}}`$, even when using the VE
and VP SDEs . This means that for the VE and VP SDEs , the prior
mismatch is substantially larger compared to the other SDEs .

Figure [1](#S4.F1 "Fig. 1 ‣ IV-G2 SDE ‣ IV-G Experiments ‣ IV Experimental setup ‣ Investigating the Design Space of Diffusion Models for Speech Enhancement")
shows an example of the evolution of the diffusion process for each SDE
, in the case of speech enhancement where the long-term mean of the
diffusion process is the noisy speech $`{\boldsymbol{y}}`$, and in the
case of image generation where the long-term mean of the diffusion
process is zero. In the image generation case, the OUVE₂ and VE SDEs are
equivalent, since no drift towards the conditioner occurs. This is
modeled by setting $`{\boldsymbol{y}\!=\!\mathbf{0}}`$ in our framework.
However, in the speech enhancement case, the last state of the OUVE₂
process is the noisy speech $`{\boldsymbol{y}}`$ with a small amount of
Gaussian noise added, while the VE process is the clean speech
$`{\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}`$ with a larger amount of Gaussian noise added.
Similar observations can be made for the OUVP and VP SDEs . Finally,
while the cosine and BBED perturbation kernels are both centered around
the noisy speech $`{\boldsymbol{y}}`$ at $`{t\!=\!1}`$, the variance is
very high for the cosine SDE , while it is very small for the BBED SDE .

#### IV-G3 Stochasticity injected in reverse process

We fix the preconditioning parameters and the loss weighting as in the
previous experiment. We also chose the cosine SDE , since it was shown
to provide superior performance in image generation compared to linear
noise schedules \[[62](#bib.bib62), [60](#bib.bib60),
[61](#bib.bib61)\]. To integrate the reverse process, we use either the
PC sampler from Song et al. \[[19](#bib.bib19)\], which has been
extensively used for speech enhancement \[[23](#bib.bib23),
[24](#bib.bib24), [51](#bib.bib51), [52](#bib.bib52),
[25](#bib.bib25)\], or the 2^(nd) order Heun-based sampler from the EDM
framework \[[50](#bib.bib50)\]. Pseudo-code for both samplers is
provided in
Alg. [1](#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ IV-G3 Stochasticity injected in reverse process ‣ IV-G Experiments ‣ IV Experimental setup ‣ Investigating the Design Space of Diffusion Models for Speech Enhancement")
and
Alg. [2](#alg2 "Algorithm 2 ‣ IV-G3 Stochasticity injected in reverse process ‣ IV-G Experiments ‣ IV Experimental setup ‣ Investigating the Design Space of Diffusion Models for Speech Enhancement").
In the case of the PC sampler, the parameter controlling the amount of
stochasticity injected in the reverse process is the step size of the
annealed Langevin dynamics correction $`{r}`$. In the case of the
Heun-based sampler, this is controlled by the parameter
$`{S_{\mathrm{churn}}}`$. We vary
$`{r\mskip-1.5mu\in\mskip-1.5mu[0,\mskip-1.5mu1]}`$ when using the PC
sampler and
$`{S_{\mathrm{churn}}\mskip-1.5mu\in\mskip-1.5mu[0,\mskip-1.5mu20]\mskip-1.5mu\cup\mskip-1.5mu\{\infty\}}`$
when using the Heun-based sampler. Note that the Heun-based sampler
algorithm clamps the amount of noise injected in the reverse process
such that it does not exceed the amount of noise already present in the
current diffusion state. The clamping threshold increases with the
number of sampling steps $`{n_{\mathrm{steps}}}`$. For the largest
number of sampling steps $`{n_{\mathrm{steps}}\!=\!64}`$ considered in
this study, the threshold is $`{S_{\mathrm{churn}}\!=\!26.5}`$, which is
why we do not investigate $`{S_{\mathrm{churn}}}`$ values between 20 and
$`{\infty}`$. The remaining parameters for the Heun-based sampler are
empirically set to $`{S_{\mathrm{noise}}\!=\!1}`$, $`{S_{\min}\!=\!0}`$
and $`{S_{\max}\!=\!\infty}`$ based on preliminary experiments. To the
best of our knowledge, the Heun-based sampler has not been used for
speech enhancement before, except in our previous
study \[[33](#bib.bib33)\] where $`{S_{\mathrm{churn}}}`$ was fixed to
$`{\infty}`$. For the PC sampler, we use a single corrector step as
suggested in \[[24](#bib.bib24)\]. This also ensures that the number of
neural network evaluations is the same with both samplers for a fixed
$`{n_{\mathrm{steps}}}`$. Note that while the effect of the step size
$`{r}`$ on speech enhancement performance was investigated
in \[[24](#bib.bib24)\], this was done with a fixed
$`{n_{\mathrm{steps}}}`$. It is thus unclear if the optimal step size
$`{r}`$ is the same for different $`{n_{\mathrm{steps}}}`$ values.
Moreover, this was investigated in terms of PESQ and SI-SNR , but not in
terms of ESTOI .

### V-B SDE

Fig. 4: Speech enhancement performance as a function of the number of
sampling steps $`{n_{\mathrm{steps}}}`$ for different SDEs .

Figure [4](#S5.F4 "Fig. 4 ‣ V-B SDE ‣ V Results ‣ Investigating the Design Space of Diffusion Models for Speech Enhancement")
shows the results of the SDE experiment. It can be seen that the OUVE
and OUVE₂ SDEs show very similar performance. This means that while we
argue the OUVE₂ formulation is more practical, it does not lead to a
performance improvement. More interestingly, the VE SDE shows similar
performance to OUVE and OUVE₂ for $`{n_{\mathrm{steps}}\!\geq\!8}`$.
Since the VE SDE presents no drift of the diffusion process from the
clean speech towards the noisy speech, this result suggests that the
performance of diffusion-based speech enhancement cannot be attributed
to such a drift as suggested in \[[23](#bib.bib23), [24](#bib.bib24)\].
Moreover, since the VE SDE imposes a substantially larger prior mismatch
compared to OUVE and OUVE₂, this result also suggests that a prior
mismatch is not necessarily responsible for a performance drop as
suggested in \[[52](#bib.bib52)\]. Similar conclusions can be drawn when
comparing the VP and OUVP SDEs : they show similar performance at large
$`{n_{\mathrm{steps}}}`$, and the VP SDE shows substantially higher
performance at small $`{n_{\mathrm{steps}}}`$. This means that in this
particular example, the drift of the diffusion process from the clean
speech towards the noisy speech actually has a detrimental effect on
performance, despite the reduced prior mismatch. The BBED SDE shows
similar performance to the OUVE and OUVE₂ SDEs despite the minimized
prior mismatch. Finally, the cosine SDE shows significantly higher
$`{\Delta\text{\lx@glossaries@gls@link{acronym}{pesq}{{{}}PESQ} }}`$
scores on VBDMD.

stochasticity reduces as the model improves, models trained with diverse
datasets continue to benefit from stochasticity. Therefore, assuming
these observations are valid for speech enhancement, our results suggest
that the model can be further improved, or that the datasets are diverse
enough.

## VI Conclusion

In the present work, we extended the EDM framework proposed
in \[[50](#bib.bib50)\] to include a recent diffusion-based speech
enhancement model \[[23](#bib.bib23), [24](#bib.bib24)\]. Specifically,
we considered a more general SDE with a non-zero long-term mean, we
added a new parameter $`{c_{\mathrm{shift}}}`$ to the neural network
preconditioning, and we showed that the score function can be evaluated
on the unshifted and unscaled process instead of the unscaled process.
This allowed to shed light on the different design aspects of diffusion
models in the case of speech enhancement, and to apply recent
developments from image generation literature that were not investigated
for speech enhancement before.

By experimenting with SDEs that were not used for speech enhancement
before, we were able to show that similar performance can be obtained
with SDEs that present a drift from the clean speech towards the noisy
speech compared to SDEs that do not present such a drift. This suggests
that the model does not require such a drift to reconstruct speech
corrupted by environmental noises that differ from the stationary
Gaussian noise of the diffusion process, contrary to previous
beliefs \[[24](#bib.bib24)\]. This similar performance was observed
despite the increased mismatch between the final distribution of the
forward diffusion process and the prior distribution used to initialize
the reverse process, even though this was argued to be responsible for a
drop in performance \[[52](#bib.bib52)\]. Omitting the drift term makes
the speech enhancement task fully generative, since this models a
progressive transformation from Gaussian noise to clean speech, rather
than from noisy speech to clean speech. Moreover, we hypothesize that
this improves training efficiency, since the network does not need to
learn to undo the scaling $`{s(t)}`$ before denoising.

The usage of the Heun-based sampler in conjunction with the neural
network preconditioning from \[[50](#bib.bib50)\] led to a substantial
improvement of speech enhancement performance when using few sampling
steps. More specifically, the Heun-based sampler required as few as 4
sampling steps to perform similarly to the PC sampler with 16 sampling
steps, thus allowing for a fourfold decrease in computational cost. The
Heun-based sampler was also shown to be beneficial for different SDEs .

Despite the encouraging results, diffusion-based speech enhancement is
still computationally more costly compared to state-of-the-art
discriminative systems due to the multiple neural network evaluations in
the reverse process. This raises the question of whether diffusion-based
speech enhancement is worth the additional computational cost. While
further reducing the computational cost is an obvious direction for
future work, other options include systematically investigating the
performance in mismatched conditions, or exploring the benefit of an
unsupervised training procedure for speech enhancement. Indeed, the
present work only investigated the performance in matched conditions,
and while our previous study \[[33](#bib.bib33)\] reported results in
mismatched conditions using multiple speech, noise and BRIR databases,
this was not done in conjunction with the different design aspects of
the diffusion model. It is thus unclear if some specific SDEs or the
stochasticity in the reverse process are beneficial to generalizing to
unseen acoustic conditions. Moreover, while the proposed diffusion-based
approach is generative in that it learns a conditional probability
distribution over clean speech, the models were trained in a supervised
setting using pairs of clean and noisy speech signals. It would be
interesting to investigate if an unsupervised training procedure
improves the robustness to arbitrary additive and convolutional
distortions.
````
