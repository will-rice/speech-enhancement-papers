# Do We Need EMA for Diffusion-Based Speech Enhancement? Toward a Magnitude-Preserving Network Architecture

Julius Richter    Danilo de Oliveira    Timo Gerkmann

###### Abstract

We study diffusion-based speech enhancement using a Schrödinger bridge
formulation and extend the EDM2 framework to this setting. We employ
time-dependent preconditioning of network inputs and outputs to
stabilize training and explore two skip-connection configurations that
allow the network to predict either environmental noise or clean speech.
To control activation and weight magnitudes, we adopt a
magnitude-preserving architecture and learn the contribution of the
noisy input within each network block for improved conditioning. We
further analyze the impact of exponential moving average (EMA) parameter
smoothing by approximating different EMA profiles post training, finding
that, unlike in image generation, short or absent EMA consistently
yields better speech enhancement performance. Experiments on
VoiceBank-DEMAND and EARS-WHAM demonstrate competitive
signal-to-distortion ratios and perceptual scores, with the two
skip-connection variants exhibiting complementary strengths. These
findings provide new insights into EMA behavior, magnitude preservation,
and skip-connection design for diffusion-based speech enhancement.

###### Index Terms: 

Speech enhancement, diffusion models, Schrödinger bridge, exponential
moving average, EMA

## 1 Introduction

Diffusion-based generative models have emerged as powerful tools for
speech enhancement, learning clean speech posteriors conditioned on
noisy inputs \[[1](#bib.bib3)\]. They excel at mitigating a wide range
of communication artifacts, including background noise, reverberation,
bandwidth limitation, codec artifacts, and packet
loss \[[2](#bib.bib14)\], while offering flexibility for integration
with model-based approaches \[[3](#bib.bib12)\].

Diffusion models, or score-based generative models, operate by
progressively removing noise through a learned reverse
process \[[4](#bib.bib21), [5](#bib.bib11)\]. Various stochastic
processes have been explored for speech enhancement, including the
Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process \[[1](#bib.bib3)\], the Brownian
bridge \[[6](#bib.bib13)\], and the [SB](#id15)
([SB](#id15)) \[[7](#bib.bib8)\]. In all cases, we train a neural
network to separate clean speech from environmental and Gaussian noise
introduced during the forward process. Typical architectural choices
include a U-Net structure \[[8](#bib.bib22)\], optionally augmented with
self-attention layers \[[9](#bib.bib23)\].

Recent work has highlighted the importance of preconditioning to
stabilize training and prevent large gradients \[[10](#bib.bib1),
[11](#bib.bib2)\], yet such techniques have not been fully explored for
diffusion bridges \[[12](#bib.bib7), [13](#bib.bib25)\], particularly
those applied to speech \[[7](#bib.bib8), [14](#bib.bib24)\]. Similarly,
uncontrolled magnitude growth in network activations and weights can
degrade performance \[[15](#bib.bib4)\], motivating the use of
magnitude-preserving architectures \[[14](#bib.bib24), [16](#bib.bib26),
[17](#bib.bib29)\]. Another underexplored aspect is the role of
[EMA](#id14) ([EMA](#id14)) parameter smoothing. While long [EMA](#id14)
horizons improve mode coverage in image generation, their benefit for
speech enhancement remains unclear.

To address these gaps, we extend prior work on diffusion training
dynamics to speech enhancement and systematically investigate the
effects of magnitude preservation, skip-connection design, and EMA
behavior.

Our main contributions are as follows:

- •
  Extending the EDM2 framework to SE. We build on the improved training
  dynamics of [EDM2](#id17) ([EDM2](#id17)) \[[15](#bib.bib4)\] by
  adopting a magnitude-preserving architecture with time-dependent
  input/output preconditioning for stable training of Schrödinger bridge
  diffusion models.
- •
  Skip-connection design. We introduce and compare two skip-connection
  configurations enabling the neural network to predict either
  environmental noise or clean speech, revealing complementary
  advantages across SI-SDR and perceptual quality metrics.
- •
  EMA ablation. We systematically approximate and evaluate different EMA
  profiles post-training and show, for the first time, that shorter or
  even absent EMA horizons outperform longer ones in diffusion-based
  speech enhancement.
- •
  Comprehensive evaluation. We demonstrate competitive results on
  VoiceBank-DEMAND and EARS-WHAM and provide code, audio examples, and
  pretrained checkpoints for reproducibility¹¹ 1
  [https://github.com/sp-uhh/edm2se](https://github.com/sp-uhh/edm2se).

Our experiments on VoiceBank-DEMAND \[[18](#bib.bib19)\] and
EARS-WHAM \[[19](#bib.bib10)\] show that shorter or even absent EMA
lengths consistently outperform long EMA horizons, and that the two
skip-connection designs trade off between higher SDR and better
perceptual scores. Together, these findings provide new insights into
EMA behavior, skip-connection design, and stable training dynamics for
diffusion-based speech enhancement. By exploring the [MP-ADM](#id19)
([MP-ADM](#id19)) architecture \[[15](#bib.bib4)\] in the EDM2
framework, we provide an alternative to the commonly employed NCSN++
architecture \[[5](#bib.bib11)\] in generative SE.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Schrödinger Bridge for Speech Enhancement

The Schrödinger bridge (SB), first introduced in quantum
mechanics \[[20](#bib.bib16)\] and later connected to optimal
transport \[[21](#bib.bib27)\], formulates a stochastic optimal control
problem interpolating between two distributions. It is commonly
formulated as

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\min_{\mathbb{Q}\in\Pi(p_{x},p_{y})}D_{\text{KL}}\!\bigl(\mathbb{Q}\,\|\,\mathbb{P}\bigr),
``` |  | (1) |

where $`\mathbb{Q}`$ and $`\mathbb{P}`$ denote controlled and
uncontrolled path measures, respectively, and $`\Pi(p_{x},p_{y})`$ is
the set of path measures whose boundary marginals are given by the
distributions $`p_{x}`$ and $`p_{y}`$. This yields the control
formulation

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\begin{gathered}\min_{\mathbf{u}}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\int_{0}^{1}\!\!\tfrac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)\|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right]\\
\text{s.t.}\quad\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{t}=[\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)+\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)]\mathrm{d}t+g(t)\mathrm{d}\mathbf{w}_{t},\\
\mathbf{x}_{0}\sim p_{x},\quad\mathbf{x}_{1}\sim p_{y}\end{gathered}
``` |  | (2) |

where $`\{\mathbf{x}_{t}\}`$ is a stochastic process governed by the
[SDE](#id5) ([SDE](#id5)) with drift $`\mathbf{f}`$, diffusion
coefficient $`g`$, and $`\mathbf{w}_{t}`$ a standard Wiener process. The
control function
$`\mathbf{u}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}`$ specifies the
control input at each state and time. Applying the Cole-Hopf transform
to the necessary conditions of
([2](#S2.E2 "In 2.1 Schrödinger Bridge for Speech Enhancement ‣ 2 Background ‣ Do We Need EMA for Diffusion-Based Speech Enhancement? Toward a Magnitude-Preserving Network Architecture"))
leads to a system of coupled PDEs \[[12](#bib.bib7)\],

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\begin{gathered}\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\Psi_{t}=-\nabla\Psi_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{f}-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Tr}(g^{2}\nabla^{2}\Psi_{t}),\\
\partial_{t}\widehat{\Psi}_{t}=-\nabla\!\cdot\!(\widehat{\Psi}_{t}\mathbf{f})+\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Tr}(g^{2}\nabla^{2}\widehat{\Psi}_{t}),\end{cases}\\
\text{s.t.}\quad\Psi_{0}\widehat{\Psi}_{0}=p_{x},\quad\Psi_{1}\widehat{\Psi}_{1}=p_{y},\end{gathered}
``` |  | (3) |

where $`\Psi_{t}`$ and $`\widehat{\Psi}_{t}`$ are called time-varying
(Schrödinger) potentials that are linked through Nelson’s identity
$`\Psi_{t}\widehat{\Psi}_{t}=p_{t}`$ \[[22](#bib.bib17)\]. Then, the
optimal control is
$`\mathbf{u}^{*}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)=g(t)^{2}\nabla\log\Psi_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})`$,
yielding the forward and reverse [SDE](#id5):

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{t}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\!\left[\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)+g^{2}(t)\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\log\Psi_{t}\right]\!\mathrm{d}t+g(t)\mathrm{d}\mathbf{w}_{t},`$ |  | (4) |
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{t}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\!\left[\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)-g^{2}(t)\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\log\widehat{\Psi}_{t}\right]\!\mathrm{d}t+g(t)\mathrm{d}\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{t}.`$ |  | (5) |

For Gaussian boundary marginals, closed-form solutions
exist \[[23](#bib.bib9)\]. Assuming a drift $`\mathbf{f}=0`$, the SB
marginal conditioned on $`(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{y})`$ is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
p_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{y})=\mathcal{N}\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{t};\bm{\mu}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{y}),\sigma_{t}^{2}\mathbf{I}\right),
``` |  | (6) |

with mean and variance

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\bm{\mu}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{y})`$ | $`\displaystyle=w_{x}(t)\mathbf{x}_{0}+w_{y}(t)\mathbf{y},`$ |  | (7) |
|  | $`\displaystyle\sigma_{t}^{2}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{\overset{\rightarrow}{\phantom{.}\sigma_{t}}\!\phantom{}^{2}\overset{\leftarrow}{\phantom{.}\sigma_{t}}\!\phantom{}^{2}}{\overset{\rightarrow}{\phantom{.}\sigma_{t}}\!\phantom{}^{2}+\overset{\leftarrow}{\phantom{.}\sigma_{t}}\!\phantom{}^{2}}`$ |  | (8) |

where
$`\overset{\rightarrow}{\phantom{.}\sigma_{t}}\!\phantom{}^{2}\!=\!\int_{0}^{t}g^{2}(\tau)\mathrm{d}\tau`$
and
$`\overset{\leftarrow}{\phantom{.}\sigma_{t}}\!\phantom{}^{2}\!=\!\int_{t}^{1}g^{2}(\tau)\mathrm{d}\tau`$
are variances accumulated from either side, and
$`w_{x}(t)=\overset{\leftarrow}{\phantom{.}\sigma_{t}}\!\phantom{}^{2}/(\overset{\rightarrow}{\phantom{.}\sigma_{t}}\!\phantom{}^{2}+\overset{\leftarrow}{\phantom{.}\sigma_{t}}\!\phantom{}^{2})`$
and
$`w_{y}(t)=\overset{\rightarrow}{\phantom{.}\sigma_{t}}\!\phantom{}^{2}/(\overset{\rightarrow}{\phantom{.}\sigma_{t}}\!\phantom{}^{2}+\overset{\leftarrow}{\phantom{.}\sigma_{t}}\!\phantom{}^{2})`$ \[[13](#bib.bib25)\].

Following previous work \[[1](#bib.bib3), [7](#bib.bib8),
[24](#bib.bib15)\], we define the diffusion process in the [STFT](#id3)
([STFT](#id3)) domain, where $`\mathbf{x}_{0}`$ and $`\mathbf{y}`$
denote the clean and noisy speech, respectively. The real and imaginary
components are treated as separate channels, and the time–frequency
coefficients are flattened into vectors in $`\mathbb{R}^{d}`$, where
$`d`$ denotes the number of time–frequency bins. To train an SB model, a
denoiser $`D_{\theta}`$ is optimized using a data prediction
loss \[[7](#bib.bib8)\]:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\hskip-2.00749pt\mathcal{J}_{\text{SB}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|D_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{y},t)-\mathbf{x}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}+\alpha\|\underline{\hat{\mathbf{x}}}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{y},t)-\underline{\mathbf{x}}_{0}\|_{1}\right]
``` |  | (9) |

where the time-domain signals
$`\underline{\hat{\mathbf{x}}}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{y},t)=\operatorname{iSTFT}(D_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{y},t))`$
and
$`\underline{\mathbf{x}}_{0}\!=\!\operatorname{iSTFT}(\mathbf{x}_{0})`$
are obtained via the [iSTFT](#id4) ([iSTFT](#id4)), and $`\alpha`$
weights the time-domain loss term. At inference, the reverse SDE
in ([5](#S2.E5 "In 2.1 Schrödinger Bridge for Speech Enhancement ‣ 2 Background ‣ Do We Need EMA for Diffusion-Based Speech Enhancement? Toward a Magnitude-Preserving Network Architecture"))
is solved with an ODE or SDE sampler; we use the ODE sampler
following \[[7](#bib.bib8)\].

### 2.2 Magnitude-Preserving Learned Layers

Diffusion models can suffer from uncontrolled growth in activations and
weights \[[15](#bib.bib4)\]. Magnitude-preserving layers address this by
normalizing weights and activations to keep their variance
constant \[[15](#bib.bib4), [25](#bib.bib30)\]. For a linear or
convolutional layer, each channel-specific weight vector $`\mathbf{v}`$
is normalized:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\tilde{\mathbf{v}}=\frac{\mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{v}\|_{2}+\epsilon},
``` |  | (10) |

with a small constant $`\epsilon`$ added for numerical stability to
avoid division by zero. After each gradient step, the weight vector is
rescaled such that its norm satisfies
$`\|\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|_{2}=\sqrt{k}`$, where $`k`$ is the
dimensionality of $`\tilde{\mathbf{v}}`$. This preserves gradient flow
and stabilizes training \[[15](#bib.bib4)\].

### 2.3 Post-training Exponential Moving Average (EMA)

[EMA](#id14) maintains a running average of the parameters over the
training iterations according to

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\hat{\theta}_{\beta}^{(i)}=\beta\hat{\theta}_{\beta}^{(i-1)}+(1-\beta)\theta^{(i)}\,,
``` |  | (11) |

where $`i`$ denotes the current training step and $`\beta`$ specifies
the momentum of the running average, typically set close to 1. Karras et
al. \[[15](#bib.bib4)\] propose a power-law [EMA](#id14) with
step-dependent $`\beta_{\gamma}^{(i)}`$ defined as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\hat{\theta}_{\gamma}^{(i)}=\beta_{\gamma}^{(i)}\hat{\theta}_{\gamma}^{(i-1)}+(1-\beta_{\gamma}^{(i)})\theta^{(i)},\quad\beta_{\gamma}^{(i)}=(1-1/i)^{\gamma+1}\,,
``` |  | (12) |

where the constant $`\gamma`$ controls the sharpness of the profile. The
[EMA](#id14) profile is characterized by its relative standard deviation
$`\sigma_{\text{rel}}`$, representing the peak width relative to
training duration.

They also introduced a post-training approximation method to reconstruct
arbitrary EMA profiles from stored snapshots, enabling dense evaluation
of EMA length. While longer EMA horizons are known to improve diversity
in image generation, their impact on speech enhancement remains largely
unexplored.

## 3 Method

We call our diffusion-based speech enhancement framework [EDM2SE](#id16)
([EDM2SE](#id16)), which is based on the official implementation²² 2
[https://github.com/NVlabs/edm2](https://github.com/NVlabs/edm2) of
[EDM2](#id17) \[[15](#bib.bib4)\]. We formulate diffusion-based speech
enhancement as an SB process
(Sec. [2.1](#S2.SS1 "2.1 Schrödinger Bridge for Speech Enhancement ‣ 2 Background ‣ Do We Need EMA for Diffusion-Based Speech Enhancement? Toward a Magnitude-Preserving Network Architecture"))
in the STFT domain with amplitude compression as in \[[1](#bib.bib3)\],
treating the real and imaginary parts as separate channels and
flattening time-frequency coefficients into vectors in
$`\mathbb{R}^{d}`$.

Let $`\mathbf{x}_{0}`$ denote the clean speech signal and $`\mathbf{y}`$
the corresponding noisy speech mixture, modeled as

|     |                                       |     |      |
|-----|---------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       \mathbf{y}=\mathbf{x}_{0}+\mathbf{n},
       ```                                    |     | (13) |

where $`\mathbf{n}`$ represents the additive environmental noise. We
assume that the clean speech $`\mathbf{x}_{0}`$ and the environmental
noise $`\mathbf{n}`$ are statistically independent, with variances
$`\operatorname{Var}(\mathbf{x}_{0})=\sigma_{x}^{2}`$ and
$`\operatorname{Var}(\mathbf{n})=\sigma_{n}^{2}`$, which we compute from
the training data. Moreover, we consider the [SB](#id15) conditional
marginal distribution
in ([6](#S2.E6 "In 2.1 Schrödinger Bridge for Speech Enhancement ‣ 2 Background ‣ Do We Need EMA for Diffusion-Based Speech Enhancement? Toward a Magnitude-Preserving Network Architecture"))
with mean $`\bm{\mu}_{t}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})`$ and variance
$`\sigma_{{}_{t}}^{2}`$.

### 3.1 Preconditioning

Preconditioning rescales the network inputs and outputs at each process
time $`t`$ to stabilize optimization \[[10](#bib.bib1)\]. Given

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbf{x}_{t}=\bm{\mu}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{y})+\sigma_{t}\mathbf{z},\qquad\mathbf{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}),
``` |  | (14) |

we train a denoiser $`D_{\theta}`$ with the time-weighted objective

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{J}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{t,\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}}\!\left[\lambda(t)\bigl\|D_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{y},t)-\mathbf{x}_{0}\bigr\|_{2}^{2}\right],
``` |  | (15) |

where $`t\sim\mathcal{U}(t_{\text{eps}},1)`$ and $`\lambda(t)`$ is a
time-dependent weight. We parameterize $`D_{\theta}`$ as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
D_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{y},t)=c_{\text{s}}\mathbf{x}_{t}+c_{\text{out}}(t)F_{\theta}(c_{\text{in}}(t)\mathbf{x}_{t},c_{\text{in}}(1)\mathbf{y},t),
``` |  | (16) |

where $`F_{\theta}`$ is the neural network, $`c_{\text{in}}`$ and
$`c_{\text{out}}`$ are time-dependent input/output scalings, and
$`c_{\text{s}}`$ is a constant defining the skip connection applied to
$`\mathbf{x}_{t}`$. The conditioner uses $`c_{\text{in}}(1)`$,
corresponding to the normalization that yields unit variance for
$`\mathbf{x}_{t}`$ at $`t=1`$.

Substituting
([16](#S3.E16 "In 3.1 Preconditioning ‣ 3 Method ‣ Do We Need EMA for Diffusion-Based Speech Enhancement? Toward a Magnitude-Preserving Network Architecture"))
into
([15](#S3.E15 "In 3.1 Preconditioning ‣ 3 Method ‣ Do We Need EMA for Diffusion-Based Speech Enhancement? Toward a Magnitude-Preserving Network Architecture"))
yields

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{J}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\,\underbrace{\lambda(t)\,c_{\text{out}}(t)^{2}}_{w(t)}\,\bigl\|F_{\theta}(\cdot)-F_{\text{target}}(\cdot)\bigr\|_{2}^{2}\Bigr],
``` |  | (17) |

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
F_{\text{target}}(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{x}_{t},t)=(\mathbf{x}_{0}-c_{\text{s}}\,\mathbf{x}_{t})/c_{\text{out}}(t),
``` |  | (18) |

where $`w(t)`$ is the effective weight and $`F_{\theta}`$ learns a
normalized target.

Input Scaling:  We require unit-variance inputs to $`F_{\theta}`$, such
that

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}\!\left(c_{\text{in}}(t)\mathbf{x}_{t}\right)\overset{!}{=}1\;\;\Leftrightarrow\;\;c_{\text{in}}(t)^{2}\,\operatorname{Var}\!\left(\bm{\mu}_{t}+\sigma(t)\mathbf{z}\right)=1.`$ |  | (19) |

Using
([13](#S3.E13 "In 3 Method ‣ Do We Need EMA for Diffusion-Based Speech Enhancement? Toward a Magnitude-Preserving Network Architecture")),
we have
$`\bm{\mu}_{t}=(w_{x}(t)+w_{y}(t))\mathbf{x}_{0}+w_{y}(t)\mathbf{n}`$;
hence

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
c_{\text{in}}(t)=1/\sqrt{\,\bigl(w_{x}(t)+w_{y}(t)\bigr)^{2}\sigma_{x}^{2}+w_{y}(t)^{2}\sigma_{n}^{2}+\sigma_{t}^{2}}\,.
``` |  | (20) |

Output Scaling:  We require
$`\operatorname{Var}\!\left(F_{\text{target}}(\cdot,\cdot,t)\right)\overset{!}{=}1`$.
Thus, we get

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle c_{\text{out}}(t)^{2}`$ | $`\displaystyle=(1-c_{\text{s}}(w_{x}(t)+w_{y}(t)))^{2}\sigma_{x}^{2}+c_{\text{s}}^{2}w_{y}(t)^{2}\sigma_{n}^{2}+c_{\text{s}}^{2}\sigma_{t}^{2}.`$ |  | (21) |

Skip Scaling:  We explore $`c_{\text{s}}\!\in\!\{0,1\}`$. For
$`c_{\text{s}}\!=\!1`$ (noise prediction),

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
c_{\text{out},n}(t)=\sqrt{\,\bigl(1-w_{x}(t)-w_{y}(t)\bigr)^{2}\sigma_{x}^{2}+w_{y}(t)^{2}\sigma_{n}^{2}+\sigma_{t}^{2}\,}.
``` |  | (22) |

For $`c_{\text{s}}=0`$ (clean speech prediction), we get

|     |                                     |     |      |
|-----|-------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       c_{\text{out},x}(t)\;=\;\sigma_{x}.
       ```                                  |     | (23) |

Loss Weighting:  We set $`w(t)=1`$, i.e.,
$`\lambda(t)=1/c_{\text{out}}(t)^{2}`$. For noise prediction and clean
speech prediction, respectively, we get

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\lambda_{n}(t)`$ | $`\displaystyle=1/((1-w_{x}(t)-w_{y}(t))^{2}\sigma_{x}^{2}+w_{y}(t)^{2}\sigma_{n}^{2}+\sigma_{t}^{2}),`$ |  | (24) |
|  | $`\displaystyle\lambda_{x}(t)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\sigma_{x}^{-2}.`$ |  | (25) |

### 3.2 Network Architecture

We adopt the [MP-ADM](#id19) architecture \[[15](#bib.bib4)\] with
magnitude-preserving learned layers
(Sec. [2.2](#S2.SS2 "2.2 Magnitude-Preserving Learned Layers ‣ 2 Background ‣ Do We Need EMA for Diffusion-Based Speech Enhancement? Toward a Magnitude-Preserving Network Architecture");
see also
Fig. [1](#S3.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 3.2 Network Architecture ‣ 3 Method ‣ Do We Need EMA for Diffusion-Based Speech Enhancement? Toward a Magnitude-Preserving Network Architecture")).
To inject conditioning, we add the noisy speech (or its downsampled
version at the corresponding resolution) to the feature maps immediately
after merging the residual branch in each block, using a
magnitude-preserving fusion:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\operatorname{MP\text{-}Add}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},\tau)=\frac{(1-\tau)\,\mathbf{a}+\tau\,\mathbf{b}}{\sqrt{(1-\tau)^{2}+\tau^{2}}},
``` |  | (26) |

where $`\tau\in[0,1]`$ is a learned interpolation coefficient
(implemented via a sigmoid). This preserves feature magnitudes while
allowing the model to learn how strongly to condition on $`\mathbf{y}`$
at each block.

Figure 1: Encoder and decoder blocks of the magnitude-preserving ADM
architecture \[[15](#bib.bib4)\]. We modify the block-wise fusion of the
conditioner via a learnable MP-Add operation, see
([26](#S3.E26 "In 3.2 Network Architecture ‣ 3 Method ‣ Do We Need EMA for Diffusion-Based Speech Enhancement? Toward a Magnitude-Preserving Network Architecture")).

## 4 Experimental Setup

Following \[[1](#bib.bib3)\], we use the same amplitude-compressed
[STFT](#id3) representation as input, given by
$`0.15\,|x|^{0.5}e^{i\angle(x)}`$ for each coefficient
$`x\!\in\!\mathbb{C}`$. The forward and reverse SDEs in
([4](#S2.E4 "In 2.1 Schrödinger Bridge for Speech Enhancement ‣ 2 Background ‣ Do We Need EMA for Diffusion-Based Speech Enhancement? Toward a Magnitude-Preserving Network Architecture"))
and
([5](#S2.E5 "In 2.1 Schrödinger Bridge for Speech Enhancement ‣ 2 Background ‣ Do We Need EMA for Diffusion-Based Speech Enhancement? Toward a Magnitude-Preserving Network Architecture"))
are parameterized with $`\mathbf{f}\!=\!0`$ and
$`g(t)\!=\!\sqrt{c}k^{t}`$ where $`c\!=\!0.4`$ and $`k\!=\!2.6`$. For
numerical stability, we set $`t_{\text{eps}}\!=\!0.02`$, which
corresponds to the step size of our uniform discretization scheme with
50 sampling steps.

All experiments are trained using two graphics processing units (NVIDIA
RTX A6000) with a batch size of 16 until convergence. Model checkpoints
are saved once every 1,024k training samples (equivalently, once every
64k training steps with a batch size of 16). We employ an inverse square
root learning rate decay schedule \[[26](#bib.bib28)\], starting from an
initial rate of $`2.5\!\times\!10^{-3}`$ that decreases after processing
$`3\!\times\!10^{4}`$ training samples. This explicit decay
follows \[[15](#bib.bib4)\], which shows that in weight-normalized
networks the implicit learning-rate decay caused by growing weight
magnitudes is removed, making it necessary to schedule the learning rate
directly over the course of training. Employing such a schedule improves
stability and enables the optimizer to converge more reliably.

We train a total of eight models covering all combinations of three
factors. First, models are trained on either the
VoiceBank-DEMAND \[[18](#bib.bib19)\] or the EARS-WHAM
(v2) \[[19](#bib.bib10)\]. Second, the skip connection coefficient is
set to either $`c_{\text{s}}\!=\!1`$ or $`c_{\text{s}}\!=\!0`$. Third,
the auxiliary loss weighting parameter is set to either $`\alpha\!=\!0`$
or $`\alpha\!=\!0.001`$. This full factorial design enables us to
systematically evaluate the impact of each component on performance.

For preconditioning, we estimate the signal variances $`\sigma_{x}^{2}`$
and $`\sigma_{n}^{2}`$ as mean values from the amplitude-compressed
training spectrograms. For VoiceBank-DEMAND, we obtain
$`\sigma_{x}^{2}\!=\!0.402`$ and $`\sigma_{n}^{2}\!=\!0.342`$; for
EARS-WHAM, $`\sigma_{x}^{2}\!=\!0.368`$ and
$`\sigma_{n}^{2}\!=\!0.353`$. These values are fixed before training and
kept constant at inference.

To assess the speech enhancement performance, we report a set of metrics
commonly used in the field. We compute the [SI-SDR](#id12)
([SI-SDR](#id12)) \[[27](#bib.bib6)\] to quantify the separation
performance in the time domain. Perceptual quality is evaluated using
the [PESQ](#id8) ([PESQ](#id8)) score \[[28](#bib.bib5)\]. We include
the non-intrusive speech quality assessment models
NISQA \[[29](#bib.bib31)\] and DNSMOS \[[30](#bib.bib18)\] to capture
perceptual aspects not directly reflected in reference-based metrics.

## 5 Results

We first study the effect of the [EMA](#id14) length using the model
with $`c_{\text{s}}\!=\!1`$ and $`\alpha\!=\!0.001`$, trained on
VoiceBank-DEMAND \[[18](#bib.bib19)\].
Fig. [2](#S4.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 4 Experimental Setup ‣ Do We Need EMA for Diffusion-Based Speech Enhancement? Toward a Magnitude-Preserving Network Architecture")
shows the validation set performance versus EMA length. Shorter
[EMA](#id14) lengths consistently outperform longer ones across all
metrics. Performance then plateaus before dropping sharply once
$`\sigma_{\text{rel}}\!>\!0.2`$. This differs from image generation,
where larger EMA lengths are often preferred \[[15](#bib.bib4)\]. While
EMA can improve system-level metrics such as the [FID](#id20)
([FID](#id20)) in image synthesis, our results indicate that longer EMA
lengths harm instance-level metrics in speech enhancement. We therefore
fix $`\sigma_{\text{rel}}\!=\!0.001`$ (the smallest possible EMA length
using the post-training reconstruction method) for all subsequent
experiments.

We next compare skip configurations, auxiliary loss weights, and matched
vs. mismatched training-test conditions.
Table [1](#S4.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 4 Experimental Setup ‣ Do We Need EMA for Diffusion-Based Speech Enhancement? Toward a Magnitude-Preserving Network Architecture")
summarizes results on the VoiceBank-DEMAND test set. Skip scaling
affects the metrics differently: $`c_{\text{s}}\!=\!1`$ (noise
prediction) favors PESQ, whereas $`c_{\text{s}}\!=\!0`$ (clean speech
prediction) slightly improves SI-SDR and non-intrusive measures. Using
an $`\ell_{1}`$ loss in the time-domain gives small gains in matched
conditions but degrades mismatched performance. Based on the best PESQ
scores in the matched condition, we adopt $`c_{\text{s}}\!=\!1`$ and
$`\alpha\!=\!0.001`$ for the next experiments.

Table [2](#S4.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 4 Experimental Setup ‣ Do We Need EMA for Diffusion-Based Speech Enhancement? Toward a Magnitude-Preserving Network Architecture")
compares EDM2SE with SGMSE+ \[[1](#bib.bib3)\] and
SB-VE \[[7](#bib.bib8)\]. Overall, EDM2SE performs on par with the other
models, with no statistically significant differences across metrics.
Under mismatched conditions, however, EDM2SE shows enhanced robustness,
highlighting the method’s strong generalization capability. For
completeness, we also evaluate SGMSE+ and SB-VE without EMA, which
yields slightly higher SI-SDR scores at the cost of small drops in the
other metrics. This indicates that EMA may also not be critical in
non-magnitude-preserving architectures such as NCSN++. However, the
training dynamics may differ since the baselines do not use an explicit
learning-rate schedule but rely on the Adam optimizer with momentum,
which could influence convergence behavior. Investigating the
interaction between EMA, learning-rate scheduling, and the Adam
optimizer is beyond the scope of this paper but represents an
interesting direction for future work.

Finally,
Table [3](#S4.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 4 Experimental Setup ‣ Do We Need EMA for Diffusion-Based Speech Enhancement? Toward a Magnitude-Preserving Network Architecture")
reports results on the EARS-WHAM (v2) test set at 16 kHz. For SGMSE+ and
SB-VE, we use pretrained checkpoints from \[[31](#bib.bib20)\], trained
at 48 kHz. Therefore, test files are upsampled before enhancement and
downsampled afterward. On this dataset, EDM2SE and SGMSE+ perform
similarly, while SB-VE struggles--likely due to the resampling
procedure.

## 6 Conclusion

We presented [EDM2SE](#id16), a diffusion-bridge speech enhancement
method based on the [EDM2](#id17) framework using the adapted
[MP-ADM](#id19) architecture. Our approach combines time-dependent
input/output preconditioning with two complementary skip-connection
configurations, enabling the neural network to predict either
environmental noise or clean speech. This design stabilizes training,
controls activation magnitudes, and exposes a trade-off between
signal-to-distortion ratio and perceptual scores.

For the first time, we examined the role of EMA in diffusion-based
speech enhancement by approximating different EMA profiles
post-training. In contrast to image generation, we found that longer EMA
horizons can harm instance-level metrics, whereas shorter or absent EMA
may yield better performance.

Across VoiceBank-DEMAND and EARS-WHAM, our method achieves competitive
speech enhancement performance and exhibits strong robustness under
mismatched conditions. These findings provide new insights into EMA
behavior, magnitude preservation, and skip-connection design, and
highlight promising directions for future work on generalizable
diffusion-based speech enhancement.
````
