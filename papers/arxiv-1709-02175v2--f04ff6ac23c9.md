# Normalized Features for Improving the Generalization of DNN Based Speech Enhancement

Robert Rehr and Timo Gerkmann

###### Abstract

Enhancing noisy speech is an important task to restore its quality and
to improve its intelligibility. In traditional non-machine-learning
based approaches the parameters required for noise reduction are
estimated blindly from the noisy observation while the actual filter
functions are derived analytically based on statistical assumptions.
Even though such approaches generalize well to many different acoustic
conditions, the noise suppression capability in transient noises is low.
To amend this shortcoming, machine-learning (machine-learning) methods
such as deep learning have been employed for speech enhancement.
However, due to their data-driven nature, the generalization of
machine-learning based approaches to unknown noise types is still
discussed. To improve the generalization of machine-learning based
algorithms and to enhance the noise suppression of non-machine-learning
based methods, we propose a combination of both approaches. For this, we
employ the _a priori_ signal-to-noise ratio and the _a posteriori_
signal-to-noise ratio estimated as input features in a deep neural
network based enhancement scheme. We show that this approach allows
machine-learning based speech estimators to generalize quickly to
unknown noise types even if only few noise conditions have been seen
during training. Further, the proposed features outperform a competing
approach where an estimate of the noise power spectral density is
appended to the noisy spectra. Instrumental measures such as Perceptual
Evaluation of Speech Quality and short-time objective intelligibility
indicate strong improvements in unseen conditions when the proposed
features are used. Listening experiments confirm the improved
generalization of our proposed combination.

###### Index Terms: 

Deep neural networks, machine learning, generalization, limited training
data, speech enhancement.

## I Introduction

In the presence of background noise, speech may be distorted such that
the speech intelligibility, as well as the quality of the speech signal
is deteriorated. Besides human perception, background noise also affects
automatic speech recognition algorithms for human-machine interfaces and
results in lower recognition rates. Speech enhancement algorithms
therefore play an important role for noise robust speech recognition and
for improving the speech quality in hearing aid and telecommunication
applications. In this paper, single-channel speech enhancement
algorithms are considered that either assume that the noisy signal has
been captured by a single microphone or process the output of a
beamformer \[[1](#bib.bib1)\].

Single-channel speech enhancement has been a research topic for many
decades and has led to many different methods, e.g., \[[2](#bib.bib2),
[3](#bib.bib3), [4](#bib.bib4), [5](#bib.bib5), [6](#bib.bib6),
[7](#bib.bib7), [8](#bib.bib8), [9](#bib.bib9), [10](#bib.bib10),
[11](#bib.bib11), [12](#bib.bib12), [13](#bib.bib13)\]. Here, we
distinguish between two broad categories of single-channel speech
enhancement schemes, namely machine-learning based and
non-machine-learning based approaches. machine-learning based
enhancement schemes generally follow a two-step approach to enhance a
noisy speech signal. First, the model parameters of an machine-learning
algorithm are tuned on training examples. After that, the obtained
models are used to separate the speech component from the background
noise. On the contrary, non-machine-learning based approaches do not
learn any models from training data prior to processing. Instead
carefully designed algorithms are used to estimate the parameters
required for the enhancement on-line and blindly from the noisy
observation.

Non-machine-learning based enhancement schemes such as \[[2](#bib.bib2),
[14](#bib.bib14), [15](#bib.bib15), [8](#bib.bib8), [9](#bib.bib9),
[10](#bib.bib10)\] commonly operate in the short-time Fourier transform
domain where a filter function is applied to suppress the coefficients
that mainly contain noise. The employed filter functions are often
derived in a statistical framework where the speech and noise
coefficients are modeled by parametric distributions. The consideration
of various statistical models and target functions has led to many
different solutions \[[2](#bib.bib2), [14](#bib.bib14),
[16](#bib.bib16), [17](#bib.bib17)\]. The estimators are functions of
the distributions’ parameters such as the speech power spectral density
and the noise power spectral density, which are estimated blindly from
the noisy observation. Various methods have been proposed to estimate
the noise power spectral density, e.g. \[[15](#bib.bib15),
[4](#bib.bib4), [5](#bib.bib5), [9](#bib.bib9), [10](#bib.bib10)\].
Generally, the algorithms’ design is based on the assumption that the
background noise changes more slowly than the speech signal. From the
noise power spectral density estimate and the noisy observation, the
speech power spectral density is obtained, e.g., \[[2](#bib.bib2),
[8](#bib.bib8)\]. Non-machine-learning approaches have been proven to
generalize well to many different acoustical environments and provide
good results in moderately varying noise types. However, they lack the
ability to track fast changes of the background noise due to their
underlying assumptions. As a consequence, transient sounds such as the
cutlery in a restaurant environment are generally not suppressed by
non-machine-learning based enhancement schemes.

The shortcomings of non-machine-learning based enhancement schemes
especially with respect to the limited noise tracking capabilities have
motivated the usage of machine-learning algorithms for speech
enhancement, e.g., \[[3](#bib.bib3), [18](#bib.bib18), [6](#bib.bib6),
[7](#bib.bib7), [11](#bib.bib11), [19](#bib.bib19), [12](#bib.bib12),
[13](#bib.bib13), [20](#bib.bib20), [21](#bib.bib21),
[22](#bib.bib22)\]. For this, various machine-learning algorithms have
been considered, e.g., codebooks \[[6](#bib.bib6), [21](#bib.bib21)\],
hidden Markov models \[[3](#bib.bib3), [7](#bib.bib7)\], Gaussian
mixture models \[[18](#bib.bib18)\] and non-negative matrix
factorization \[[11](#bib.bib11)\]. Recently, deep neural networks are
more intensively investigated for speech enhancement
applications \[[19](#bib.bib19), [12](#bib.bib12), [13](#bib.bib13),
[20](#bib.bib20), [22](#bib.bib22)\]. Neural networks potentially allow
to approximate any non-linear function on a limited range of the input
space. They have been used to replace or to improve building blocks,
e.g., the speech power spectral density and noise power spectral density
estimation in non-machine-learning algorithms \[[23](#bib.bib23),
[24](#bib.bib24), [25](#bib.bib25)\]. Other approaches use deep neural
networks to find a mapping from the noisy observation or features
extracted from it to a filter or masking function \[[19](#bib.bib19),
[20](#bib.bib20), [22](#bib.bib22)\]. Deep neural networks can as well
be utilized to learn a mapping where the target is directly given by the
clean speech coefficients as in \[[26](#bib.bib26), [12](#bib.bib12)\].
For this, various network types have been considered, e.g., feed-forward
networks \[[27](#bib.bib27), [12](#bib.bib12)\], recurrent neural
networks \[[28](#bib.bib28)\] including long short-term memory based
methods \[[29](#bib.bib29)\], convolutional neural
networks \[[30](#bib.bib30)\], generative adversarial
networks \[[31](#bib.bib31), [32](#bib.bib32)\] and WaveNet based
architectures \[[33](#bib.bib33), [34](#bib.bib34)\]. Studies on
machine-learning enhancement approaches, e.g., \[[12](#bib.bib12),
[20](#bib.bib20), [22](#bib.bib22)\], show that deep neural network
based approaches in principal have the ability to reduce transient
noises, but one of the major concerns towards machine-learning based
approaches is their generalization to noise types that have not been
seen during training. This issue is encountered for example with large
and diverse training data \[[12](#bib.bib12), [20](#bib.bib20)\], where
hundreds or even thousands of different noise types are included to
allow the deep neural network based enhancement schemes to generalize to
unseen noise conditions. Even though large training sets increase the
generalization, a huge number of noise types may still be inappropriate
as in real scenarios virtually infinitely many noise types can possibly
occur as argued in \[[13](#bib.bib13), [35](#bib.bib35)\].

To improve the robustness in unseen noise conditions, other approaches
incorporate estimates of non-machine-learning based noise power spectral
density estimators, e.g., \[[36](#bib.bib36), [37](#bib.bib37),
[12](#bib.bib12), [35](#bib.bib35)\]. For this, an estimate of the noise
power spectral density is appended to the noisy input features which is
referred to as noise aware training. In \[[36](#bib.bib36),
[12](#bib.bib12)\], a fixed noise power spectral density estimate is
used which has been obtained from the first frames of the noisy input
signal. In \[[37](#bib.bib37), [35](#bib.bib35)\], this idea has been
advanced by employing a dynamic, i.e., time-varying noise power spectral
density estimate, obtained from a non-machine-learning based estimator.
However, the results in \[[37](#bib.bib37), [35](#bib.bib35)\] show only
small improvements over the approaches that are not aware of the
background noise if a non-machine-learning approach is used to estimate
the noise power spectral density.

In this paper, we show that non-machine-learning based estimates of the
speech and noise power spectral density can considerably increase the
robustness of machine-learning based speech enhancement schemes towards
unseen noises. In contrast to noise aware training approaches, we
propose to employ the _a priori_ signal-to-noise ratio, i.e., the ratio
between the noisy periodogram and the noise power spectral density, and
the _a posteriori_ signal-to-noise ratio, i.e., the ratio of the noisy
periodogram and the noise power spectral density, as features. Thus,
instead of appending the noise power spectral density to the input
features extracted from the noise observation as in \[[36](#bib.bib36),
[37](#bib.bib37), [12](#bib.bib12), [35](#bib.bib35)\], here, the noise
power spectral density estimate is used for normalization. The usage of
the _a priori_ signal-to-noise ratio and the _a posteriori_
signal-to-noise ratio is motivated by non-linear clean speech
estimators, e.g., \[[2](#bib.bib2), [14](#bib.bib14)\] where these
quantities result from the derivation of Bayesian estimators. We show
that the proposed features outperform features where the noise power
spectral density estimate is appended to the noisy input vector.
Further, the proposed signal-to-noise ratio based features have the
advantage that the enhancement system is independent of the scaling of
the input signal, i.e., the overall level has no effect on the
enhancement.

These claims are confirmed in the evaluation using instrumental
measures. For this, Perceptual Evaluation of Speech
Quality \[[38](#bib.bib38)\] scores and the short-time objective
intelligibility \[[39](#bib.bib39)\] are evaluated in a cross-validation
based experimental setup, where different sets of noise types for
training and testing are used. Further, the instrumental evaluation is
supported by subjective evaluations. First, we describe the employed
algorithms in
Section [II](#S2 "II Non-ML Enhancement Algorithms ‣ Normalized Features for Improving the Generalization of DNN Based Speech Enhancement")
and
Section [III](#S3 "III ML Based Enhancement Algorithms ‣ Normalized Features for Improving the Generalization of DNN Based Speech Enhancement").
The results of the instrumental evaluation is given in
Section [IV](#S4 "IV Instrumental Evaluation ‣ Normalized Features for Improving the Generalization of DNN Based Speech Enhancement")
while the subjective evaluation is described in
Section [V](#S5 "V Subjective Evaluation ‣ Normalized Features for Improving the Generalization of DNN Based Speech Enhancement").

## II Non-ML Enhancement Algorithms

This section gives an overview over the non-machine-learning based
enhancement algorithms which form the basis of the proposed features in
Section [III](#S3 "III ML Based Enhancement Algorithms ‣ Normalized Features for Improving the Generalization of DNN Based Speech Enhancement").
First, the non-machine-learning based estimation of the clean speech
coefficients is considered which is based on the Wiener filter. After
that, the non-machine-learning based speech and noise power spectral
density estimators used in this paper are described.

### II-A Estimation of the Clean Speech Coefficients

For estimating the clean speech coefficients, the non-machine-learning
based clean speech estimators makes use of the short-time Fourier
transform. This representation is obtained by splitting the noisy input
signal into overlapping segments and taking the Fourier transform of
each segment after a tapered spectral analysis window has been applied.
The physically plausible assumption is used that the speech signal and
the noise signal mix additively, i.e.,

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       {Y}_{{k,\ell}}={S}_{{k,\ell}}+{N}_{{k,\ell}}.
       ```                                            |     | (1) |

The symbols $`{S}_{{k,\ell}}`$ and $`{N}_{{k,\ell}}`$ denote the complex
clean speech spectrum and the complex noise spectrum, respectively,
while $`{Y}_{{k,\ell}}`$ is the resulting spectrum of the noisy signal.
Furthermore, $`k`$ is the frequency index and $`\ell`$ is the segment
index. The speech coefficients are estimated using the Wiener filter
gain function $`G_{{k,\ell}}`$ as

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\hat{{S}}_{{k,\ell}}=\max(G_{{k,\ell}},G_{\text{min}}){Y}_{{k,\ell}},
``` |  | (2) |

where $`G_{\text{min}}`$ acts as a lower limit on the Wiener filter
gain $`G_{{k,\ell}}`$. The minimum gain $`G_{\text{min}}`$ is an
important parameter to limit artifacts in the enhanced signal such as
fluctuations in the reduced background noise or musical
tones \[[40](#bib.bib40)\]. The Wiener gain $`G_{{k,\ell}}`$ is given by

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
G_{{k,\ell}}=\frac{\Lambda^{s}_{k,\ell}}{\Lambda^{s}_{k,\ell}+\Lambda^{n}_{k,\ell}},
``` |  | (3) |

where $`\Lambda^{s}_{k,\ell}`$ and $`\Lambda^{n}_{k,\ell}`$ denote the
speech power spectral density and the noise power spectral density,
respectively. The clean speech estimates $`\hat{{S}}_{{k,\ell}}`$ are
transformed back to the time-domain for each segment $`\ell`$. Each
enhanced time-domain segment is weighted by a tapered synthesis window
and by using an overlap-add method, the time-domain signal is
reconstructed.

The Wiener filter is the minimum mean-squared error optimal estimator of
the clean speech coefficients if the speech
coefficients $`{S}_{{k,\ell}}`$ and the noise
coefficients $`{N}_{{k,\ell}}`$ are assumed to be uncorrelated and to
follow a complex circular-symmetric Gaussian distribution. This
assumption is often justified by the central limit theorem which may be
argued to apply due to the Fourier sum which needs to be evaluated for
obtaining the spectral coefficients \[[41](#bib.bib41), Chapter 4\]. The
speech power spectral density $`\Lambda^{s}_{k,\ell}`$ and the noise
power spectral density $`\Lambda^{n}_{k,\ell}`$ are estimated blindly
from the noisy observation using \[[9](#bib.bib9), [8](#bib.bib8)\].
Both algorithms are summarized in the following sections.

### II-B Non-ML Noise PSD Estimation

The algorithm presented in \[[9](#bib.bib9), [10](#bib.bib10)\] is used
to estimate the noise power spectral density. This estimator allows to
track moderate changes in the background noise such as passing cars.
However, it cannot track transient disturbances. In the remainder of
this section, the algorithm is briefly introduced.

The noise power spectral density estimator in \[[9](#bib.bib9),
[10](#bib.bib10)\] models the complex noisy coefficients under the
hypotheses of speech presence $`H_{1}`$ and speech absence $`H_{0}`$
using parametric distributions. Given $`H_{0}`$, the noisy observations
equals $`{Y}_{{k,\ell}}={N}_{{k,\ell}}`$ while under $`H_{1}`$ the noisy
coefficients are given by
$`{Y}_{{k,\ell}}={S}_{{k,\ell}}+{N}_{{k,\ell}}`$ as
in ([1](#S2.E1 "In II-A Estimation of the Clean Speech Coefficients ‣ II Non-ML Enhancement Algorithms ‣ Normalized Features for Improving the Generalization of DNN Based Speech Enhancement")).
As for the Wiener filter, the speech coefficients $`{S}_{{k,\ell}}`$ and
the noise coefficients $`{N}_{{k,\ell}}`$ are assumed to follow a
complex circular-symmetric Gaussian distribution. Accordingly, the
likelihoods under the hypotheses $`H_{0}`$ and $`H_{1}`$,
i.e., $`p({Y}_{{k,\ell}}|H_{0})`$ and $`p({Y}_{{k,\ell}}|H_{1})`$, are
also modeled using Gaussian distributions. The speech presence
probability is defined as the posterior probability
$`P(H_{1}|{Y}_{{k,\ell}})`$ which can be obtained using Bayes’ theorem.
A modified posterior is used in \[[9](#bib.bib9), [10](#bib.bib10)\] as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
P(H_{1}|{Y}_{{k,\ell}})={\left(1+(1+\xi_{H_{1}})\exp\left(-\frac{|{Y}_{{k,\ell}}|^{2}}{\hat{\Lambda}^{n}_{k,\ell-1}}\frac{\xi_{H_{1}}}{1+\xi_{H_{1}}}\right)\right)}^{-1},
``` |  | (4) |

which has been derived under the assumption that the prior
$`P(H_{1})=P(H_{0})=1/2`$. Here, a fixed signal-to-noise
ratio $`\xi_{H_{1}}`$ is used which is interpreted as the local
signal-to-noise ratio that is expected if the hypothesis $`H_{1}`$
holds \[[9](#bib.bib9), [10](#bib.bib10)\]. The likelihood
models $`p({Y}_{{k,\ell}}|H_{0})`$ and $`p({Y}_{{k,\ell}}|H_{1})`$ have
been used to formulate a speech detection problem
in \[[10](#bib.bib10)\]. By minimizing the total risk of
error \[[10](#bib.bib10)\], the optimal value
$`\xi_{H_{1}}=-15~\text{dB}`$ has been found.

The posterior probability $`P(H_{1}|{Y}_{{k,\ell}})`$ is used to
estimate the noise periodogram as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
|\hat{{N}}_{{k,\ell}}|^{2}=(1-P(H_{1}|{Y}_{{k,\ell}}))|{Y}_{{k,\ell}}|^{2}+P(H_{1}|{Y}_{{k,\ell}})\hat{\Lambda}^{n}_{k,\ell-1}.
``` |  | (5) |

The estimated noise periodogram $`|\hat{{N}}_{{k,\ell}}|^{2}`$ is
smoothed temporally to obtain an estimate of the noise power spectral
density as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\hat{\Lambda}^{n}_{k,\ell}=(1-\beta)|\hat{{N}}_{{k,\ell}}|^{2}+\beta\hat{\Lambda}^{n}_{k,\ell-1},
``` |  | (6) |

where $`\beta`$ is a fixed smoothing constant. This estimator can be
implemented in speech enhancement framework by
evaluating ([4](#S2.E4 "In II-B Non-ML Noise PSD Estimation ‣ II Non-ML Enhancement Algorithms ‣ Normalized Features for Improving the Generalization of DNN Based Speech Enhancement")), ([5](#S2.E5 "In II-B Non-ML Noise PSD Estimation ‣ II Non-ML Enhancement Algorithms ‣ Normalized Features for Improving the Generalization of DNN Based Speech Enhancement"))
and ([6](#S2.E6 "In II-B Non-ML Noise PSD Estimation ‣ II Non-ML Enhancement Algorithms ‣ Normalized Features for Improving the Generalization of DNN Based Speech Enhancement"))
for each frequency band $`k`$ when a new segment $`\ell`$ is processed.
If the noise power spectral density is strongly underestimated, the
speech presence probability
in ([4](#S2.E4 "In II-B Non-ML Noise PSD Estimation ‣ II Non-ML Enhancement Algorithms ‣ Normalized Features for Improving the Generalization of DNN Based Speech Enhancement"))
is overestimated, i.e., it is close to 1. As a result, the noise
periodogram
in ([5](#S2.E5 "In II-B Non-ML Noise PSD Estimation ‣ II Non-ML Enhancement Algorithms ‣ Normalized Features for Improving the Generalization of DNN Based Speech Enhancement"))
may no longer be updated. To avoid such stagnations, the speech presence
probability is set to a lower value if it has been stuck at 1 for a
longer period of time \[[9](#bib.bib9), [10](#bib.bib10)\].

### II-C Non-ML Speech PSD Estimation

For estimating the speech power spectral
density $`\Lambda^{s}_{k,\ell}`$, the temporal cepstrum smoothing
approach described in \[[8](#bib.bib8)\] is employed. In contrast to the
commonly used decision-directed approach \[[2](#bib.bib2)\], this
approach causes less isolated estimation estimation errors, which may be
perceived as annoying musical tones. In this section, we recapitulate
the main concepts of this algorithm.

Under the assumption that the spectral speech and noise coefficients
follow a complex circular-symmetric Gaussian distribution, the limited
maximum likelihood estimator is given by \[[2](#bib.bib2)\]

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\hat{\Lambda}^{s,\text{ml}}_{k,\ell}=\hat{\Lambda}^{n}_{k,\ell}\max\left(\frac{|{Y}_{{k,\ell}}|^{2}}{\hat{\Lambda}^{n}_{k,\ell}}-1,\xi^{\text{ml}}_{\text{min}}\right),
``` |  | (7) |

where the $`\max(\cdot)`$ operator in combination
with $`\xi^{\text{ml}}_{\text{min}}`$ is used to avoid negative speech
power spectral densitys and numerical issues in the following steps. For
the practical applicability, $`\Lambda^{n}_{k,\ell}`$ has been replaced
by its estimate $`\hat{\Lambda}^{n}_{k,\ell}`$.

The maximum likelihood estimate is transformed to the cepstral domain
via

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\hat{\Lambda}^{s,\text{ml}}_{q,\ell}=\text{IDFT}\{\log(\hat{\Lambda}^{s,\text{ml}}_{k,\ell})\},
``` |  | (8) |

where $`q`$ is the quefrency index and $`\text{IDFT}(\cdot)`$ denotes
the inverse discrete Fourier transform. In the cepstral domain, speech
can be represented by using only a few coefficients: The speech spectral
envelope, which reflects the impact of the vocal tract filter, is
represented by the lower coefficients with $`q<2.5~\text{ms}`$ whereas
the speech spectral fine structure, i.e., the fundamental frequency and
its harmonics, is approximated by a single peak among the high cepstral
coefficients. This peak is also referred to as pitch peak. The compact
representation of speech is exploited by the temporal cepstrum smoothing
approach by using a quefrency and time dependent smoothing constant
$`\alpha_{q,\ell}`$ to smooth $`\hat{\Lambda}^{s,\text{ml}}_{q,\ell}`$
as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\hat{\Lambda}^{s}_{q,\ell}=(1-\alpha_{q,\ell})\hat{\Lambda}^{s,\text{ml}}_{q,\ell}+\alpha_{q,\ell}\hat{\Lambda}^{s}_{q,\ell-1}.
``` |  | (9) |

For the cepstral coefficients that are associated with speech only
little smoothing is applied while the remaining cepstral coefficients
are strongly smoothed. Accordingly, $`\alpha_{q,\ell}`$ is set close to
0 for the lower cepstral coefficients and close to 1 for the high
coefficients. In voiced segments, the $`\alpha_{q,\ell}`$ in close
vicinity to the cepstral pitch peak are changed to values close to 0.

The cepstrally smoothed speech power spectral
density $`\hat{\Lambda}^{s}_{q,\ell}`$ is transformed back to the
spectral domain as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\hat{\Lambda}^{s}_{k,\ell}=\exp(\text{DFT}\{\hat{\Lambda}^{s}_{q,\ell}\}+\kappa).
``` |  | (10) |

As the smoothing in the cepstral domain results in a biased
estimate \[[42](#bib.bib42)\], the correction term $`\kappa`$ is added.
In \[[8](#bib.bib8)\], it has been argued that the bias of computing the
expected value of a spectral quantity following a Gaussian distribution
in the logarithmic domain amounts to the Euler constant. Due to the
smoothing, the estimate in the cepstral domain is between an
instantaneous value and the expected value. Hence, $`\kappa`$ is set
$`1/2`$ of the Euler constant, i.e.,
$`\kappa\approx 0.5\cdot 0.5772\dots`$ is used. A more rigorous analysis
of the bias is given in \[[42](#bib.bib42)\].

## III ML Based Enhancement Algorithms

In this section, the machine-learning based algorithms used in this
paper are presented. First, the enhancement framework is described and,
after that, the employed input features are considered. Note that the
algorithms share the same machine-learning based enhancement framework
but differ in the input features.

### III-A ML Based Enhancement Framework

The architecture of the used framework resembles the approaches that
have been proposed in \[[12](#bib.bib12), [22](#bib.bib22)\]. Similar to
the non-machine-learning based enhancement scheme, also the
machine-learning based approaches operate in the short-time Fourier
transform domain. A feed-forward deep neural network is used to predict
an ideal ratio mask from the input features extracted from the noisy
input signal. The features considered in this paper are described in
Section [III-B](#S3.SS2 "III-B Non-Normalized Features ‣ III ML Based Enhancement Algorithms ‣ Normalized Features for Improving the Generalization of DNN Based Speech Enhancement")
and
Section [III-C](#S3.SS3 "III-C Proposed Normalized Features ‣ III ML Based Enhancement Algorithms ‣ Normalized Features for Improving the Generalization of DNN Based Speech Enhancement")
in detail. The ideal ratio mask has been proposed
in \[[19](#bib.bib19)\] and is similar to the Wiener filter gain
function shown
in ([3](#S2.E3 "In II-A Estimation of the Clean Speech Coefficients ‣ II Non-ML Enhancement Algorithms ‣ Normalized Features for Improving the Generalization of DNN Based Speech Enhancement"))
with the difference that the speech periodogram $`|{S}_{{k,\ell}}|^{2}`$
and the noise periodogram $`|{N}_{{k,\ell}}|^{2}`$ are employed instead
of the respective power spectral densitys as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\text{IRM}(k,\ell)=\frac{|{S}_{{k,\ell}}|^{2}}{|{S}_{{k,\ell}}|^{2}+|{N}_{{k,\ell}}|^{2}}.
``` |  | (11) |

Similar to the Wiener filter, the predicted ideal ratio mask obtained
from the deep neural network is used to estimate the clean speech
coefficients $`\hat{{S}}_{{k,\ell}}`$ as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\hat{{S}}_{{k,\ell}}=\max\left(\widehat{\text{IRM}}(k,\ell),G_{\text{min}}\right){Y}_{{k,\ell}},
``` |  | (12) |

where $`\widehat{\text{IRM}}(\cdot)`$ denotes the ideal ratio mask
estimated by the deep neural network. We enforce a lower limit
$`G_{\text{min}}`$ as
in ([2](#S2.E2 "In II-A Estimation of the Clean Speech Coefficients ‣ II Non-ML Enhancement Algorithms ‣ Normalized Features for Improving the Generalization of DNN Based Speech Enhancement"))
and the time-domain signal is reconstructed using an overlap-add method.

### III-B Non-Normalized Features

In this part, the non-normalized feature inputs of the deep neural
network are presented. The first representative of the non-normalized
features is the logarithmized noisy periodogram, i.e.,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
{y}^{\text{(log)}}_{{k,\ell}}=\log\left(|{Y}_{{k,\ell}}|^{2}\right),
``` |  | (13) |

which has also been employed in \[[37](#bib.bib37), [12](#bib.bib12)\].
All spectral coefficients of a segment $`\ell`$, i.e.,
$`{y}^{\text{(log)}}_{{k,\ell}}`$ for all frequency bins $`k`$ for a
given frame $`\ell`$, are stacked in a feature vector.

Given only the log-spectral coefficients, the deep neural network needs
to learn how to distinguish between speech and noise using the training
data. This is a potentially challenging task, as a large amount of
different acoustic scenarios is required for training to match real
conditions. Hence, the approaches in \[[37](#bib.bib37),
[35](#bib.bib35)\] sought to improve the robustness to unseen noise
environments using non-machine-learning based noise power spectral
density estimators. For this, the noisy log-spectral features given
above have been extended by appending an estimate of the noise power
spectral density \[[37](#bib.bib37), [35](#bib.bib35)\], which is also
known as noise aware training \[[36](#bib.bib36)\]. Similar
to ([13](#S3.E13 "In III-B Non-Normalized Features ‣ III ML Based Enhancement Algorithms ‣ Normalized Features for Improving the Generalization of DNN Based Speech Enhancement")),
the logarithmized estimate of the noise power spectral density is given
by

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\hat{\Lambda}^{n,\text{(log)}}_{k,\ell}=\log\left(\hat{\Lambda}^{n}_{k,\ell}\right).
``` |  | (14) |

As a result, the feature vector for this set has twice the
dimensionality as using only the log-spectral features. In our
experiments, the noise power spectral density is estimated using the
algorithm proposed in \[[9](#bib.bib9), [10](#bib.bib10)\], i.e., using
the method described in
Section [II-B](#S2.SS2 "II-B Non-ML Noise PSD Estimation ‣ II Non-ML Enhancement Algorithms ‣ Normalized Features for Improving the Generalization of DNN Based Speech Enhancement").
For both features sets, a context of three past segments is added to
this vector by appending the respective feature vectors to the end of
the vector. We do not add context from future segments to keep the
algorithmic latency as low as for the non-machine-learning based
enhancement scheme.

### III-C Proposed Normalized Features

The main goal of the proposed normalized features is also to increase
the robustness of deep neural network based enhancement schemes to
unseen noise conditions. However, instead of appending the noise power
spectral densitys to the noisy input features, we incorporate the
generalization of non-machine-learning based enhancement schemes by
using the estimated noise power spectral density as normalization term.
More specifically, we employ the logarithmized *a priori*
signal-to-noise
ratio $`\xi_{{k,\ell}}^{\text{(log)}}=\log(\xi_{{k,\ell}})`$ and *a
posteriori* signal-to-noise
ratio $`\gamma_{{k,\ell}}^{\text{(log)}}=\log(\gamma_{{k,\ell}})`$ as
input features. The *a priori* signal-to-noise ratio and *a posteriori*
signal-to-noise ratio are defined as

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\xi_{{k,\ell}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{\Lambda^{s}_{k,\ell}}{\Lambda^{n}_{k,\ell}}`$ |  | (15) |
|  | $`\displaystyle\gamma_{{k,\ell}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{|{Y}_{{k,\ell}}|^{2}}{\Lambda^{n}_{k,\ell}}.`$ |  | (16) |

The usage of the *a priori* and *a posteriori* signal-to-noise ratios is
motivated by non-machine-learning based clean speech estimators,
e.g., \[[2](#bib.bib2), [14](#bib.bib14), [16](#bib.bib16),
[17](#bib.bib17)\], where the quantities appear in the analytical
solutions derived in a statistical framework. The speech power spectral
density $`\Lambda^{s}_{k,\ell}`$ and the noise power spectral
density $`\Lambda^{n}_{k,\ell}`$ are estimated blindly from the noisy
observation using the methods described in
Section [II-B](#S2.SS2 "II-B Non-ML Noise PSD Estimation ‣ II Non-ML Enhancement Algorithms ‣ Normalized Features for Improving the Generalization of DNN Based Speech Enhancement") \[[9](#bib.bib9)\]
and
Section [II-C](#S2.SS3 "II-C Non-ML Speech PSD Estimation ‣ II Non-ML Enhancement Algorithms ‣ Normalized Features for Improving the Generalization of DNN Based Speech Enhancement") \[[8](#bib.bib8)\].
Both signal-to-noise ratios can be used separately or in combination by
concatenating both in a single vector. Note that the dimensionality of
the features is the same as the noisy log-spectra if one of the
signal-to-noise ratios is used as input. In all considered cases, a
temporal context of three previous segments is appended to the feature
vectors.

In contrast to the non-normalized features in
Section [III-B](#S3.SS2 "III-B Non-Normalized Features ‣ III ML Based Enhancement Algorithms ‣ Normalized Features for Improving the Generalization of DNN Based Speech Enhancement"),
the *a priori* and the *a posteriori* signal-to-noise ratio exhibit the
advantage that these features are scale-invariant. As their value does
not depend on the overall level, differently scaled training data
results in identical normalized features as when the scaling is not
varied. To make the scale-invariance also available to a deep neural
network using non-normalized features, e.g.,
Section [III-B](#S3.SS2 "III-B Non-Normalized Features ‣ III ML Based Enhancement Algorithms ‣ Normalized Features for Improving the Generalization of DNN Based Speech Enhancement"),
the training data has to reflect these gain variations. This increases
the variations in the training examples such that learning potentially
becomes challenging. Such gain variations do not increase the
variability for the normalized features, which may allow to improve the
enhancement.

## IV Instrumental Evaluation

In this section, the algorithms described in
Section [II](#S2 "II Non-ML Enhancement Algorithms ‣ Normalized Features for Improving the Generalization of DNN Based Speech Enhancement")
and
Section [III](#S3 "III ML Based Enhancement Algorithms ‣ Normalized Features for Improving the Generalization of DNN Based Speech Enhancement")
are compared using instrumental measures. Further, the optimally
modified log-spectral amplitude estimator proposed in \[[4](#bib.bib4),
[5](#bib.bib5)\] is used as a reference. First, the audio material, the
used parameters and instrumental measures are considered. Afterwards,
the results of the experimental analysis are presented which compares
the properties of the normalized and the non-normalized input features
in the deep neural network framework. Further, the computational
complexity and the training convergence speed are considered. In the
last part of the instrumental evaluation, the performance of the
evaluated algorithms is compared.

### IV-A Audio Material, Parameters and Instrumental Measures

For all algorithms, the short-time Fourier transform uses 32 ms segments
which overlap by 50 %. For the analysis step as well as the synthesis
step a square-root Hann window is employed. All signals have a sampling
rate of 16 kHz. For computing the features, the mirror spectrum is
omitted such that the resulting dimension of the spectra is 257.
Correspondingly, the feature dimensionality of the noisy
log-spectra $`{y}^{\text{(log)}}_{{k,\ell}}`$, the *a priori*
signal-to-noise ratio $`\xi^{\text{(log)}}_{{k,\ell}}`$ and the *a
posteriori* signal-to-noise ratio $`\gamma^{\text{(log)}}_{{k,\ell}}`$
is $`257\times(3+1)=1028`$ including the context. The dimensionality of
the input features doubles to $`2056`$ for the combination of the *a
priori* signal-to-noise ratio $`\xi_{{k,\ell}}`$ and the *a
posteriori* signal-to-noise ratio, as well as, for the combination of
the noisy log-spectra $`{y}^{\text{(log)}}_{{k,\ell}}`$ and the
logarithmized noise power spectral
density $`\hat{\Lambda}^{n,\text{(log)}}_{k,\ell}`$ as employed
in \[[37](#bib.bib37), [35](#bib.bib35)\]. The deep neural network’s
architecture comprises three hidden layers with rectified linear
units \[[43](#bib.bib43)\] as non-linearities and an output layer with
sigmoidal activation functions. The number of units in each hidden
layers amounts to 1024 for both deep neural network based approaches.
For the evaluations in this section, the minimum gain is set to
$`G_{\text{min}}=-20~\text{dB}`$ for all employed enhancement schemes.
For the non-machine-learning algorithms in
Section [II](#S2 "II Non-ML Enhancement Algorithms ‣ Normalized Features for Improving the Generalization of DNN Based Speech Enhancement")
the parameters in the respective publication \[[8](#bib.bib8),
[9](#bib.bib9)\] are used.

The employed background noises are taken from a fixed pool of nine noise
types. It includes the babble noise and the factory 1 noise taken from
the NOISEX-92 database \[[44](#bib.bib44)\]. Further, a modulated
version of NOISEX-92’s pink and white noise are included as
in \[[9](#bib.bib9)\]. Additional noise types are taken from the
freesound database http://www.freesound.org. Among them are the sounds
of an overpassing propeller plane (https://freesound.org/s/115387/), the
interior of a passenger jet during flight
(https://freesound.org/s/188810/), a vacuum cleaner
(https://freesound.org/s/67421/) and a traffic noise
(https://freesound.org/s/75375/). Further, a two-talker babble noise is
included which is generated using two read out stories taken from
https://www.vorleser.net. The two stories are read by a male and a
female speaker, respectively, and are mixed at an signal-to-noise ratio
of $`0~\text{dB}`$ after speech pauses have been removed. The noise
types are used to conduct cross-validation experiments where all noise
types except one are included in the training set. The training data of
each cross-validation set are augmented by additionally including a
highly non-stationary noise type which is generated from the noise
snippets collected by \[[45](#bib.bib45)\]. The noise excerpts in this
database are generally short and are, hence, concatenated multiple times
in various orders to give a continuous noise signal. Long noise excerpts
are split into roughly 2 second long snippets during this generation.
This noise type is referred to as concatenated short noise excerpts. The
remaining unseen noise type is used for testing in the evaluations.

The speech material for training is taken from the TIMIT
database \[[46](#bib.bib46)\]. For the training of the deep neural
network based enhancement schemes, a set of 1196 female and 1196 male
sentences taken from the TIMIT training set is employed. All sentences
are embedded once in each noise type used for training at a random
temporal position. For the employed noise power spectral density
estimator, a two second initialization period is added at the beginning
of each sentence to avoid initialization artifacts during feature
extraction. This period is removed from the final features used for
training. However, a noise only period which amounts to $`15~\%`$ of the
utterance length is included for each sentence in the training data. To
allow the deep neural network to learn the effect of different
signal-to-noise ratios, the sentences are embedded in the background
noise at signal-to-noise ratios ranging from $`-10~\text{dB}`$ to
$`15~\text{dB}`$. The signal-to-noise ratio is randomly chosen for each
sentence and also the scaling is randomly varied for each sentence by
adjusting the peak level of the speech signal from $`-26~\text{dB}`$ and
$`-3~\text{dB}`$. These variations are included in the training data, to
allow the deep neural network based on the non-normalized features to
learn a scale-independent function of the ideal ratio mask.

The parameters of the deep neural network are adapted by minimizing the
following optimization criterion

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
J=\sum_{\ell}\sum_{k}\left|\log\Big(\widehat{\text{IRM}}(k,\ell)+\varepsilon\Big)-\log\Big(\text{IRM}(k,\ell)+\varepsilon\Big)\right|^{2}.
``` |  | (17) |

Here, the squared error of the logarithmized quantities is minimized
which is motivated by the human loudness perception which approximately
follows a logarithmic law. Further, $`\varepsilon`$ is a bias term which
is used to avoid that extremely low gains of the target ideal ratio mask
are overly penalized by the cost function. Here, $`\varepsilon=0.1`$ is
employed such that differences between the target ideal ratio mask and
the deep neural network output are treated as irrelevant if the target
ideal ratio mask is below $`-20~\text{dB}`$. The weights and biases of
the layers are initialized using the Glorot method \[[47](#bib.bib47)\].
After the initialization, the weights are optimized using the AdaGrad
approach \[[48](#bib.bib48)\] where the learning rate has been set to
0.005 while a batch size of 128 samples has been used. The order of the
training observations is randomized. To avoid overfitting of the
network, an early stopping scheme is employed where the training
procedure is stopped if the error $`J`$ is not reduced by more than
$`1\,\%`$ over 10 iterations on a validation set. The validation set is
constructed by randomly selecting 15 % of the training set.

For testing, 128 sentences, 64 female and 64 male, are taken from the
TIMIT test set. Similar to the training, the clean speech sentences are
embedded at random positions in the background. All sentences are mixed
at signal-to-noise ratios ranging from $`-5~\text{dB}`$ to
$`20~\text{dB}`$ in $`5~\text{dB}`$ steps. Furthermore, also here, an
initialization period of two seconds is added to avoid initialization
artifacts of the employed noise power spectral density
estimator \[[9](#bib.bib9)\]. This period is omitted during the
evaluation, i.e., the instrumental measures are only evaluated on the
part that contains the embedded sentence.

For the comparison, Perceptual Evaluation of Speech
Quality \[[38](#bib.bib38)\] is used as an instrumental measure to
evaluate the quality of the enhanced signals. Generally, Perceptual
Evaluation of Speech Quality improvement scores ($`\Delta`$PESQ) are
shown which are obtained by computing the difference between the
Perceptual Evaluation of Speech Quality score of the enhanced and the
noisy signal. Further, short-time objective
intelligibility \[[39](#bib.bib39)\] is used to instrumentally predict
the speech intelligibility of the enhanced signals. In this evaluation,
the short-time objective intelligibility scores are mapped to actual
intelligibility scores, i.e., the percentage of words a human would
correctly identify in a listening experiment. As no mapping is available
for the TIMIT database, the mapping function given for the IEEE
sentences in \[[39](#bib.bib39)\] is used. Also here, improvements are
computed ($`\Delta`$STOI) which are obtained by subtracting the mapped
speech intelligibility of the enhanced and the noisy signal.

### IV-B Analysis

In this part, we give an analysis on the features proposed in
Section [III-B](#S3.SS2 "III-B Non-Normalized Features ‣ III ML Based Enhancement Algorithms ‣ Normalized Features for Improving the Generalization of DNN Based Speech Enhancement")
and
Section [III-C](#S3.SS3 "III-C Proposed Normalized Features ‣ III ML Based Enhancement Algorithms ‣ Normalized Features for Improving the Generalization of DNN Based Speech Enhancement").
We demonstrate that the proposed normalized features in
Section [III-C](#S3.SS3 "III-C Proposed Normalized Features ‣ III ML Based Enhancement Algorithms ‣ Normalized Features for Improving the Generalization of DNN Based Speech Enhancement")
are independent of scaling of the input signal. Further, we show that
the deep neural network converges more quickly if the proposed features
are employed. In the last part of this section, the computational
complexity of the various approaches is considered.



Fig. 1: PESQ and STOI improvements for the considered enhancement
algorithms in dependence of the peak level of the clean speech signal
averaged over all noise types at an input SNR of 0 dB.

To demonstrate the scale-invariance of the proposed features, the
considered enhancement approaches are evaluated on speech material where
the peak level of the speech utterances is varied systematically. For
this, we set the peak level of the speech utterances to
$`-6~\text{dB}`$, $`-12~\text{dB}`$, $`-18~\text{dB}`$,
$`-24~\text{dB}`$ and $`-40~\text{dB}`$. The $`-40~\text{dB}`$ peak
level has not been seen during training and can be considered an extreme
case whereas the remaining levels are within the range of variations
included in the training data. For this evaluation, the signal-to-noise
ratio of the input signals is fixed at $`-5~\text{dB}`$ for short-time
objective intelligibility and $`5~\text{dB}`$ for Perceptual Evaluation
of Speech Quality. A lower signal-to-noise ratio is used for short-time
objective intelligibility because the speech intelligibility reduces
only considerably for signal-to-noise ratios lower than $`0~\text{dB}`$.
The results in terms of Perceptual Evaluation of Speech Quality and
short-time objective intelligibility improvements are depicted in
Fig. [1](#S4.F1 "Fig. 1 ‣ IV-B Analysis ‣ IV Instrumental Evaluation ‣ Normalized Features for Improving the Generalization of DNN Based Speech Enhancement").
For this, the averages over all noise types excluding the concatenated
short noise excerpts \[[45](#bib.bib45)\] are computed. The results show
that the non-machine-learning based speech enhancement algorithms and
the machine-learning based approaches based on the normalized features
yield the same outcome independent of the scaling of the input signal.
Contrarily, the performance of the machine-learning based enhancement
scheme using noisy log-spectra varies over the peak level of the input
signal. The same can be observed for the combination with the estimated
noise power spectral density. This indicates that by using the
normalized features, the machine-learning based algorithm does not
depend on the overall level. Contrarily, despite the efforts taken to
increase the scale-independence during the training process, the
non-normalized features result in scale-dependent results.

The convergence speed of the proposed features is measured using the
number of epochs that have been required until the validation error
converges. Due to the cross-validation setup, nine models are trained
for each feature type which allows to average the number of epochs
required to train each model. About 28 to 29 epochs are required on
average if the non-normalized features are employed, whereas only 20 to
23 iterations are required for the normalized features. This result
provides evidence that the proposed normalized features simplify the
training of the respective deep neural networks.

Fig. 2: Computational complexity of the considered algorithms in terms
of the real-time factor. This factor describes how many seconds of the
audio material can be processed within a second in the real world. The
number on top of the bar shows the actual value of the real-time factor.

Last, the computational complexity of the considered algorithms is
considered.
Fig. [2](#S4.F2 "Fig. 2 ‣ IV-B Analysis ‣ IV Instrumental Evaluation ‣ Normalized Features for Improving the Generalization of DNN Based Speech Enhancement"),
shows the processing speed of the various algorithms in terms of the
real-time factor. The factor describes how many seconds of the audio
signal can be processed within a second in the real world.
Correspondingly, if the factor is larger than one, the algorithm
processes the signals faster than real-time and if the factor is smaller
than one, the processing is slower than real-time. The algorithms have
been evaluated on the CPU (Intel Core i7-5930K) of a desktop PC. For
this, their respective Python or Matlab implementations have been used.
Fig. [2](#S4.F2 "Fig. 2 ‣ IV-B Analysis ‣ IV Instrumental Evaluation ‣ Normalized Features for Improving the Generalization of DNN Based Speech Enhancement")
shows that the optimally modified log-spectral amplitude estimator runs
slowest while the non-machine-learning described in
Section [II](#S2 "II Non-ML Enhancement Algorithms ‣ Normalized Features for Improving the Generalization of DNN Based Speech Enhancement")
and the machine-learning based algorithms where the noisy log-spectra
$`{y}^{\text{(log)}}_{{k,\ell}}`$ or the *a posteriori* signal-to-noise
ratio $`\gamma_{{k,\ell}}`$ are used as input feature run fastest. On
the used hardware, the quickest algorithms run roughly 20 times faster
than real-time. The optimally modified log-spectral amplitude estimator
is only about three times faster than real-time which may be explained
by the fact that the Matlab implementation is run through Python which
potentially introduces further processing overhead. Using the *a priori*
signal-to-noise ratio $`\xi^{\text{(log)}}_{{k,\ell}}`$ instead of the
*a posteriori* $`\gamma^{\text{(log)}}_{{k,\ell}}`$ reduces the
real-time factor to 10. This is because, in comparison to
$`\gamma^{\text{(log)}}_{{k,\ell}}`$, the cepstral smoothing given in
Section [II-C](#S2.SS3 "II-C Non-ML Speech PSD Estimation ‣ II Non-ML Enhancement Algorithms ‣ Normalized Features for Improving the Generalization of DNN Based Speech Enhancement")
needs to be additionally computed to
obtain $`\xi^{\text{(log)}}_{{k,\ell}}`$. Using both, the *a priori*
signal-to-noise ratio $`\xi^{\text{(log)}}_{{k,\ell}}`$ and the *a
posteriori* signal-to-noise ratio $`\gamma^{\text{(log)}}_{{k,\ell}}`$,
as input features, the real-time factor further drops to 9.
Concatenating $`{y}^{\text{(log)}}_{{k,\ell}}`$ and
$`\Lambda^{n,\text{(log)}}_{k,\ell}`$ is computationally more complex
than using the $`{Y}_{{k,\ell}}`$ normalized by
$`\Lambda^{n}_{k,\ell}`$, i.e., $`\gamma^{\text{(log)}}_{{k,\ell}}`$.
For the concatenated features, the input dimensionality is twice as
large as for the *a posteriori* signal-to-noise ratio
$`\gamma^{\text{(log)}}_{{k,\ell}}`$ which results in the additional
computational complexity. From this, it is followed that the inclusion
of the noise power spectral density generally increases the
computational complexity as expected. Using
$`\gamma^{\text{(log)}}_{{k,\ell}}`$, the increase is only small whereas
including the *a priori* signal-to-noise ratio considerably increases
the complexity.

The following results show the outcome of the cross-validation procedure

For both instrumental measures, first the performance of the

signal-to-noise ratios. This is, however, an exception as for the

the performance of the employed non-machine-learning enhancement

amplitude estimator. In terms of the speech intelligibility predicted by
short-time objective intelligibility, both non-machine-learning
approaches have either little effect or reduce the intelligibility. In
all cases, however, $`\Delta`$short-time objective intelligibility is
higher for the approach described in
Section [II](#S2 "II Non-ML Enhancement Algorithms ‣ Normalized Features for Improving the Generalization of DNN Based Speech Enhancement")
than for the optimally modified log-spectral amplitude estimator.
Consequently, the algorithm described in
Section [II](#S2 "II Non-ML Enhancement Algorithms ‣ Normalized Features for Improving the Generalization of DNN Based Speech Enhancement")
generally outperforms the optimally modified log-spectral amplitude
estimator \[[4](#bib.bib4), [5](#bib.bib5)\].

The speech intelligibility predicted by short-time objective
intelligibility is generally higher for the machine-learning based
algorithms than for the non-machine-learning approaches. In factory
noise and the aircraft interior noise, short-time objective
intelligibility predicts a higher speech intelligibility for the
non-normalized features. The same is true for the overpassing plane and
the two-talker noise. Here, however, $`\Delta`$short-time objective
intelligibility is generally smaller compared to the other noise types.
Among the non-normalized features, $`\Delta`$short-time objective
intelligibility is generally higher for the combination of the noisy
log-spectra $`{y}^{\text{(log)}}_{{k,\ell}}`$ and the estimated noise
power spectral density $`\Lambda^{n,\text{(log)}}_{k,\ell}`$. For the
remaining noise types, however, the proposed normalized features yield
similar or higher short-time objective intelligibility improvements than
the non-normalized features. Comparing the normalized feature sets
amongst each other shows that using only the *a priori* signal-to-noise
ratio $`\xi^{\text{(log)}}_{{k,\ell}}`$ often results in the lowest
scores. Contrarily, the combination of the *a priori* signal-to-noise
ratio $`\xi^{\text{(log)}}_{{k,\ell}}`$ and the *a posteriori*
signal-to-noise ratio $`\gamma^{\text{(log)}}_{{k,\ell}}`$ generally
yields the highest scores. In many cases, using only the *a posteriori*
signal-to-noise ratio $`\gamma^{\text{(log)}}_{{k,\ell}}`$ yields scores
similar to the combination. For cases, where the computational
complexity plays an important role this feature type is thus a
considerable alternative.

The Perceptual Evaluation of Speech Quality improvements for the
machine-learning based algorithms indicate a clear preference for the
proposed normalized features. Only for the two talker noise, the
Perceptual Evaluation of Speech Quality improvements obtained for the
non-normalized features are higher than for the normalized features.
However, as basically all the considered enhancement algorithms struggle
in this noise type, the gains of 0.05 points are rather small and
therefore negligible. For most of the remaining noise types, the
performance of the non-normalized features predicted by Perceptual
Evaluation of Speech Quality is between the optimally modified
log-spectral amplitude estimator and the non-machine-learning approach
described in
Section [II](#S2 "II Non-ML Enhancement Algorithms ‣ Normalized Features for Improving the Generalization of DNN Based Speech Enhancement").
Except for the modulated white noise, Perceptual Evaluation of Speech
Quality does not indicate considerable advantages if an estimate of the
noise power spectral density $`\Lambda^{n,\text{(log)}}_{k,\ell}`$ is
appended to the noisy log-spectra $`{y}^{\text{(log)}}_{{k,\ell}}`$.
This changes if the normalized features are used. Using these features,
the performance of the machine-learning approach is more robust and,
often, both non-machine-learning approaches are outperformed. Again, the
combination of the *a priori* signal-to-noise
ratio $`\xi^{\text{(log)}}_{{k,\ell}}`$ and *a posteriori*
signal-to-noise ratio $`\gamma^{\text{(log)}}_{{k,\ell}}`$ yields the
highest scores in most noise types. Also here, using the *a posteriori*
signal-to-noise ratio $`\gamma^{\text{(log)}}_{{k,\ell}}`$ without the
*a priori* signal-to-noise ratio $`\xi^{\text{(log)}}_{{k,\ell}}`$
yields similar results as the combination of both. Consequently, it is
possible to benefit from the advantages of the normalized features
without severely increasing the computational complexity. Further, as
this feature type has the same dimensionality as the noisy log-spectra,
this demonstrates the importance of the normalized features on the
generalization of machine-learning based enhancement schemes.

## V Subjective Evaluation

Instrumental measures such as Perceptual Evaluation of Speech Quality
give an indication on how the quality of the processed signals would be
judged by humans. Still, as such measures cannot perfectly model human
perception, we verify the instrumental results in
Section [IV](#S4 "IV Instrumental Evaluation ‣ Normalized Features for Improving the Generalization of DNN Based Speech Enhancement")
using subjective evaluation tests. Here, a multi-stimulus test with
hidden reference and anchor \[[49](#bib.bib49)\] is employed to compare
the algorithms described in
Section [II](#S2 "II Non-ML Enhancement Algorithms ‣ Normalized Features for Improving the Generalization of DNN Based Speech Enhancement")
and
Section [III](#S3 "III ML Based Enhancement Algorithms ‣ Normalized Features for Improving the Generalization of DNN Based Speech Enhancement").
First, the audio material, parameters and evaluation are explained and,
after that, the results are discussed.

### V-A Audio Material, Parameters and Setup

For this experiment, a sentence of a male and a female speaker is
embedded in factory 1 noise and traffic noise at an signal-to-noise
ratio of 5 dB. The noisy signals are processed by the speech enhancement
schemes described in
Section [II](#S2 "II Non-ML Enhancement Algorithms ‣ Normalized Features for Improving the Generalization of DNN Based Speech Enhancement")
and
Section [III](#S3 "III ML Based Enhancement Algorithms ‣ Normalized Features for Improving the Generalization of DNN Based Speech Enhancement").
The machine-learning based algorithm is included once using the noisy
log-spectra as features $`{y}^{\text{(log)}}_{{k,\ell}}`$ and once using
the combination of *a priori* signal-to-noise
ratio $`\xi^{\text{(log)}}_{{k,\ell}}`$ and *a posteriori*
signal-to-noise ratio $`\gamma^{\text{(log)}}_{{k,\ell}}`$. For this
experiment, the concatenated short noise excerpts \[[45](#bib.bib45)\]
and the two talker noise are excluded from the noise type pool such that
eight noise types remain. We train the deep neural network once on a set
which includes mod. pink noise, mod. white noise, factory 1 noise and
traffic noise. Note that this includes the traffic and factory noise
which is also used for testing, i.e., this corresponds to a seen
condition. For this condition, it is ensured that the noise realizations
used for the training are not reused for testing. Therefore, only the
first $`120~\text{s}`$ of the noise types are used while the last
$`120~\text{s}`$ are used to embed the sentences for the listening
experiment. The algorithms have also been evaluated in an unseen
condition where all noise types are included in the training set except
the one used for evaluation. Here, the full length of the training noise
is utilized. For each sentence embedded in the training noise type, the
peak level is varied between $`-26~\text{dB}`$ and $`-6~\text{dB}`$,
while the signal-to-noise ratio is chosen between $`-5~\text{dB}`$ and
$`15~\text{dB}`$. The minimum gain is set to
$`G_{\text{min}}=-15~\text{dB}`$ in this experiment.

In each trial of the experiment, the participants compared six stimuli.
In addition to the processed signals, the noisy signal is included and a
reference signal is presented where the speech signal and the background
noise are mixed at an signal-to-noise ratio of $`20~\text{dB}`$. Lastly,
a low quality anchor is added where the speech signal is low pass
filtered at $`2~\text{kHz}`$ and mixed at an signal-to-noise ratio of
$`-5~\text{dB}`$. This signal is enhanced using a non-machine-learning
based enhancement algorithm where the noise power spectral density is
estimated using \[[9](#bib.bib9)\] while the speech power spectral
density is obtained using the decision-directed
approach \[[2](#bib.bib2)\]. The smoothing constant is set to $`0.9`$
and the signal is enhanced using the Wiener filter where a more
aggressive lower limit of $`-20~\text{dB}`$ is employed. This results in
an anchor signal with very poor quality due to many musical tone
artifacts and strong speech distortions. The audio examples used for the
listening experiment are available under
https://www.inf.uni-hamburg.de/en/inst/ab/sp/publications/tasl2017-dnn-rr.

A total of 11 subjects with age in the range of 24 to 38 years who are
not familiar with single-channel signal processing have participated in
the multi-stimulus test with hidden reference and anchor. The experiment
took place in a quiet office. The diotic signals were presented via
Beyerdynamic DT-770 Pro 250 Ohm headphones attached to an RME Fireface
UFX+ sound card. All signals were normalized in amplitude. The test
consisted of two phases. First, the participants were asked to complete
a training phase to familiarize with the presented sounds and to adjust
the volume to a comfortable level. For this, a subset of the processed
signals was presented. In the second part of the experiment, the
participants were asked to rate the signals according to their overall
preference on a scale from 0 to 100, where 0 was labeled with “bad” and
100 with “excellent”. The order of the presentation of algorithms and
conditions were randomized between all subjects.

schemes. The left column shows the results for factory 1 noise and the
right column for traffic noise as test signals. The rows show different

### V-B Results

For the evaluation, we average the ratings over the two speakers for
each tested scenario. Further, the results are validated using a
statistical analysis. For each acoustic scenario, a repeated measures
analysis of variance \[[50](#bib.bib50)\] is performed to test if the
factor “enhancement algorithm” has a significant effect on the
participants’ rating. For this, we employ a significance level of 5 %
for all statistical tests. For each acoustic scenario, we validated that
the residuals of the general linear model fitted during the process of
the repeated measures analysis of variance are normally distributed
using the Shapiro-Wilk test \[[51](#bib.bib51)\]. The sphericity
assumption has been validated using Mauchly’s test \[[52](#bib.bib52)\]
and a Greenhouse-Geisser correction \[[53](#bib.bib53)\] is employed in
cases where it has been violated. In all acoustic scenarios, the
enhancement algorithms have a statistically significant effect on
ratings. Hence, post-hoc tests are used to identify the sources of
significance. For this, matched pair $`t`$-tests with a
Bonferroni-Holm \[[54](#bib.bib54)\] correction are employed to account
for the error inflation. The results are shown in
Fig. [4](#S5.F4 "Fig. 4 ‣ V-A Audio Material, Parameters and Setup ‣ V Subjective Evaluation ‣ Normalized Features for Improving the Generalization of DNN Based Speech Enhancement")
where the ratings that are statistically *not* significantly different
are indicated by linking lines.

All listeners were able to correctly identify the hidden reference and
assigned the highest score to it. The anchor signal and the noisy signal
were assigned the lowest scores in most of the cases. For the seen
conditions, all enhancement schemes have been rated similar in traffic
noise, while in factory noise, both machine-learning based speech
enhancement schemes yield slightly better results than the
non-machine-learning based algorithm. For the unseen conditions, the
ratings for the machine-learning based approach only using the
non-normalized noisy log-spectra as features drop while the ratings for
the proposed normalized features remain high. Additionally, the proposed
features show slightly higher ratings in comparison to the
non-machine-learning enhancement scheme in factory noise. The
statistical evaluation confirms that the highlighted differences are
statistically significant.

## VI Conclusions

In this paper, we propose features for machine-learning based speech
enhancement which incorporate non-machine-learning based estimates of
the speech and noise power spectral density. The goal is to improve the
robustness of machine-learning based enhancement scheme towards unseen
noise conditions. In contrast to the already existing noise aware
training \[[36](#bib.bib36), [37](#bib.bib37), [12](#bib.bib12),
[35](#bib.bib35)\], the noise power spectral density is not appended but
used as a normalizing term. This results in the *a priori*
signal-to-noise ratio and the *a posteriori* signal-to-noise ratio which
exhibit the advantageous property of being scale-invariant. For the
noisy log-spectra, the performance of the machine-learning based
enhancement scheme in terms of Perceptual Evaluation of Speech Quality
is low in unseen noise conditions. Appending an estimate of the noise
power spectral density has only a little impact on the performance in
Perceptual Evaluation of Speech Quality while the intelligibility
predicted by short-time objective intelligibility increases. Using the
proposed normalized features, however, the performance of the
machine-learning based enhancement scheme is generally higher as for the
compared algorithms in both instrumental measures. This is supported by
the multi-stimulus test with hidden reference and anchor based listening
experiments where, in unseen noise conditions, the proposed combination
was significantly preferred over the machine-learning based enhancement
scheme using only the log-spectra of the noisy observations. Audio
examples are available under
https://www.inf.uni-hamburg.de/en/inst/ab/sp/publications/tasl2017-dnn-rr.
Feed-forward networks clearly benefit from the proposed normalized
features, but their effect on other architectures such as recurrent
neural networks or convolutional networks remains a question for future
research.
