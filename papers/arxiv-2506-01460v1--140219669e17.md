# Few-step Adversarial Schrödinger Bridge for Generative Speech Enhancement

Seungu    Sungho    Juheon    Kyogu Affiliation: Department of
Intelligence and Information Affiliation:  Affiliation: Artificial
Intelligence Institute

###### Abstract

Deep generative models have recently been employed for speech
enhancement to generate perceptually valid clean speech on large-scale
datasets. Several diffusion models have been proposed, and more
recently, a tractable Schrödinger Bridge has been introduced to
transport between the clean and noisy speech distributions. However,
these models often suffer from an iterative reverse process and require
a large number of sampling steps—more than 50. Our investigation reveals
that the performance of baseline models significantly degrades when the
number of sampling steps is reduced, particularly under low-SNR
conditions. We propose integrating Schrödinger Bridge with GANs to
effectively mitigate this issue, achieving high-quality outputs on
full-band datasets while substantially reducing the required sampling
steps. Experimental results demonstrate that our proposed model
outperforms existing baselines, even with a single inference step, in
both denoising and dereverberation tasks.

###### keywords

speech enhancement, speech denoising, speech dereverberation,
Schrödinger bridge, Diffusion-GAN Hybrids

## 1 Introduction

Deep generative models have been employed for various speech enhancement
and restoration tasks \[[1](#bib.bib1), [2](#bib.bib2), [3](#bib.bib3),
[4](#bib.bib4), [5](#bib.bib5), [6](#bib.bib6)\]. This approach is
motivated by the inherent nature of the problem: under severe
conditions, such as low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) scenarios, some
information is lost, making it necessary to generate the missing
parts \[[7](#bib.bib7)\]. Furthermore, unlike predictive models, which
map inputs to a single deterministic output, generative models learn the
underlying data distribution, allowing them to generate multiple valid
estimates for a given input. This ability enhances their performance on
non-intrusive metrics \[[8](#bib.bib8)\], making them valuable for
real-world applications. Following the recent success in various
domains, diffusion models or score-based generative models
(SGMs) \[[9](#bib.bib9)\], have been proposed for speech
enhancement \[[1](#bib.bib1), [2](#bib.bib2), [3](#bib.bib3)\].
Diffusion models aim to learn the reverse process that transports the
tractable prior distribution, typically Gaussian, into the data
distribution. To better address the speech enhancement task,
\[[1](#bib.bib1), [3](#bib.bib3)\] proposed conditional diffusion
models, in which the reverse process begins with a mixture of noisy
speech and Gaussian noise. However, these models suffer from a mismatch
in the mean of the prior. To resolve this, the Schrödinger Bridge (SB)
framework \[[10](#bib.bib10), [11](#bib.bib11), [12](#bib.bib12),
[13](#bib.bib13)\], whose goal is to find the path measure between one
probability distribution and another, has been introduced as a natural
solution. It enables the generative process to start directly from the
noisy input \[[4](#bib.bib4), [5](#bib.bib5), [6](#bib.bib6)\]. However,
solving the general SB requires executing costly iterative procedures
for simulating stochastic processes \[[11](#bib.bib11),
[12](#bib.bib12), [13](#bib.bib13)\], which limits its scalability and
applicability. On the other hand, in the special case where paired data
is available, SB can be solved using a simulation-free
approach \[[14](#bib.bib14)\]. Several studies have built on this,
exploring its incorporation into speech-related tasks, such as
text-to-speech \[[15](#bib.bib15)\] and audio
super-resolution \[[16](#bib.bib16)\]. Furthermore, recent studies have
demonstrated that SB outperforms diffusion models in speech enhancement
for both wide-band \[[4](#bib.bib4), [5](#bib.bib5), [6](#bib.bib6)\]
and full-band \[[17](#bib.bib17)\] scenarios.

Diffusion models and the SB approach achieve high-quality results but
suffer from slow inference speeds. This is due to the need for
discretizing time into small step size to formulate each step of the
reverse process as a Gaussian form. On the other hand,
\[[4](#bib.bib4)\] reported that the SB model is robust to the number of
steps used in the reverse process for speech enhancement on wide-band
data. However, our experiments reveal that this robustness does not
always hold for full-band data, where a large number of iterations is
still required, particularly in low-SNR cases. Meanwhile, studies such
as \[[18](#bib.bib18), [19](#bib.bib19), [20](#bib.bib20)\] have
explored combining Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) with diffusion
models, achieving both high-quality outputs and reduced sampling steps
through adversarial training to match the denoising distribution.
Building on these advancements, we propose a novel approach that
integrates the distribution matching GANs into the SB framework for
speech enhancement. Our model not only avoids the prior mismatch, just
like the original SB models, but also enables inference with as few as
one to four steps. We evaluated our approach on full-band datasets,
specifically using EARS-WHAM for denoising and EARS-Reverb for
dereverberation \[[21](#bib.bib21)\]. Experimental results demonstrate
that our model outperforms $`50`$-step SB model with only a single-step
inference of our model. Notably, our model achieves a large margin of
improvement over the SB baselines under low-SNR conditions. Audio
samples are available at
[https://bit.ly/4i06Pgm](https://bit.ly/4i06Pgm).

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Schrödinger Bridge for Speech Enhancement

The Schrödinger Bridge (SB) problem \[[10](#bib.bib10)\] considers the
optimization of path measures with boundary conditions:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\min_{\mathcal{Q}\in\mathcal{P}_{[p_{0},p_{T}]}}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mathcal{Q}||\mathcal{P}).
``` |  | (1) |

Here, $`\mathcal{P}`$ denotes a reference measure, which is generally
set to the path measure of the forward stochastic differential equation
(SDE):
$`d\mathbf{x}_{t}=f(t,\mathbf{x}_{t})dt+g(t)d\mathbf{w}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{0}\sim p_{0}`$
as SGMs \[[9](#bib.bib9)\]. The drift term is denoted by $`f`$, the
diffusion term by $`g`$, and $`\mathcal{P}_{[p_{0},p_{T}]}`$ is a set of
path measures with marginal densities $`p_{0}`$ and $`p_{T}`$ at $`t=0`$
and $`t=T`$, respectively. Then, the solution to the
Eq. [1](#S2.E1 "In 2.1 Schrödinger Bridge for Speech Enhancement ‣ 2 Background ‣ Few-step Adversarial Schrödinger Bridge for Generative Speech Enhancement")
can be expressed by the path measure of a pair of forward and reverse
SDEs \[[12](#bib.bib12)\]:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  |  |  |  |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle d\mathbf{x}_{t}`$ | $`\displaystyle=[f(t,\mathbf{x}_{t})+g^{2}(t)\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\log\Psi(t,\mathbf{x}_{t})]dt+g(t)\,d\mathbf{w}_{t}`$ |  | (2a) |
|  | $`\displaystyle d\mathbf{x}_{t}`$ | $`\displaystyle=[f(t,\mathbf{x}_{t})-g^{2}(t)\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\log\overline{\Psi}(t,\mathbf{x}_{t})]dt+g(t)\,d\overline{\mathbf{w}}_{t}`$ |  | (2b) |

where $`\mathbf{x}_{0}\sim p_{0}`$ and $`\mathbf{x}_{T}\sim p_{T}`$.
While $`f`$ and $`g`$ are the same as in the reference SDEs, the
additional nonlinear drift terms,
$`\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\log\Psi(t,\mathbf{x}_{t})`$ and
$`\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\log\overline{\Psi}(t,\mathbf{x}_{t})`$, are
introduced. The functions $`\Psi`$ and $`\overline{\Psi}`$ are
time-varying energy potentials governed by coupled partial differential
equations (PDEs):

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  |  |  |  |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t}`$ | $`\displaystyle=-\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\Psi^{\intercal}f-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}\left(g^{2}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}^{2}\Psi\right),`$ |  | (3a) |
|  | $`\displaystyle\frac{\partial\overline{\Psi}}{\partial{t}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=-\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\cdot\left(\overline{\Psi}f\right)+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}\left(g^{2}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}^{2}\overline{\Psi}\right),`$ |  | (3b) |

subject to the conditions $`\Psi_{0}\overline{\Psi}_{0}=p_{0}`$ and
$`\Psi_{T}\overline{\Psi}_{T}=p_{T}`$. The marginal of the SB state
$`\mathbf{x}_{t}`$ is then given as
$`p_{t}=\Psi_{t}\overline{\Psi}_{t}`$ \[[12](#bib.bib12)\]. SB allows
transportation between two arbitrary distributions beyond Gaussian
priors. Thus, it is natural to formulate the speech enhancement task as
an SB problem, where the goal is to transport between noisy and clean
data distributions.

While the SB problem typically has no analytic solution, closed-form
solutions exist for specific cases, such as when Gaussian boundary
conditions are employed \[[14](#bib.bib14), [15](#bib.bib15)\]. This
formulation can be adapted to avoid the prior mismatch encountered in
SGMs for speech enhancement. For that, \[[4](#bib.bib4),
[6](#bib.bib6)\] assume a linear drift
$`f(\mathbf{x}_{t})=f(t)\mathbf{x}_{t}`$ and Gaussian boundary
conditions
$`p_{0}=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{x}_{0},\epsilon_{0}^{2}\mathbf{I})`$
and
$`p_{T}=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{y},\epsilon_{T}^{2}\mathbf{I})`$
with
$`\epsilon_{T}=\exp(\smash{\int_{0}^{T}f(\tau)d\tau})\cdot\epsilon_{0}`$.
For $`\epsilon_{0}\to 0`$, $`\overline{\Psi}_{t},\Psi_{t}`$ converge to
the tractable solution between clean data $`\mathbf{x}_{0}`$ and noisy
data $`\mathbf{y}`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\overline{\Psi}_{t}=\mathcal{N}(\alpha_{t}\mathbf{x}_{0},\alpha_{t}^{2}\sigma_{t}^{2}\mathbf{I}),\quad\Psi_{t}=\mathcal{N}(\bar{\alpha}_{t}\mathbf{y},\alpha_{t}^{2}\bar{\sigma}_{t}^{2}\mathbf{I})
``` |  | (4) |

where the parameters are given as
$`\alpha_{t}=\exp\smash{(\int_{0}^{t}f(\tau)d\tau}),\sigma_{t}=\smash{\int_{0}^{t}{g^{2}(\tau)}/{\alpha_{\tau}^{2}}d\tau},\bar{\alpha}_{t}=\alpha_{t}\alpha_{T}^{-1}`$
and $`\bar{\sigma}_{t}^{2}=\sigma_{T}^{2}-\sigma_{t}^{2}`$. Therefore,
the marginal distributions of $`\mathbf{x}_{t}`$ can be derived, and
they follow Gaussian distributions, given as:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
q(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{y})=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_{t};w_{\mathbf{x}}(t)\mathbf{x}_{0}+w_{\mathbf{y}}(t)\mathbf{y},\sigma_{x}^{2}(t)\mathbf{I}),
``` |  | (5) |

where the parameters are defined as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\sigma_{x}^{2}(t)=\frac{\alpha_{t}^{2}\bar{\sigma}_{t}^{2}\sigma_{t}^{2}}{\sigma_{T}^{2}},\quad w_{\mathbf{x}}(t)=\frac{\alpha_{t}\bar{\sigma}_{t}^{2}}{\sigma_{T}^{2}},\quad w_{\mathbf{y}}(t)=\frac{\bar{\alpha}_{t}\sigma_{t}^{2}}{\sigma_{T}^{2}}.
``` |  | (6) |

Here, $`w_{\mathbf{x}}(t)`$ and $`w_{\mathbf{y}}(t)`$ are the weights
for $`\mathbf{x}_{0}`$ and $`y`$, respectively, while
$`\sigma_{x}^{2}(t)`$ is the variance of the Gaussian distribution at
time $`t`$. Based on this design, the intermediate step can be sampled
directly, and the same denoising objective as in the diffusion model can
be applied. Moreover, the inference process starts with a noisy sample
and iteratively follows the reverse SDE or the probability flow ordinary
differential equation (ODE) to produce a clean speech
estimate \[[4](#bib.bib4), [6](#bib.bib6)\].

### 2.2 Diffusion-GAN Hybrids

Diffusion models require small step sizes, resulting in a large number
of iterations to maintain the Gaussian assumption on the denoising
distribution. In contrast, when the denoising step is large and the data
distribution is non-Gaussian, the Gaussian assumption may not hold,
resulting in a more complex distribution. To address this,
DDGAN \[[18](#bib.bib18)\] was the first to propose combining
adversarial training with diffusion models, enabling denoising with
larger discretization sizes and reducing the number of sampling steps.
Continuous processes are discretized by selecting intermediate time
points as $`t_{n}=n/N`$ with fixed $`N`$, $`t_{0}=\epsilon`$ and
$`t_{N}=T=1`$. They leverage conditional GANs to optimize the divergence
between the complex denoising distribution
$`q(\mathbf{x}_{t_{n-1}}|\mathbf{x}_{t_{n}})`$ with
$`p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{t_{n-1}}|\mathbf{x}_{t_{n}})`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\min_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{q(\mathbf{x}_{t_{n}})}[D_{\mathrm{adv}}(q(\mathbf{x}_{t_{n-1}}|\mathbf{x}_{t_{n}})||p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{t_{n-1}}|\mathbf{x}_{t_{n}}))].
``` |  | (7) |

On the other hand, SIDDM \[[19](#bib.bib19)\] has been introduced to
address scalability issues observed with DDGAN on large-scale datasets.
The objective in
Eq. [7](#S2.E7 "In 2.2 Diffusion-GAN Hybrids ‣ 2 Background ‣ Few-step Adversarial Schrödinger Bridge for Generative Speech Enhancement")
can be reformulated as matching the joint distributions
$`q(\mathbf{x}_{t_{n-1}},\mathbf{x}_{t_{n}})`$ and
$`p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{t_{n-1}},\mathbf{x}_{t_{n}})`$, since
DDGAN indirectly aligns the conditional distributions
$`q(\mathbf{x}_{t_{n-1}}|\mathbf{x}_{t_{n}})`$ and
$`p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{t_{n-1}}|\mathbf{x}_{t_{n}})`$ through
simple concatenation. Furthermore, the joint distributions can be
factorized in the reverse direction as
$`q(\mathbf{x}_{t_{n-1}},\mathbf{x}_{t_{n}})=q(\mathbf{x}_{t_{n}}|\mathbf{x}_{t_{n-1}})\,q(\mathbf{x}_{t_{n-1}})`$
and
$`p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{t_{n-1}},\mathbf{x}_{t_{n}})=p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t_{n}}|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{t_{n-1}})\,p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{t_{n-1}}).`$
Thus, joint distribution matching can be formulated as a combination of
matching marginal distributions using adversarial divergence and
matching conditional distributions using KL divergence:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\min_{\theta}`$ | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{q(\mathbf{x}_{t_{n}})}[D_{\mathrm{adv}}(q(\mathbf{x}_{t_{n-1}})|p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{t_{n-1}}))`$ |  | (8) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle+\lambda_{\mathrm{KL}}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t_{n}}|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{t_{n-1}})||q(\mathbf{x}_{t_{n}}|\mathbf{x}_{t_{n-1}}))].`$ |  |  |

This factorization enables improved performance on complex data
distributions. Unlike the loss of DDGAN in
Eq. [7](#S2.E7 "In 2.2 Diffusion-GAN Hybrids ‣ 2 Background ‣ Few-step Adversarial Schrödinger Bridge for Generative Speech Enhancement"),
where the discriminator incorporates $`\mathbf{x}_{t_{n}}`$, this
approach excludes $`\mathbf{x}_{t_{n}}`$ for the discriminator’s input.

In Diffusion-GAN hybrids, the generator produces a sample of
$`\mathbf{x}_{t_{n-1}}`$. Instead of directly producing
$`\mathbf{x}_{t_{n-1}}`$ from the generator $`G_{\theta}`$, DDGAN and
SIDDM parameterize
$`p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{t_{n-1}}|\mathbf{x}_{t_{n}})`$ with
posterior sampling of
$`q(\mathbf{x}_{t_{n-1}}|\mathbf{x}_{t_{n}},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{0}=G_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t_{n}},t_{n}))`$.
Moreover, a modified parameterization is proposed by
UFOGen \[[20](#bib.bib20)\], where
$`p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{t_{n-1}})=q(\mathbf{x}_{t_{n-1}}|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{0}=G_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t_{n}},t_{n}))`$,
based on the forward process instead of the posterior. Starting with the
objective of SIDDM in
Eq. [8](#S2.E8 "In 2.2 Diffusion-GAN Hybrids ‣ 2 Background ‣ Few-step Adversarial Schrödinger Bridge for Generative Speech Enhancement"),
this parameterization enables the model to be optimized, incorporating a
simple reconstruction loss at $`\mathbf{x}_{0}`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\min_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{q(\mathbf{x}_{t_{n}})}[D_{\mathrm{adv}}(q(\mathbf{x}_{t_{n-1}})|p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{t_{n-1}}))+\lambda_{\mathrm{KL}}||\mathbf{x}_{0}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x}_{0}||^{2}].`$ |  | (9) |

They hypothesize that SIDDM struggles with single-step generation due to
the noise variance when sampling $`\mathbf{x}_{t-1}`$ with
$`\mathbf{x}_{0}`$, whereas UFOGen enables successful one-step sampling
by directly matching $`\mathbf{x}_{0}`$ in the reconstruction term. Note
that the final loss can also be interpreted as training a diffusion
model with adversarial refinement.

## 3 Methods

### 3.1 Adversarial Schrödinger Bridge

We combine the Schrödinger Bridge (SB) for speech enhancement with GANs.
Since the SB is defined by a pair of SDE, it inherently satisfies the
Markov property. Thus, the discrete version is obtained by discretizing
the continuous process and then adapting it into the Diffusion-GAN
Hybrids, in the same way as the usual SGMs. Furthermore, since noisy
data $`\mathbf{y}`$ can serve as a condition in the speech enhancement
task, the reverse process can be formulated with this condition at each
step. In this case, the true denoising distribution
$`q(\mathbf{x}_{t_{n-1}}|\mathbf{x}_{t_{n}},\mathbf{y})`$ is matched
with
$`p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{t_{n-1}}|\mathbf{x}_{t_{n}},\mathbf{y})`$,
where each distribution is conditioned on $`\mathbf{y}`$. UFOGen can be
naturally extended to incorporate condition, ensuring that the noisy
input is consistently integrated into the model for speech enhancement.
The conditional transition probability is also Gaussian and is expressed
as:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
q(\mathbf{x}_{t_{n}}|\mathbf{x}_{t_{n-1}},\mathbf{y})=\mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{x}_{t_{n}};\bm{\mu}_{t_{n}|t_{n-1}},\sigma_{t_{n}|t_{n-1}}^{2}\mathbf{I}\right),
``` |  | (10) |

where the mean $`\bm{\mu}_{t_{n}|t_{n-1}}`$ and variance
$`\sigma_{t_{n}|t_{n-1}}^{2}`$ are given by:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  |  |  |  |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle\bm{\mu}_{t_{n}|t_{n-1}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{w_{\mathbf{x}}(t_{n})\mathbf{x}_{t_{n-1}}}{w_{\mathbf{x}}(t_{n-1})}+\bigg(w_{\mathbf{y}}(t_{n})-\frac{w_{\mathbf{x}}(t_{n})w_{\mathbf{y}}(t_{n-1})}{w_{\mathbf{x}}(t_{n-1})}\bigg)\mathbf{y},`$ |  | (11a) |
|  | $`\displaystyle\sigma_{t_{n}|t_{n-1}}^{2}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\sigma_{t_{n}}^{2}-\left(\frac{w_{\mathbf{x}}(t_{n})}{w_{\mathbf{x}}(t_{n-1})}\right)^{2}\sigma_{t_{n-1}}^{2}`$ |  | (11b) |

based on the mean and variance defined at each step in
Eq. [6](#S2.E6 "In 2.1 Schrödinger Bridge for Speech Enhancement ‣ 2 Background ‣ Few-step Adversarial Schrödinger Bridge for Generative Speech Enhancement").
The training process of our model is outlined in
Algorithm [1](#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ 3.1 Adversarial Schrödinger Bridge ‣ 3 Methods ‣ Few-step Adversarial Schrödinger Bridge for Generative Speech Enhancement")
following the UFOGen scheme. The model includes a generator
$`G_{\theta}`$ and a discriminator $`D_{\phi}`$, conditioned on noisy
data $`\mathbf{y}`$, and trained within an adversarial framework. First,
at a given time $`t_{n}`$, sample $`\mathbf{x}_{t_{n-1}}`$ using the
marginal distribution in
Eq. [5](#S2.E5 "In 2.1 Schrödinger Bridge for Speech Enhancement ‣ 2 Background ‣ Few-step Adversarial Schrödinger Bridge for Generative Speech Enhancement")
and then sample $`\mathbf{x}_{t_{n}}`$ using the conditional transition
probability in
Eq. [10](#S3.E10 "In 3.1 Adversarial Schrödinger Bridge ‣ 3 Methods ‣ Few-step Adversarial Schrödinger Bridge for Generative Speech Enhancement").
Then, the generator produces a denoised estimate
$`\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{0}`$ and sample $`\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{t_{n-1}}`$
through $`q(\mathbf{x}_{t_{n-1}}|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{0},\mathbf{y})`$.
The discriminator and generator are updated using an adversarial loss to
match intermediate data at time $`t_{n-1}`$ and a reconstruction loss.
Instead of relying solely on the $`l_{2}`$-norm, we incorporate an
auxiliary loss for the reconstruction loss, as done in \[[4](#bib.bib4),
[6](#bib.bib6)\]. Note that this framework can be applied to other
diffusion processes that can be defined as a Markov chain, such as the
Ornstein-Uhlenbeck Variance Exploding (OUVE) process \[[3](#bib.bib3)\].
While Adversarial Schrödinger Bridge Matching
(ASBM) \[[22](#bib.bib22)\] requires an explicit procedure for solving
the Schrödinger Bridge problem, our model does not, as it relies on a
simulation-free framework.

1: Generator $`G_{\theta}`$, Discriminator $`D_{\phi}`$

2: repeat

3:
$`\mathbf{x}_{0}\sim q(\mathbf{x}_{0}),n\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(1,\ldots,N),t_{n}=n/N`$.

4:
$`\mathbf{x}_{t_{n-1}}\sim q(\mathbf{x}_{t_{n-1}}|\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{y})`$,
$`\mathbf{x}_{t_{n}}\sim q(\mathbf{x}_{t_{n}}|\mathbf{x}_{t_{n-1}},\mathbf{y})`$.

5:
$`\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{t_{n-1}}\sim q(\mathbf{x}_{t_{n-1}}|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{0},\mathbf{y})`$
where
$`\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{0}=G_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t_{n}},\mathbf{y},t_{n})`$.

6: Update $`D_{\phi}`$ with gradient:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\begin{split}\nabla_{\phi}\Bigl(-\log(D_{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{t_{n-1}},&\mathbf{y},t_{n-1}))\\
-&\log(1-D_{\phi}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{t_{n-1}},\mathbf{y},t_{n-1}))\Bigr).\end{split}
``` |  |

7: Update $`G_{\theta}`$ with gradient:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\nabla_{\theta}\left(-\log(D_{\phi}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{t_{n-1}},\mathbf{y},t_{n-1}))+\lambda_{\mathrm{recon}}L_{\mathrm{recon}}\right).
``` |  |

8: until converged

### 3.2 Implementation Details

For the noise schedule, the Variance Exploding (VE) schedule is chosen,
following \[[4](#bib.bib4), [6](#bib.bib6)\], given by
$`g^{2}(t)=ck^{2t}`$ and $`f(t)=0`$. This results in $`\alpha_{t}=1`$
and $`\sigma^{2}_{t}=c(k^{2t}-1)/2\log k`$. The recommended
hyperparameters from \[[4](#bib.bib4)\] are employed for the
parameterization of the diffusion process. We select $`N=4`$ for the
discretization, as \[[18](#bib.bib18), [19](#bib.bib19),
[20](#bib.bib20)\] reported it to yield the best results.

The generator is based on the Noise Conditional Score Network (NCSN++)
architecture. It utilizes power-compressed complex spectrograms as the
input representation and uses the same model hyperparameters and STFT
parameterization as in \[[21](#bib.bib21)\], consistent with the
baseline models.

We employ a multi-scale short-time Fourier transform (MS-STFT)
discriminator with $`5`$ different scales \[[23](#bib.bib23)\]. The FFT
sizes and window lengths are set to $`[4096,2048,1024,512,256]`$, and
their hop lengths are given as $`[1024,512,256,128,64]`$. We follow
\[[24](#bib.bib24)\] in applying the diffusion process in the time
domain, instead of the non-linear spectrogram domain used in
\[[3](#bib.bib3), [4](#bib.bib4), [5](#bib.bib5), [6](#bib.bib6)\], so
our discriminator operates on white Gaussian noise.

For denoising, we use the same reconstruction loss as in
\[[4](#bib.bib4), [6](#bib.bib6), [17](#bib.bib17)\], given by
$`L_{\mathrm{recon}}=||\mathbf{X}_{0}^{\prime}-\mathbf{X}_{0}||_{2}^{2}+\alpha||\mathbf{x}_{0}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x}_{0}||_{1}`$,
where $`\textbf{X}_{0}^{\prime}`$ is the estimated power-compressed
complex spectrogram output by the generator, and
$`\mathbf{x}_{0}^{\prime}`$ is the corresponding time-domain signal
obtained through inverse STFT. $`\mathbf{X}_{0}`$ and $`\mathbf{x}_{0}`$
are the corresponding targets. For dereverberation, since the model does
not train effectively with simple losses alone, we additionally
incorporate the multi-scale mel loss, following the setup in
\[[25](#bib.bib25)\]. The weighting coefficients for reconstruction loss
are set as follows: $`\alpha=10^{-3}`$, $`0.01`$ for the additional
multi-scale mel loss for dereverberation, and
$`\lambda_{\mathrm{recon}}=100`$.

## 4 Evaluation

### 4.1 Experimental Setup

We trained our model with a batch size of 8, and the optimizer was
AdamW \[[26](#bib.bib26)\] with a learning rate of $`10^{-4}`$. We use
the exponential moving average (EMA) of the weights with a decay of
0.999 \[[27](#bib.bib27)\]. The average SI-SDR of 20 randomly selected
validation examples is logged during training, and the best-performing
model is chosen for evaluation. All models are trained on 4 NVIDIA RTX
4090 or 2 A6000 GPUs.

### 4.2 Denoising Results

We trained and tested the models on the full-band EARS-WHAM
dataset \[[21](#bib.bib21)\] for the denoising task. We used the ODE
sampler as it has demonstrated better performance for the denoising
task \[[4](#bib.bib4)\]. Results were evaluated using intrusive metrics:
Perceptual Evaluation of Speech Quality (PESQ) \[[28](#bib.bib28)\],
Scale-Invariant Signal-to-Distortion Ratio
(SI-SDR) \[[29](#bib.bib29)\], and Extended Short-Time Objective
Intelligibility (ESTOI) \[[30](#bib.bib30)\]. Additionally, we report
non-intrusive metrics: DNSMOS \[[31](#bib.bib31)\] and

## 5 Conclusion

Diffusion models have gained attention in speech enhancement for their
generative capabilities, while SB models have recently emerged as a
promising alternative. However, both approaches are still constrained by
the iterative reverse process. Our investigation reveals that SB models
suffer notable performance decline, particularly under low-SNR
conditions. To address this, we combine SB and GAN models, achieving
better performance than the baselines with single-step inference for
both denoising and dereverberation. Notably, single-step inference, as a
direct predictive mapping, yields higher scores on intrusive metrics
than 4-step inference. In future work, similar studies can be conducted
under more severe conditions and a wider range of degradations.
Moreover, our model can be extended to handle a broader range of
degradations, including super-resolution \[[37](#bib.bib37)\] and
universal speech enhancement \[[2](#bib.bib2)\].
````
