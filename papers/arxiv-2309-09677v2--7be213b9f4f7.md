# Single and Few-step Diffusion for Generative Speech Enhancement

Bunlong Lay    Jean-Marie Lermercier    Julius Richter ^(†)^(†)thanks:
This work has been funded by the German Research Foundation (DFG) in the
transregio project Crossmodal Learning (TRR 169).    Timo Gerkmann

###### Abstract

Diffusion models have shown promising results in single-channel speech
enhancement, using a task-adapted diffusion process for the conditional
generation of clean speech given a noisy mixture. However, at test time,
the neural network used for score estimation is called multiple times to
solve the iterative reverse process. This results in a slow inference
process and causes discretization errors that accumulate over the
sampling trajectory. In this paper, we address these limitations through
a two-stage training approach. In the first stage, we train the
diffusion model the usual way using the generative denoising score
matching loss. In the second stage, we compute the enhanced signal by
solving the reverse process and compare the resulting estimate to the
clean speech target using a predictive loss. We show that using this
second training stage enables achieving the same performance as the
baseline model using only 5 function evaluations instead of 60 function
evaluations. While the performance of usual generative diffusion
algorithms drops dramatically when lowering the number of function
evaluations to obtain single-step diffusion, we show that our proposed
method keeps a steady performance and therefore largely outperforms the
diffusion baseline in this setting and also generalizes better than its
predictive counterpart¹¹ 1 Find code and audio examples:
[https://github.com/sp-uhh/sgmse_crp](https://github.com/sp-uhh/sgmse_crp).

## 1 Introduction

The objective of speech enhancement (SE) is to retrieve the original
clean speech signal from a noisy mixture that is affected by
environmental noise \[[1](#bib.bib1)\]. Traditional approaches attempt
to leverage statistical relationships between the clean speech signal
and the surrounding environmental noise \[[2](#bib.bib2)\]. In recent
years, various machine learning techniques have been introduced,
treating SE as a discriminative learning task \[[3](#bib.bib3),
[4](#bib.bib4)\] resulting in so-called _predictive_ approaches.

In contrast to predictive approaches that learn a direct mapping from
noisy to clean speech, generative approaches learn a prior distribution
over clean speech data. Recently, so-called _score-based generative
models_, also known as _diffusion models_ are introduced for SE
\[[5](#bib.bib5), [6](#bib.bib6), [7](#bib.bib7), [8](#bib.bib8)\]. The
fundamental concept behind these models involves iteratively adding
Gaussian noise to the data using a discrete and fixed Markov chain known
as the _forward process_, thereby transforming data into a tractable
distribution such as the standard normal distribution. Then, a neural
network is trained to invert this diffusion process in a so-called
_reverse process_ \[[9](#bib.bib9)\]. By letting the step size between
two discrete Markov chain states approach zero, the discrete Markov
chain becomes a continuous-time stochastic process that can be described
using [sde](#id9). This offers more flexibility and opportunities than
approaches based on discrete Markov chains \[[10](#bib.bib10)\]. For
example, it allows the use of general-purpose [sde](#id9) solvers to
numerically integrate the reverse process, which can affect the
performance and number of iteration steps.

Solving the reverse process, also called reverse SDE when employing
SDEs, means enhancing noisy mixtures as the reverse SDE transforms the
distribution of noisy speech into the distribution of clean speech. To
solve the reverse SDE at inference, it is essential to evaluate the
score function of the perturbed data, which is typically intractable.
Thus, a neural network, also called _score model_, is trained to
approximate the required score function. The score model is typically
trained using the [dsm](#id35) ([dsm](#id35)) loss \[[11](#bib.bib11)\],
which has shown great success for SE \[[8](#bib.bib8), [12](#bib.bib12),
[13](#bib.bib13)\]. However, solving the reverse SDE differs from the
training condition in various aspects. First, there exists the so-called
prior mismatch \[[13](#bib.bib13)\], which we define as the difference
between the terminating distribution of the forward process and the
initial distribution used to solve the reverse SDE. Second, the reverse
process introduces a discretization error when using a numerical solver.
This error can be mitigated by using more sophisticated solvers, at the
cost of increased [nfe](#id30) ([nfe](#id30)). Last, the score model
itself is not a perfect estimator of the score function and produces
errors. As the aforementioned errors accumulate over the reverse
trajectory, the obtained diffusion states can deviate from the
distribution seen during training, which further impairs the score model
performance.

This work aims to mitigate these errors by employing a two-stage
training. We first train on the [dsm](#id35) objective. In the second
stage we fine-tune the score model with a predictive loss to correct the
discretization and score model errors. To do so, we pick a reverse
diffusion solver, e.g. the [em](#id32) ([em](#id32)) first-order method
in our case, to obtain a clean speech estimate. Then, we fine-tune the
score model parameters with respect to the mean squared error (MSE) loss
between the estimate and the clean speech data. Backpropagating through
the whole reverse trajectory is computationally infeasible when using
many steps. Therefore, we propose here to only use the gradients
accumulated during the last reverse diffusion step to update the
parameters.

We compare our approach against (a) our generative baseline BBED
\[[13](#bib.bib13)\], (b) a predictive baseline using a similar network
architecture as used for score estimation, as well as (c) the StoRM
approach \[[12](#bib.bib12)\], which also combines a generative loss and
a predictive loss. We show that the proposed method is very robust to a
decrease in the NFEs used for reverse diffusion as opposed to the purely
generative approach and StoRM \[[12](#bib.bib12)\]. In fact, with only 5
NFEs the proposed method performs virtually the same as the generative
baseline with 60 NFEs. Furthermore, when taking only one reverse
diffusion step, we achieve competitive SE performance and we show that
the proposed generative approach generalizes better to unseen data than
the predictive baseline.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Notation and data representation

The task of SE is to estimate the clean speech signal $`\mathbf{S}`$
from a noisy mixture $`\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{S}+\mathbf{N}`$, where
$`\mathbf{N}`$ is environmental noise. All variables in bold are the
coefficients of a compressed complex-valued spectrogram obtained through
[stft](#id7) ([stft](#id7)) and magnitude-compression, e.g.
$`\mathbf{Y}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}`$ and $`d=KF`$ with $`K`$ number of
[stft](#id7) frames and $`F`$ number of frequency bins. As in
\[[8](#bib.bib8)\], we use the magnitude compression
$`\bar{c}=\beta|c|^{\alpha}\mathrm{e}^{i\angle(c)}`$ with parameters
$`\beta,\alpha>0`$ to compensate for the typically heavy-tailed
distribution of [stft](#id7) speech magnitudes \[[14](#bib.bib14)\]. We
set $`\beta=0.15,\alpha=0.5`$ as in previous work \[[8](#bib.bib8),
[13](#bib.bib13), [12](#bib.bib12)\].

### 2.2 Stochastic Differential Equations

Following \[[7](#bib.bib7), [8](#bib.bib8), [13](#bib.bib13)\], we model
the forward process of the score-based generative model with an
[sde](#id9) defined on $`0\leq t<T_{\text{max}}`$:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{\mathbf{X}}_{t}}=\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{\mathbf{X}}_{t},\mathbf{\mathbf{Y}})\mathrm{d}{t}+g(t)\mathrm{d}{{\mathbf{w}}},
``` |  | (1) |

where $`\mathbf{w}`$ is the standard Wiener process
\[[15](#bib.bib15)\], $`\mathbf{\mathbf{X}}_{t}`$ is the current process
state with initial condition
$`\mathbf{\mathbf{X}}_{0}=\mathbf{\mathbf{S}}`$, and $`t`$ a continuous
diffusion time variable describing the progress of the process ending at
the last diffusion time $`T_{\text{max}}`$. The drift term
$`\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{\mathbf{X}}_{t},\mathbf{\mathbf{Y}})\mathrm{d}{t}`$
can be integrated by Lebesgue integration \[[16](#bib.bib16)\], and the
diffusion term $`g(t)\mathrm{d}{{\mathbf{w}}}`$ follows Ito integration
\[[15](#bib.bib15)\]. The diffusion coefficient $`g`$ regulates the
amount of Gaussian noise that is added to the process, and the drift
$`\mathbf{f}`$ affects the mean and the variance of
$`\mathbf{\mathbf{X}}_{t}`$ in the case of linear SDEs (see
\[[15](#bib.bib15), Eq. (6.10), (6.11)\]). The process state
$`\mathbf{\mathbf{X}}_{t}`$ follows a Gaussian distribution
\[[15](#bib.bib15), Section 5\] called *perturbation kernel*:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
p_{0t}(\mathbf{\mathbf{X}}_{t}|\mathbf{\mathbf{X}}_{0},\mathbf{\mathbf{Y}})=\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}\left(\mathbf{\mathbf{X}}_{t};\boldsymbol{\mu}(\mathbf{\mathbf{X}}_{0},\mathbf{\mathbf{Y}},t),\sigma(t)^{2}\mathbf{I}\right).
``` |  | (2) |

Under mild regularity conditions, each forward SDE
([1](#S2.E1 "In 2.2 Stochastic Differential Equations ‣ 2 Background ‣ Single and Few-step Diffusion for Generative Speech Enhancement"))
can be associated to a reverse SDE \[[17](#bib.bib17)\]:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}}_{t}}=\left[-\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}}_{t},\mathbf{\mathbf{Y}})+g(t)^{2}\mathbf{\nabla}_{\mathbf{\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}}_{t}}\log p_{t}(\mathbf{\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}}_{t}|\mathbf{\mathbf{Y}})\right]\mathrm{d}{t}+g(t)\mathrm{d}{\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}}\,,
``` |  | (3) |

where $`\mathrm{d}{\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}}`$ is a Wiener process going
backward through the diffusion time. In particular, the reverse process
starts at $`t=T`$ and ends at $`t=0`$. Here $`T<T_{\text{max}}`$ is a
parameter that needs to be set for practical reasons, as the last
diffusion time $`T_{\text{max}}`$ is only reached in limit. The *score
function*
$`\nabla_{\mathbf{X}_{t}}\log p_{t}(\mathbf{\mathbf{X}}_{t}|\mathbf{\mathbf{Y}})`$
is approximated by a neural network called *score model*
$`\mathbf{s}_{\theta}(\mathbf{\mathbf{X}}_{t},\mathbf{\mathbf{Y}},t)`$,
which is parameterized by a set of parameters $`\theta`$. Assuming
$`\mathbf{s}_{\theta}`$ is available, we generate an estimate of clean
speech data from $`\mathbf{\mathbf{Y}}`$ by solving the reverse SDE.

### 2.3 Solving the reverse process

To solve the reverse SDE the first-order EuM method can be employed to
keep the [nfe](#id30) low. For the [em](#id32) method, a discretization
schedule $`(t_{N},t_{N-1},\dots,t_{0}=0)`$ for the reverse process is
chosen. The reverse process that is used for inference starts with
$`\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{t_{N}}\sim\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbf{\mathbf{Y}},\sigma({t_{N}})^{2}\mathbf{I})`$
and iteratively computes \[[10](#bib.bib10)\]

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{t_{i}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{{t_{i+1}}}-\left[\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}}_{{t_{i+1}}},\mathbf{\mathbf{Y}})-g(t_{i})^{2}\mathbf{s}_{\theta}(\mathbf{\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}}_{{t_{i+1}}},\mathbf{\mathbf{Y}},t_{i})\right]\Delta t(i)`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle+g(t_{i})\sqrt{\Delta t(i)}\,\mathbf{Z}_{t_{i}},`$ |  | (4) |

where $`\Delta t(i)={{t_{i+1}}}-{{t_{i}}}`$ and
$`\mathbf{Z}_{t_{i}}\sim\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})`$.
When fixing the step size $`\Delta t(i)`$, the reverse starting point
$`0<t_{\text{rsp}}\coloneqq t_{N}\leq T`$ can be used for trading
performance for computational speed. For optimal performance, one should
set $`t_{\text{rsp}}=T`$ to reduce the so-called prior mismatch
\[[13](#bib.bib13)\] between the terminating forward distribution and
the initial reverse distribution. Using a smaller $`t_{\text{rsp}}`$
reduces the number of iterations, but may also degrade the performance
\[[13](#bib.bib13)\]. The last iteration outputs
$`\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{t_{0}}`$ approximating the clean speech
signal.

### 2.4 Brownian Bridge with Exponential Diffusion Coefficient (BBED)

Recently, the BBED SDE \[[13](#bib.bib13)\] has been shown to outperform
existing SDEs \[[7](#bib.bib7), [8](#bib.bib8)\] for the task of SE in
terms of several intrusive instrumental performance metrics. The BBED
drift and diffusion coefficients are given by:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{\mathbf{X}}_{t},\mathbf{\mathbf{Y}})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbf{\mathbf{Y}}-\mathbf{\mathbf{X}}_{t}}{1-t},`$ |  | (5) |

and

|     |                                                    |     |     |
|-----|----------------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       g(t)=ck^{t},\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{where }c,k>0
       ```                                                 |     | (6) |

for $`0\leq t\leq T<1`$, where $`T`$, as detailed in Section
[2.2](#S2.SS2 "2.2 Stochastic Differential Equations ‣ 2 Background ‣ Single and Few-step Diffusion for Generative Speech Enhancement"),
is the terminating time for the forward SDE, which we set slightly
smaller than $`1`$ to avoid division by zero in
([5](#S2.E5 "In 2.4 Brownian Bridge with Exponential Diffusion Coefficient (BBED) ‣ 2 Background ‣ Single and Few-step Diffusion for Generative Speech Enhancement")).
The closed-form solution for the variance of the perturbation kernel can
be computed from \[[15](#bib.bib15), (6.11)\]:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\hskip-6.49994pt\sigma(t)^{2}`$ | $`\displaystyle=(1-t)c^{2}\left[(k^{2t}-1+t)+\log(k^{2k^{2}})(1-t)E\right],`$ |  | (7) |
|  | $`\displaystyle E`$ | $`\displaystyle=\text{Ei}\left[2(t-1)\log(k)\right]-\text{Ei}\left[-2\log(k)\right],`$ |  | (8) |

where $`\text{Ei}[\cdot]`$ denotes the exponential integral function
\[[18](#bib.bib18)\]. The variance admits one peak between $`0`$ and
$`1`$ and is zero for $`t\in\{0,1\}`$. The mean can be computed from
\[[15](#bib.bib15), (6.10)\] and linearly interpolates between the noisy
mixture $`\mathbf{Y}`$ and the clean speech signal $`\mathbf{X}_{0}`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\boldsymbol{\mu}(\mathbf{\mathbf{X}}_{0},\mathbf{\mathbf{Y}},t)=\left(1-t\right)\mathbf{\mathbf{X}}_{0}+t\mathbf{\mathbf{Y}}\,.
``` |  | (9) |

## 3 Proposed Two-Stage Training

To reduce the NFEs during inference, we now propose a two-stage training
procedure. In the first training stage we train the score model on the
[dsm](#id35) loss \[[19](#bib.bib19), [8](#bib.bib8), [7](#bib.bib7)\].
In the second training stage, we propose as a novelty to fine-tune the
score model on a predictive loss which we denote in the following as
*correcting the reverse process* (CRP) loss.

### 3.1 Denoising Score Matching (DSM)

Following Vincent \[[11](#bib.bib11)\], we fit the score model to the
score of the perturbation kernel

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\hskip-1.49994pt\nabla_{\mathbf{\mathbf{X}}_{t}}\log p_{0t}(\mathbf{\mathbf{X}}_{t}|\mathbf{\mathbf{X}}_{0},\mathbf{\mathbf{Y}})=-\frac{\mathbf{Z}}{\sigma(t)},
``` |  | (10) |

where $`\mathbf{Z}\sim\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})`$.
At each training step we proceed as follows: 1) we sample a training
pair of clean speech data and noisy speech ($`\mathbf{\mathbf{X}}_{0}`$,
$`\mathbf{\mathbf{Y}}`$), 2) we sample $`t`$ uniformly from
$`[t_{\epsilon},T]`$, where $`t_{\epsilon}>0`$ is a small hyperparameter
that assured numerical stability \[[8](#bib.bib8)\], 3) we compute
$`\mathbf{\mathbf{X}}_{t}=\boldsymbol{\mu}(\mathbf{\mathbf{X}}_{0},\mathbf{\mathbf{Y}},t)+\sigma(t)\mathbf{Z}`$.
Finally, we obtain the [dsm](#id35) loss, by minimizing the distance of
the term on the right in
([10](#S3.E10 "In 3.1 Denoising Score Matching (DSM) ‣ 3 Proposed Two-Stage Training ‣ Single and Few-step Diffusion for Generative Speech Enhancement"))
and the score model based on the $`L_{2}`$ norm:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\argmin_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{t,(\mathbf{\mathbf{X}}_{0},\mathbf{\mathbf{Y}}),\mathbf{Z},\mathbf{\mathbf{X}}_{t}|(\mathbf{\mathbf{X}}_{0},\mathbf{\mathbf{Y}})}\left[\left\lVert\mathbf{s}_{\theta}(\mathbf{\mathbf{X}}_{t},\mathbf{\mathbf{Y}},t)+\frac{\mathbf{Z}}{\sigma(t)}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right]
``` |  | (11) |

### 3.2 Correcting the Reverse Process (CRP)

By solving the reverse process as described in Section
[2.3](#S2.SS3 "2.3 Solving the reverse process ‣ 2 Background ‣ Single and Few-step Diffusion for Generative Speech Enhancement"),
we obtain an estimate $`\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{0}`$ of the clean speech
signal $`\mathbf{X}_{0}`$. This estimate, however, contains errors from
several sources. First, there is the discretization error from the
[em](#id32) method. Second, there are errors caused by the score model
over the discretization schedule. Moreover, there is the prior mismatch,
as discussed in Section
[2.3](#S2.SS3 "2.3 Solving the reverse process ‣ 2 Background ‣ Single and Few-step Diffusion for Generative Speech Enhancement").
This mismatch and the aforementioned error sources are not addressed
using the [dsm](#id35) loss in
([11](#S3.E11 "In 3.1 Denoising Score Matching (DSM) ‣ 3 Proposed Two-Stage Training ‣ Single and Few-step Diffusion for Generative Speech Enhancement")).
This is because in the third step of computing the [dsm](#id35) loss, we
calculate $`\mathbf{X}_{t}`$ by using the solution of the forward SDE
given by
([2](#S2.E2 "In 2.2 Stochastic Differential Equations ‣ 2 Background ‣ Single and Few-step Diffusion for Generative Speech Enhancement")),
but during inference, we compute the solution
$`\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{t}`$ of the reverse SDE. After training on the
[dsm](#id35) loss, we therefore propose to retrain the score model to
adapt to these errors and the prior mismatch. Specifically, we first fix
a reverse starting point $`t_{\text{rsp}}`$ and a discretization
schedule $`(t_{\text{rsp}}=t_{N},\dots,t_{0}=0)`$. Then, for each
training pair of clean speech and noisy speech
($`\mathbf{\mathbf{X}}_{0}`$, $`\mathbf{\mathbf{Y}}`$), we compute
$`\mathbf{\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}}_{0}`$ based on
([4](#S2.Ex1 "In 2.3 Solving the reverse process ‣ 2 Background ‣ Single and Few-step Diffusion for Generative Speech Enhancement"))
by starting at $`t_{\text{rsp}}`$ and taking steps according to the
fixed discretization schedule, i.e. we specifically run the inference
process.

In the second training stage, we then optimize on [crp](#id34)
([crp](#id34)) loss

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\argmin_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{\mathbf{X}}_{0},\mathbf{\mathbf{Y}}),\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{0}|(\mathbf{\mathbf{X}}_{0},\mathbf{\mathbf{Y}})}\left\lVert\mathbf{\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}}_{0}-\mathbf{\mathbf{X}}_{0}\right\rVert_{2}^{2},
``` |  | (12) |

by updating the weights based on the gradients of the last score model
call
$`\mathbf{s}_{\theta}(\mathbf{\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}}_{t_{1}},\mathbf{\mathbf{Y}},t_{1})`$
used to obtain $`\mathbf{\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}}_{0}`$. This also means
that when we iterate through
([4](#S2.Ex1 "In 2.3 Solving the reverse process ‣ 2 Background ‣ Single and Few-step Diffusion for Generative Speech Enhancement"))
the gradients of all other score model calls
$`\mathbf{s}_{\theta}(\mathbf{\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}}_{t_{k}},\mathbf{\mathbf{Y}},t_{k})`$,
$`k=N,\dots,2`$ are not used to update the weights of the neural
network. The decision to exclusively update weights in the last score
model call is driven by the fact that the accumulated discretization and
score model errors can be corrected by the last score model call while
keeping the cost of memory low.

Note that the idea of using a predictive loss for a diffusion model is
conceptually similar to cold diffusion \[[20](#bib.bib20)\]. However,
cold diffusion is not generative as it does not contain any randomness.

## 4 Experimental setup

### 4.1 Metrics

We evaluate the performance on perceptual metrics, wideband PESQ
\[[21](#bib.bib21)\] and POLQA \[[22](#bib.bib22)\], on energy-based
metrics SI-SDR \[[23](#bib.bib23)\]. We also evaluate on a
reference-free metric WVMOS using a neural network to predict MOS
values.

The NFEs is the number of score model calls for producing the enhanced
file. It is a measure of computational expenses during inference.

### 4.2 BBED parameterization

A parameterization for the BBED SDE has already been grid-searched in
\[[13](#bib.bib13)\]. We therefore simply set $`k=2.6`$, $`c=0.51`$ and
$`T=0.999`$ as it is done in \[[13](#bib.bib13)\].

For inference, we employ the predictor-corrector scheme
\[[10](#bib.bib10)\]. The predictor is the [em](#id32) method and the
corrector is the annealed Langevin Dynamics method. We start the reverse
process at $`0.999`$ and we use 30 discretization steps, resulting in 60
NFEs.

### 4.3 Training on the [dsm](#id35) loss

For the score model $`s_{\theta}(\mathbf{X}_{t},\mathbf{Y},t)`$, we
employ the Noise Conditional Score Network (NCSN++) architecture (see
\[[8](#bib.bib8), [10](#bib.bib10)\] for more details). The first
training stage follows mainly the setup from \[[13](#bib.bib13)\]. More
precisely, we first train the model on the [dsm](#id35) loss function
defined in
([11](#S3.E11 "In 3.1 Denoising Score Matching (DSM) ‣ 3 Proposed Two-Stage Training ‣ Single and Few-step Diffusion for Generative Speech Enhancement"))
with a learning rate of $`10^{-4}`$ using the ADAM optimizer with a
decay of $`10^{-6}`$. In addition, an exponential moving average (EMA)
of the weights of NCSN++ is tracked with a decay of 0.999, to be used
for sampling \[[24](#bib.bib24)\]. Moreover, a lower bound for the
diffusion step $`t`$ is set to be $`t_{\epsilon}=0.03`$ to avoid
numerical instability (see \[[8](#bib.bib8), [13](#bib.bib13)\] for
details). Furthermore, we train for 200 epochs with a batch size of 16
and logged the averaged PESQ of 10 randomly selected files from the
validation set during training and select the best-performing model for
the second training stage.

### 4.4 Training on the [crp](#id34) loss

In the second training stage, we train on
([12](#S3.E12 "In 3.2 Correcting the Reverse Process (CRP) ‣ 3 Proposed Two-Stage Training ‣ Single and Few-step Diffusion for Generative Speech Enhancement"))
with the same ADAM and exponential moving average configurations as for
the first training stage. We train on a batch size of 16 and train for
only 10 epochs. We also validate during training on 10 randomly selected
files from the validation set and chose the best-performing model
accordingly for testing.

To train on the [crp](#id34) loss in
([12](#S3.E12 "In 3.2 Correcting the Reverse Process (CRP) ‣ 3 Proposed Two-Stage Training ‣ Single and Few-step Diffusion for Generative Speech Enhancement")),
we have to choose a discretization schedule. To this end, we set the
reverse starting point $`t_{\text{rsp}}=0.5`$. This choice is based on
\[[13](#bib.bib13)\], where it has been observed that the BBED can
perform for $`t_{\text{rsp}}=0.5`$ as well as for
$`t_{\text{rsp}}=0.999`$ while reducing the [nfe](#id30). The
discretization schedule is set as follows. We take
$`n_{\text{steps}}\in\{1,2,3,4,5\}`$ discretizations steps as follows.
The first $`n_{\text{steps}}-1`$ steps are uniformly from
$`t_{\text{rsp}}`$ to $`t_{\epsilon}`$ and the last step is taken from
$`t_{\epsilon}`$ to 0. Moreover, when we take $`n_{\text{steps}}=1`$,
then we take only one step from $`t_{\text{rsp}}`$ to $`0`$ directly. We
do not use a corrector for training the [crp](#id34) loss, therefore NFE
= $`n_{\text{steps}}`$. In addition, we use the same discretization
schedule for testing as for training.

### 4.5 Baselines

Generative baseline: We call the BBED SDE parametrized as in
[4.2](#S4.SS2 "4.2 BBED parameterization ‣ 4 Experimental setup ‣ Single and Few-step Diffusion for Generative Speech Enhancement")
and trained as described in Section
[4.3](#S4.SS3 "4.3 Training on the loss ‣ 4 Experimental setup ‣ Single and Few-step Diffusion for Generative Speech Enhancement")
the generative baseline.
Predictive baseline: We investigate the benefits of the generative BBED
baseline over a predictive baseline by training the score model
architecture on a predictive loss. We, therefore, train NCSN++ on the
MSE loss between enhanced and the clean speech signal as it is done in
the second training stage in
([12](#S3.E12 "In 3.2 Correcting the Reverse Process (CRP) ‣ 3 Proposed Two-Stage Training ‣ Single and Few-step Diffusion for Generative Speech Enhancement"))
and also use the same magnitude compressed STFT representation from
Section
[2.1](#S2.SS1 "2.1 Notation and data representation ‣ 2 Background ‣ Single and Few-step Diffusion for Generative Speech Enhancement")
for the input of the predictive NCSN++ baseline. Moreover, since there
is no diffusion time embedding $`t`$ in the input for the predictive
task, we removed the noise-conditioned layers. The modified architecture
differs by less than 1 percent of its original size and therefore
changes hardly the capacity of the network. Similar to training the
[dsm](#id35) loss, we use the ADAM optimizer with a learning rate of
$`10^{-4}`$ and a decay of $`10^{-6}`$ for a maximum of 200 epochs. We
train with a batch size of 16 and log the averaged PESQ of 10 randomly
selected files from the validation set during training and select the
best-performing model for testing. We do not employ EMA for tracking the
weights.
StoRM: As the proposed method uses a generative loss and a predictive
loss, we want to compare it against StoRM \[[12](#bib.bib12)\], which is
also using the exact same generative and predictive loss. StoRM has a
predictive part and a generative part. In our setup, the predictive part
uses the predictive baseline’s NCSN++ architecture. The generative part
of StoRM is due to the generative baseline. We call this modified
version StoRM-BBED. For inference, we use the same discretization
schedule as for the proposed method described in Section
[4.4](#S4.SS4 "4.4 Training on the loss ‣ 4 Experimental setup ‣ Single and Few-step Diffusion for Generative Speech Enhancement").
Note that the predictive part must always be executed once before
solving the reverse SDE. Therefore, $`\text{NFE}=n_{\text{steps}}+1`$
for StoRM-BBED.

### 4.6 Datasets

To analyze the generalization performance of the proposed method for SE,
we cross-evaluate on two different datasets. Specifically, we train
either on the VoiceBank-DEMAND dataset (VBD) or on the WSJ0-CHiME3
(WSJ0-C3) dataset, but we test only on WSJ0-C3. We choose to test on
WSJ0-C3 over testing on VBD as the utterances of VBD have a short
average length of 2.5 seconds, which is not ideal for evaluating on PESQ
or POLQA \[[25](#bib.bib25)\].

VBD: The publicly available VBD dataset \[[26](#bib.bib26)\] is commonly
employed as a benchmark in single-channel SE tasks. Note that this
dataset does not have a validation set. To ensure unbiased validation,
we split the training data into two sets: training set and validation
set. For validation, we specifically reserved the speakers “p226” and
“p287” from the dataset. This has also been done in previous works
\[[8](#bib.bib8), [13](#bib.bib13)\].

WSJ0-C3: The WSJ0-C3 single-channel dataset that is also used in
\[[8](#bib.bib8), [13](#bib.bib13)\] mixes clean speech utterances from
the Wall Street Journal dataset \[[27](#bib.bib27)\] to noise signals
from the CHiME3 dataset \[[28](#bib.bib28)\] with a uniformly sampled
[snr](#id3) ([snr](#id3)) between 0 and 20 dB. The dataset is split into
a train (12777 files), validation (1206 files) and test set (651
files).

## 5 Results

We first show that the proposed method remains relatively stable when
lowering the NFEs. Second, we show that the proposed method with a
single NFE is not simply reduced to the predictive baseline, and third
we show that the proposed method with only 5 NFEs performs as well as
the generative baseline with 60 NFEs. Moreover, we report results that
the proposed CRP outperforms (unfolded) cold diffusion
\[[20](#bib.bib20)\] by 0.5 in PESQ when both methods are trained and
tested on VBD with one step (CRP achieves $`2.96`$ in PESQ).

First, from Fig.
[1](#S3.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 3.2 Correcting the Reverse Process (CRP) ‣ 3 Proposed Two-Stage Training ‣ Single and Few-step Diffusion for Generative Speech Enhancement")
we see the performance results of the proposed CRP, generative BBED
baseline, predictive baseline and StoRM-BBED. In all plots of Fig.
[1](#S3.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 3.2 Correcting the Reverse Process (CRP) ‣ 3 Proposed Two-Stage Training ‣ Single and Few-step Diffusion for Generative Speech Enhancement")
we clearly observe the generative baseline (blue solid line) and
StoRM-BBED (yellow solid line) also drop in performance when reducing
the NFEs to few steps. For instance, both the generative baseline and
StoRM-BBED achieve only around 1 on the MOS scale with NFE = 1 or NFE =
2 respectively. Note that a 1 on the MOS scale denotes "bad" quality
according to ITU-T P.800. This is in contrast to the proposed CRP
method, where performance remains relatively stable and achieves "good"
(POLQA and WVMOS) or "fair" (PESQ) quality (see black solid line in Fig.
[1](#S3.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 3.2 Correcting the Reverse Process (CRP) ‣ 3 Proposed Two-Stage Training ‣ Single and Few-step Diffusion for Generative Speech Enhancement")).

Second, if only one NFE is applied, the performance of the proposed
method seems to be reduced to the predictive baseline. For instance, in
the POLQA plot, we report a value of 3.87 for the proposed method at
$`\text{NFE}=1`$ and a very similar value of 3.85 for the predictive
baseline. However, the advantage of the generative approach becomes
visible in Tab.
[1](#S3.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 3.2 Correcting the Reverse Process (CRP) ‣ 3 Proposed Two-Stage Training ‣ Single and Few-step Diffusion for Generative Speech Enhancement")
where we show results for the mismatched case, i.e., we train on the VBD
training set, but test on the WSj0-C3 test set. We observe that the
proposed generative method outperforms the predictive baseline by 0.15
in PESQ also with only one NFE. These results imply that the better
generalization to unseen data observed when comparing diffusion models
against predictive models observed in \[[8](#bib.bib8),
[29](#bib.bib29)\] carries over to the proposed approach even when NFE =
1.

Third, we find that the proposed method achieves virtually the same
performance results with 5 NFEs for the matched (see Fig.
[1](#S3.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 3.2 Correcting the Reverse Process (CRP) ‣ 3 Proposed Two-Stage Training ‣ Single and Few-step Diffusion for Generative Speech Enhancement"),
compare solid black line to dotted blue line) as the generative baseline
with 60 NFEs and mismatched case (see Tab.
[1](#S3.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 3.2 Correcting the Reverse Process (CRP) ‣ 3 Proposed Two-Stage Training ‣ Single and Few-step Diffusion for Generative Speech Enhancement"),
compare first row to last row).

## 6 Conclusions

In this work, we proposed to fine-tune a score-based diffusion model by
optimizing a predictive loss to correct errors caused by solving the
reverse process. We find that the proposed method remains relatively
stable in performance when lowering the [nfe](#id30) ([nfe](#id30)). In
contrast, the pure generative approach and StoRM-BBED drastically reduce
in performance when lowering the NFEs to a few or single reverse
diffusion steps. Moreover, we showed that in contrast to a predictive
baseline, the proposed method achieves an improved generalization to
unseen data even with one diffusion step.
````
