# Are Modern Speech Enhancement Systems Vulnerable to Adversarial Attacks?

Rostislav Makarov    Lea Schönherr    Timo Gerkmann

###### Abstract

Machine learning approaches for speech enhancement are becoming
increasingly expressive, enabling ever more powerful modifications of
input signals. In this paper, we demonstrate that this expressiveness
introduces a vulnerability: advanced speech enhancement models can be
susceptible to adversarial attacks. Specifically, we show that
adversarial noise, carefully crafted and psychoacoustically masked by
the original input, can be injected such that the enhanced speech output
conveys an entirely different semantic meaning. We experimentally verify
that contemporary predictive speech enhancement models can indeed be
manipulated in this way. Furthermore, we highlight that diffusion models
with stochastic samplers exhibit inherent robustness to such adversarial
attacks by design.

###### Index Terms: 

speech enhancement, adversarial attack, diffusion model, score-based
model, psychoacoustic hiding.

## 1 Introduction

Adversarial attacks are a method where adversarial noise, designed to be
hardly perceivable by humans, is added to the data such that the output
of a dnn (dnn) is yielding a result that is controlled by the attacker
but unintended by the user. A well-known example is the addition of
adversarial noise to an image of a panda so that a classifier would
detect a gibbon instead \[1\]. In speech processing, first attempts were
made to attack asr (asr) \[2\] systems. For this, auditory masking
effects can be exploited to psychoacoustically hide the adversarial
noise in the speech commands \[3\].

While adversarial attacks are most commonly designed for classification
systems such as asr, they can also be applied to regression tasks. For
instance, one could think of a scenario where adversarial noise is added
by an attacker, such that a se (se) system of a speech communication
device such as a hearing aid or a telephony system outputs speech with a
completely different and unintended semantic meaning. However, modifying
a speech signal such that the semantic meaning is deliberately changed
by a se system has received considerably less attention, also because
traditional speech enhancement systems \[4\], e.g. based on Wiener
filtering, are hardly expressive enough to change the semantic meaning
of speech. However, now that both predictive and generative se systems
are becoming increasingly expressive, the risk of adversarial attacks
increases. It is therefore important to investigate such potential
vulnerabilities in more detail.

Therefore, in this paper, we explore the possibility of targeted
adversarial attacks on modern expressive predictive and generative se
systems to produce an output with a completely different meaning than
the original speech signal had. We consider a white-box scenario in
which the adversary has full knowledge of the enhancement model and can
compute gradients through it. We use a psychoacoustic model to make the
adversarial noise as inaudible as possible, while keeping the
intelligibility and quality of the enhanced speech as high as possible.
We draw inspiration from psychoacoustic masking attacks in asr \[3\],
extending them to the se domain and addressing new challenges that
arise, especially for diffusion-based generative models. The key
contributions of this work are as follows: (1) We propose an attack loss
function and optimization procedure applicable to both traditional and
diffusion-based se, incorporating psychoacoustic constraints. (2) We
analyze the differences in vulnerability between a standard predictive
models like a regression se, a mask-based se, and a score-based
diffusion se (SGMSE+), including the effect of stochastic sampling in
diffusion. (3) We evaluate on EARS-WHAM-v2 using two metric groups:
_attack success_: DistillMOS and wer (wer), estoi (estoi), POLQA (POLQA)
against the target speech; and _perturbation impact_: POLQA, estoi
between attacked and original mixtures, plus perturbation snr (snr).

This work raises an important and novel security issue by presenting a
methodology for performing targeted adversarial examples against se
systems.

## 2 Background

se (se) aims to recover a clean speech spectrogram $`S`$ from a noisy
observation $`Y`$. We work in the complex stft (stft) domain and adopt
the standard additive model

```math
Y\;=\;S+N,\qquad S,\,Y,\,N\in\mathbb{C}^{F\times T},
```

where $`N`$ denotes environmental noise. We consider two broad families
of se models. _Predictive_ approaches directly map $`Y\mapsto\hat{S}`$,
either by regressing the complex spectrogram \[5, 6, 7\] or by
estimating a complex mask applied to $`Y`$ \[8, 9\]. _Generative_
approaches learn a prior over clean speech and perform conditional
generation given $`Y`$; among these, diffusion (score-based) models have
recently achieved state-of-the-art performance. For architectural
consistency across comparisons, all our systems use the ncsnpp (ncsnpp)
backbone \[10\] adapted to complex inputs/outputs.

We introduce an se model, denoted as $`f_{SE}`$, which serves as a
generic operator that takes a noisy stft $`Y`$ as input and aims to
estimate the clean speech signal $`S`$, producing an output
$`\hat{S}^{Y}`$.

```math
\hat{S}^{Y}=f_{SE}(Y) \tag{1}
```

### 2.1 Predictive models

For training predictive models we use a point-wise complex-valued mse
(mse) loss to the clean target $`S`$,

```math
\mathcal{L}_{\text{reg}}\;=\;\sum_{q,n}\bigl|\hat{S}_{q,n}-S_{q,n}\bigr|^{2}.
```

Here, $`q`$ and $`n`$ index frequency and time stft bins respectively.
There are two common approaches to define a predictive model:

#### Direct Mapping

is a common approach that directly maps the noisy spectrogram $`Y`$ to a
clean estimate $`\hat{S}`$ with a nn (nn) $`d_{\theta}`$ \[5, 6, 7\]; we
denote this by $`f_{\mathrm{SE}\text{-}d}(Y)=d_{\theta}(Y)`$.

#### Masking (Complex Ratio Mask).

In contrast, a mask network $`M_{\theta}`$ predicts a bounded complex
ratio mask and applies it multiplicatively to the input; i.e.,
$`f_{\mathrm{SE}\text{-}M}(Y)=M_{\theta}(Y)\odot Y`$ (element-wise in
the stft domain) \[8, 9\]. We use tanh-bounded logits to produce the
mask: $`M_{r}=2\tanh(\ell_{\Re})`$, $`M_{i}=2\tanh(\ell_{\Im})`$, and
$`M=M_{r}+\mathrm{j}M_{i}`$.

### 2.2 Generative Speech Enhancement

We adopt SGMSE+ \[11\] for generative se. It frames se as a conditional
generation task in the complex stft domain. A continuous-time diffusion
process is conditioned on the observation $`Y`$, and an ncsnpp score
network $`s_{\theta}(x_{t},y,t)`$ learns to reverse the corruption. At
test time, we obtain $`\hat{S}`$ by solving the reverse sde (sde)

```math
dx_{t}=\Big[-f(x_{t},Y)+g(t)^{2}\,s_{\theta}(x_{t},Y,t)\Big]dt+g(t)d\bar{w}, \tag{2}
```

where $`f(x_{t},Y)`$ is the drift, $`g(t)`$ the diffusion coefficient
and $`\bar{w}`$ denotes a standard Wiener process.

We define the diffusion se model as
$`f_{\mathrm{SE}\text{-}\mathrm{diff}}`$ (Y), meaning that the enhanced
estimate $`\hat{S}`$ is obtained by running a reverse sde on (2) from an
initial state
$`x_{T}\!\sim\!\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}(Y,\sigma(T)^{2}I)`$ down to
$`t{=}0`$ (conditioned on $`Y`$). In this notation,
$`f_{\mathrm{SE}\text{-}\mathrm{diff}}`$ is the integration operator
over the entire reverse sde.

In our experiments we report results under two sampling regimes: (1) the
standard stochastic reverse sde, where noise increments $`d\bar{w}`$ are
drawn at each step; and (2) a _fixed-noise_ reverse sde, in which a
single Wiener path (random seed) is frozen for the duration of
optimization. Freezing the path removes sampling variability and makes
the trajectory deterministic, enabling controlled comparisons and stable
gradient propagation in white-box adversarial attacks. This setup
isolates the effect of diffusion stochasticity on robustness.

## 3 Adversarial Attacks

We consider targeted _white-box_ attacks: the attacker knows the
enhancement system and can backpropagate through it down to the input.
Given a source mixture $`Y^{\mathrm{user}}=S^{\mathrm{user}}+N`$ and a
speech signal of comparable length targeted by the attacker
$`S^{\mathrm{attacker}}`$ in the complex stft domain, the goal is to add
a small perturbation $`\delta\in\mathbb{C}^{F\times T}`$ to the source
mixture $`Y^{\mathrm{user}}`$ so that the enhanced output
$`f_{SE}(Y^{\mathrm{user}}+\delta)`$ resembles the clean speech
$`S^{\mathrm{attacker}}`$ targeted by the attacker but unintended by the
user.

Applying $`f_{SE}`$ in its direct, mask, or diffusion form:
$`f_{\mathrm{SE}\text{-}d}`$, $`f_{\mathrm{SE}\text{-}M}`$,
$`f_{\mathrm{SE}\text{-}\mathrm{diff}}`$ to the adversarial mixture
$`Y^{\mathrm{user}}+\delta`$ yields:

```math
\hat{S}^{Y^{\mathrm{user}}+\delta}\;=\;f_{SE}\big(Y^{\mathrm{user}}+\delta\big). \tag{3}
```

Then we optimize $`\delta`$ so that the SE system’s output is close to
$`S^{\mathrm{attacker}}`$ instead of $`S^{\mathrm{user}}`$using an mse
loss function

```math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{adv}}(\delta)\;=\;\sum_{q,n}\big|\hat{S}^{Y^{\mathrm{user}}+\delta}_{q,n}-S^{\mathrm{attacker}}_{q,n}\big|^{2}. \tag{4}
```

resulting in the iterative update

```math
\delta^{(k+1)}\;\leftarrow\;\delta^{(k)}-\eta\,\nabla_{\delta^{(k)}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{adv}}(\delta^{(k)}),\quad\quad\delta^{(0)}=0. \tag{5}
```

Here, $`\eta`$ denotes the step size (learning rate), and
$`k=0,\dots,K\!-\!1`$ indexes the iteration of the optimization.
Furthermore, we employ gradient descent with momentum to stabilize and
speed up the optimization.

### 3.1 Backpropagation for diffusion models

White-box attacks on diffusion models require gradients through every
reverse step of (2). A straightforward approach saves activations and
gradients at each step, so memory grows with the number of steps. We
avoid this by using *activation checkpointing*¹¹ 1
[https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/stable/checkpoint.html](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/stable/checkpoint.html):
we skip saving intermediate states in the forward pass and recompute
them during backpropagation, trading extra compute for lower memory. We
checkpoint the score-network calls at each reverse step, which makes the
memory requirement almost independent of the step count, at the cost of
roughly $`2\times`$ more computation.

### 3.2 Psychoacoustic Hiding

Along with optimizing the adversarial loss (4), we adapt the ph (ph)
model proposed in \[3\]: the perturbation
$`\delta\!\in\!\mathbb{C}^{F\times T}`$ is optimized using a
psychoacoustic model, such that the added adversarial noise $`\delta`$
is masked by the mixture $`Y^{\mathrm{user}}`$.

#### Hearing threshold $`H`$ (dB SPL).

$`H_{q,n}`$ is the per-time-frequency audibility limit: it combines the
absolute threshold in quiet with signal-dependent masking, so that
perturbations kept below $`H`$ are hidden for the listener. Following
\[3\], we estimate $`H`$ from noisy observation $`Y^{\mathrm{user}}`$
with the MPEG-1 psychoacoustic model. For reproducibility, we use an
open-source implementation²² 2
[https://github.com/RUB-SysSec/dompteur/tree/main/standalone-psychoacoustic-filtering](https://github.com/RUB-SysSec/dompteur/tree/main/standalone-psychoacoustic-filtering).

#### Spectral difference $`D`$ (dB).

We define the perturbation per bin relative to the peak magnitude, as in
\[3\]:

```math
D_{q,n}=20\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{\big|\delta_{q,n}\big|}{\max_{q,n}(|Y^{\mathrm{user}}|)}\right). \tag{6}
```

This yields a per-bin deviation in dB and we can directly compare it to
$`H_{q,n}`$

#### Mask $`\Phi`$.

Following \[3\], we quantify the level of distortion that is still
acceptable $`\Phi`$ by comparing the per-bin spectral difference $`D`$
to the hearing threshold $`H`$:

```math
\Phi_{q,n}\;=\;H_{q,n}\;-\;D_{q,n}. \tag{7}
```

Negative values for $`\Phi_{q,n}`$ indicate that the threshold is
exceeded; positive values show the remaining headroom. We introduce a
tolerance parameter $`\lambda`$ (in dB), and set negative values to
zero:

```math
\Phi^{\!*}_{q,n}\;=\;\max\!\big(\Phi_{q,n}+\lambda,\,0\big). \tag{8}
```

The tolerance $`\lambda`$ allows to control the trade-off between
imperceptibility and attack strength. Then, we obtain a mask
$`\hat{\Phi}\!\in\![0,1]`$ by applying a standard min-max normalization.

#### Gated optimization.

Finally, we apply $`\hat{\Phi}(q,n)`$ as a psychoacoustic gate to the
gradient:

```math
\displaystyle\delta^{(k+1)} \displaystyle\leftarrow\delta^{(k)}-\eta\,\Big(\hat{\Phi}^{(k)}\odot\nabla_{\delta^{(k)}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{adv}}(\delta^{(k)})\Big) \tag{9}
```

Here, $`\odot`$ is element-wise multiplication. Because $`\hat{\Phi}`$
is real-valued, it scales the real and imaginary parts equally. In our
implementation, the hearing threshold $`H`$ is computed once per
utterance from $`Y^{\mathrm{user}}`$ and kept fixed during optimization;
the spectral difference $`D`$ and the resulting masks $`\hat{\Phi}`$ are
recomputed at every iteration as $`\delta`$ changes.

### 3.3 Projected Gradient Descent with an $`\ell_{2}`$ budget

We found an $`\ell_{2}`$ constraint effective in practice. Accordingly,
we optimize under a fixed $`\ell_{2}`$ budget using projected gradient
descent (PGD) \[12\]. We formulate the constrained optimization:

```math
\min_{\delta}\ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{adv}}(\delta)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\|\delta\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon, \tag{10}
```

One PGD update is

```math
\delta^{(k+1)}\;=\;\Pi_{B_{2}(\varepsilon)}\!\Big(\delta^{(k)}-\eta\,\big(\hat{\Phi}^{(k)}\odot\nabla_{\delta^{(k)}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{adv}}(\delta^{(k)})\big)\Big), \tag{11}
```

where $`\Pi_{{B}_{2}(\varepsilon)}`$ is the projection onto the
$`\ell_{2}`$ ball of radius $`\varepsilon`$. The corresponding
projection results in:

```math
\Pi_{B_{2}(\varepsilon)}(\delta)=\delta\cdot\min\!\Big\{1,\;\frac{\varepsilon}{\|\delta\|_{2}}\Big\},\qquad\|\delta\|_{2}=\sqrt{\sum_{q,n}\big|\delta_{q,n}\big|^{2}}.
```

PGD solves the constrained attack by alternating a gradient step with a
projection back onto the norm ball, such that in every iteration
$`\|\delta\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon`$ is satisfied. As a consequence, PGD
limits the total energy of the attack, while $`\hat{\Phi}`$ spectrally
shapes the attack such that it is masked by $`Y^{\mathrm{user}}`$.

## 4 Experimental setup

### 4.1 Model preparation

We adopt the SGMSE$`+`$ pipeline³³ 3
[https://github.com/sp-uhh/sgmse](https://github.com/sp-uhh/sgmse) as
our baseline repository to obtain a strong diffusion-based se system. We
prepared a generative diffusion model and two predictive baselines that
reuse the same backbone but differ only in how $`\hat{S}`$ is produced
(Section 2). We took the ncsnpp U-Net backbone as a baseline
architecture \[10\]. To reduce the computational complexity, we modified
the network depth by decreasing the number of residual blocks from 2
to 1. Unless noted otherwise, the remaining SGMSE$`+`$ settings are left
unchanged.

Model variants.

- •
  Direct Map - a predictive network $`d_{\theta}`$ that maps $`Y`$ to
  $`\hat{S}`$, trained with the point-wise complex mse to the clean
  target $`S`$.
- •
  Complex Ratio Mask - a predictive mask model $`M_{\theta}`$ that
  predicts a bounded complex mask and applies it to $`Y`$, trained with
  the same complex mse.
- •
  Diffusion - a score-based SGMSE$`+`$ model with the OUVE configuration
  \[11\]. At inference we run the reverse sde sampler, i.e.,
  $`f_{\mathrm{SE}\text{-}\mathrm{diff}}(Y)`$.

Training and evaluation are conducted on EARS–WHAM-v2 dataset \[13\]
with 86 hours of clean and noisy speech in the training part. In this
corpus, mixtures are defined at SNRs uniformly sampled from
$`[-2.5,17.5]`$ dB.

### 4.2 Adversarial Attack with Psychoacoustic Hiding

From the EARS-WHAM-v2 test set (850 utterances; 6 speakers, 3 male / 3
female) we sample $`100`$ random pairs
$`(Y^{\mathrm{user}},S^{\mathrm{attacker}})`$ of comparable duration,
where $`Y^{\mathrm{user}}`$ is a noisy mixture from utterance 1 and
$`S^{\mathrm{attacker}}`$ is a clean utterance 2. For each pair, we
optimize a complex-valued perturbation
$`\delta\in\mathbb{C}^{F\times T}`$ and construct the attacked input
$`Y^{\mathrm{user}}+\delta`$ and output
$`\hat{S}^{Y^{\mathrm{user}}+\delta}`$ (3). Starting with
$`\delta^{(0)}\!=\!0`$, we update $`\delta`$ with the PGD rule (11),
using $`K{=}150`$ iterations of SGD with momentum $`0.4`$ and learning
rate $`0.1`$, while keeping model parameters fixed. For the
diffusion-based se, we set the number of reverse steps to $`N{=}25`$.

Imperceptibility is promoted via the psychoacoustic gate from Sec. 3.2,
computed from $`Y^{\mathrm{user}}`$. A tolerance parameter
$`\lambda\in\{0,\allowbreak 10,\allowbreak 20,\allowbreak 40\}\,\mathrm{dB}`$
shifts the masking threshold - larger $`\lambda`$ relaxes the
constraint. We additionally impose an $`\ell_{2}`$ budget on $`\delta`$
via the projection radius $`\varepsilon`$.

### 4.3 Evaluation metrics

We evaluate adversarial attacks along two axes: _attack success_ - how
well the output signal $`\hat{S}^{Y^{\mathrm{user}}+\delta}`$ matches
the target speech $`S^{\mathrm{attacker}}`$; and _perturbation impact_ -
how audible the perturbation $`\delta`$ is within the original mixture
$`Y^{\mathrm{user}}`$.

Attack success (AS). We report wer, POLQA, estoi between
$`\hat{S}^{Y^{\mathrm{user}}+\delta}`$ and $`S^{\mathrm{attacker}}`$,
and DistillMOS \[14\] for $`\hat{S}^{Y^{\mathrm{user}}+\delta}`$. For
WER, we transcribe with five Whisper \[15\] variants (Large, Large-v1,
Large-v2, Large-v3, Large-v3-turbo) and average the resulting WERs for
stability.

Perturbation impact (PI). To quantify how much the input mixture is
altered, we use POLQA and estoi as a distance metric between
$`(Y^{\mathrm{user}}+\delta)`$ and $`Y^{\mathrm{user}}`$.

In addition, we report the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR, dB) of
$`Y^{\mathrm{user}}`$ relative to $`\delta`$ \[2\]:

```math
\mathrm{SNR}\;=\;20\log_{10}\!\frac{\|Y^{\mathrm{user}}\|_{2}}{\|\delta\|_{2}}.
```

Here $`\|\cdot\|_{2}`$ denotes the Euclidean norm. A higher SNR
indicates that the adversarial attack is of lower power.

## 5 Results and Analysis

Table 1 summarizes targeted attacks on 100 EARS-WHAM-v2 pairs. Below we
highlight the main results.

Direct Map (predictive). Unconstrained attacks
($`\lambda{=}-,\varepsilon{=}\infty`$) steer almost perfectly (e.g.,
WER$`\approx`$0.02, AS-ESTOI$`\approx`$0.94) while being clearly audible
SNR$`\approx`$-2.9 dB. Introducing constraints exposes a clean two–knob
trade-off:

_(1) Fixed energy ($`\varepsilon{=}10`$), sweep $`\lambda`$:_ with the
energy budget fixed, SNR and perturbation impact metrics stay within a
narrow band (SNR $`\approx 8-13\ {dB}`$; PI-POLQA $`\approx 2.55-3.19`$;
PI-ESTOI $`\approx 0.65-0.72`$), while _attack success_ shifts
significantly - e.g., WER $`\approx 0.20\to 1.37`$, AS–ESTOI
$`\approx 0.77\to 0.22`$, AS–POLQA $`\approx 2.62\to 1.07`$ (DistillMOS
$`\approx 3.27\to 1.72`$) as $`\lambda`$ tightens ($`40\to 0`$).

_(2) Fixed masking ($`\lambda{=}20`$), sweep $`\varepsilon`$:_ enlarging
$`\varepsilon`$ improves alignment (WER $`1.17\to 0.23`$ from
$`\varepsilon{=}3\to\infty`$) while increasing audibility (PI-POLQA
$`4.59\to 2.40`$; SNR $`32.46\to 7.72`$ dB). Practically, moderate
settings (e.g., $`\lambda{=}20`$, $`\varepsilon\in\{10,15\}`$) sit in a
balanced region (WER $`\approx 0.20-0.17`$, PI-POLQA
$`\approx 3.19-2.26`$, SNR $`\approx 12.88-5.64`$ dB).

CRM (predictive, mask). For identical $`(\lambda,\varepsilon)`$,
_perturbation impact_ matches Direct Map closely (e.g., at
$`\lambda{=}20,\varepsilon{=}10`$: PI-POLQA $`3.14`$ vs. $`3.19`$; SNR
$`12.90`$ vs. $`12.88`$ dB). _Attack success_ is consistently, but only
slightly lower (e.g., DistillMOS $`2.54`$ vs. $`2.19`$, AS-POLQA
$`1.81`$ vs. $`1.57`$, WER $`0.23`$ vs. $`0.20`$ at the same setting).
In short, the masking approach with similar perturbation impact metrics
produces a worse attack.

Diffusion (generative). Diffusion is harder to control and hides worse
under comparable or even looser constraints. Without constraints it
injects more energy yet underperforms (WER$`\approx`$0.47;
SNR$`\approx`$$`{-}10.96`$ dB). With the same energy budget as in Direct
Map ($`\varepsilon{=}10`$), it remains worse on both axes: attack
audibility is higher (at $`\lambda{=}20`$: SNR $`7.90`$ vs. $`12.88`$
dB; PI-POLQA $`2.80`$ vs. $`3.19`$), and target match is weaker
(AS-POLQA $`1.14`$ vs. $`1.81`$, WER $`0.80`$ vs. $`0.20`$).

Diffusion ablations. We conducted a small ablation study on diffusion se
under the unconstrained attack setting to examine how the diffusion
model parameters affect attack success in the absence of explicit
constraints (Table 2). Fixing the noise path makes attacks more
effective (WER $`0.47\!\to\!0.27`$) by removing protective randomness.
Fewer reverse steps ease steering (e.g., $`N{=}15`$: WER$`\approx`$0.22)
while more steps harden it ($`N{=}35`$: WER$`\approx`$0.57). Increasing
$`\sigma_{\mathrm{max}}`$ facilitates attacks ($`0.7`$:
WER$`\approx`$0.28) whereas decreasing it suppresses them ($`0.3`$:
WER$`\approx`$0.69). The robustness pattern generalizes beyond WER and
is consistently observed across all five metrics.

## 6 Conclusions

In this paper, we show that modern speech enhancement systems exhibit
some vulnerability to adversarial attacks. That is, by adding
adversarial noise, an attacker can trick a speech enhancement system to
output a completely different signal than intended by the user. To that
end, we build white-box attacks with psychoacoustic masking and an
$`\ell_{2}`$ budget for the adversarial attack. We test predictive
(direct map, complex mask) and diffusion SE on EARS-WHAM-v2 using
success and audibility metrics.

We show that predictive SE is comparably easily attacked with a
relatively small $`\ell_{2}`$ budget for the adversarial noise. The
complex mask variant behaves similarly to direct mapping but is somewhat
less vulnerable. Diffusion SE is more resistant to targeted
manipulation: even with a larger $`\ell_{2}`$ budget for the adversarial
noise, the output aligns less well with the attacker’s target signal and
remains more audible than predictive baselines. We show that stochastic
sampling in the reverse diffusion process increases robustness to
attacks even further.
